# Efficient and Stable Flexible Organic Solar Cells via the Enhanced Optical-Thermal Radiative Transfer

Ye-Fan Zhang, Hao Ren, Jing-De Chen,\* Hong-Yi Hou, Hui-Min Liu, Shuo Tian, Wei-Shuo Chen, Heng-Ru Ge, Yan-Qing Li,\* Hongying Mao, Zisheng Su, and Jian-Xin Tang\*

Heating is a knotty factor contributing to device degradation of flexible organic solar cells (FOsCs), and thermal regulation plays a crucial role in the realization of long operational lifetime. Herein, a passive cooling strategy for stable FOsCs is proposed by boosting the optical-thermal radiative transfer to reduce the insufficient thermal dissipation and the elevated temperature caused by irradiation-induced heating, while retaining their flexibility and portability. A spectrally selective coupling structure consisting of subwavelength hemisphere pattern and distributed Bragg reflector is integrated into FOSCs to collectively enhance out-coupling of infrared radiation and limit near-infrared absorption-induced heat generation, leading to a reduced heat power intensity of $2 9 2 . 5 \ \mathsf { W } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and the decreased working temperature by $9 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ under outdoor sunlight irradiation. The $\mathsf { D } 7 8 { \mathsf { : } } \mathsf { Y } 6 { \mathsf { : } } \mathsf { P C } _ { 7 } \mathsf { B M }$ -based FOSCs achieve a power conversion efficiency of over $17 \%$ with a prolonged $\mathsf { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime as long as one year under real outdoor working conditions. These results represent a new opportunity for enhancing the operational stability of FOSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Flexible organic solar cells (FOsCs) have developed tremendously over the past decade due to their cost-effectiveness, portability, flexibility, and ease of fabrication, and have been emerging as a promising power generation technique in applications such as building integrated photovoltaic, greenhouse, vehicle integration, wearable devices, and indoor photovoltaics.[1,2] The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of FOSCs has exceeded $18 \%$ owing to the continuous progress in the development of organic semiconductors, transparent conductive electrodes, light manipulation techniques, and interface engineering strategies.3-9] Despite rapid improvement in efficiency, the operational stability is another critical issue that limits the commercialization of FOSCs. FOSCs suffer from severe performance degradation due to the invading of moisture, oxygen, light, and heat.10-14] Particularly, heating is the major obstacle toward long operational lifetime, which is inevitable for the encapsulated devices under real working conditions.[15,16]

The heat generation in solar cells arises mainly from the hot-carrier cooling, open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ loss, carrier recombination, and the absorption of functional layers.17-19] Accordingly, only ${ \approx } 2 5 \%$ of the absorbed solar radiation in FOSCs can be effectively transferred to electricity due to various losses in energy conversion process.(20,21] The residual energy in FOSCs will be inevitably harvested in form of thermal energy, and the elevated working temperature caused by heating can reach over $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under continuous sunlight illumination if the thermal dissipation is inefficient.120,22] The harsh operating condition leads to severe phase segregation of the active layer, deg. radation of functional layers, and ion diffusion from the electrodes, thereby holding back the device efficiency.[11,23-26]

Since heating plays a substantial role in device degradation of FOsCs, thermal regulation is of critical importance toward high operational stability and long lifetime. Several strategies have been explored to improve the heat tolerance of FOsCs, such as the enhanced phase stability of the active layer and the utilization of organic materials with low ion mobility.19,26] Besides the reported methods based on specific donor/acceptor systems or device structures, the lowering of the working temperature by suppressing internal heat generation and facilitating thermal dissipation is a general strategy toward stable FOsCs.20,27-30]

To improve the thermal dissipation, conventional methods rely on the active cooling systems such as air fans, heat pipe cooling, and water cooling, among which the requirement of an extra energy supply is unable to satisfy the demands for low-cost, flexibility, and portability of FOSCs.[31,32] In this work, we propose an effective passive cooling strategy for the thermal regulation in FOSCs by boosting the optical-thermal radiative transfer while retaining their flexibility and portability. A spectrally selective coupling structure (SsCS) consisting of subwavelength hemisphere pattern and distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) is integrated into fexible substrate to enhance the radiation outcoupling in the infrared region but maintain efficient light-harvesting of the active layer. The SSCS enables FOSCs with a PCE of over $1 7 \%$ and the reduced working temperature by $9 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under continuous sunlight illumination. The corresponding $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime (the time for PCE to decrease to $80 \%$ of the initial value) can be significantly prolonged to 350 days in the real outdoor application scenario, which is over triple that of the reference device without the optical structure $_ { ( \approx 1 0 2 }$ days). These results represent a new opportunity for enhancing the operational stability of FOSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

As illustrated in Figure 1a, the heat exchange between FOSCs and the environment includes heat conduction, heat convection, and infrared radiation under real working conditions such as building or vehicle-integrated photovoltaics.[31] Different from heat conduction and heat convection that are governed by thermal conductivity of the host objects and air velocity, the radiative energy transfer in FOsCs is dominated by optical properties of the device architectures. FOsCs can absorb sunlight in the absorption spectral range of various functional layers and simultaneously emit infrared radiation to the environment. The net heat generation power $( P _ { h e a t } )$ of a FOSC can be expressed as

$$
P _ { h e a t } = P _ { s u n } + P _ { a t m } \left( T _ { a m b } \right) - P _ { M P P } - P _ { c o n d + c o n \nu } - P _ { r a d } \left( T \right)
$$

where $P _ { s u n }$ is the absorbed solar irradiance power, which is determined by material emissivity/absorbance $\varepsilon ( \lambda , \theta _ { \mathrm { s u n } } )$ , solar irradiance intensity $I _ { \mathrm { A M 1 . 5 } } ( \lambda )$ , and solar incidence angle $\theta _ { s u n } ,$ i.e.,

$$
P _ { s u n } = \int _ { 0 } ^ { \infty } d \lambda \varepsilon \big ( \lambda , \theta _ { s u n } \big ) I _ { A M 1 . 5 } \big ( \lambda \big ) c o s \big ( \theta _ { s u n } \big )
$$

$P _ { a t m } ( T _ { a m b } )$ is another source of thermal energy, representing the absorbed infrared radiation from the atmosphere, and can be derived by the formula of

$$
P _ { a t m } \left( T _ { a m b } \right) = \int d \Omega c o s \theta \int _ { 0 } ^ { \infty } d \lambda I _ { B B } \left( T _ { a m b } , \lambda \right) \varepsilon ( \lambda , \Omega ) \varepsilon _ { a t m } \left( \lambda , \Omega \right)
$$

where $\int d \varOmega$ represents the angular integral over the hemisphere. $I _ { \mathrm { B B } } ( T _ { \mathrm { a m b } } , \lambda )$ is the spectral radiance of the ideal blackbody at ambient temperature $\left( T _ { \mathrm { a m b } } \right)$ , which is calculated by

$$
I _ { B B } \left( T _ { a m b } , \lambda \right) = \frac { 2 h c ^ { 2 } } { \lambda ^ { 5 } } \left( e ^ { \frac { h c } { \lambda k _ { B } T } } - 1 \right) ^ { - 1 }
$$

where $h$ is the plank constant, $c$ is the light velocity, $\lambda$ is the wavelength, and $k _ { \mathrm { B } }$ is the Boltzmann constant. $\varepsilon ( \lambda , \varOmega )$ is the infrared emissivity of the material, and $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { a t m } } ( \lambda , \varOmega )$ is the atmosphere emissivity that follows

$$
\mathcal { E } _ { a t m } \left( \lambda , \Omega \right) = 1 - t \left( \lambda \right) ^ { \frac { 1 } { \cos { \theta } } }
$$

in which $t ( \lambda )$ is the transmittance of the atmosphere. $P _ { \mathrm { M P P } }$ is the output power of FOsCs at the maximum power point (MPP). $P _ { \mathrm { c o n d + c o n v } }$ represents the nonradiative heat exchange of FOsCs with the environment that is heat conduction and heat convection, and can be expressed as

$$
P _ { c o n d + c o n \nu } = h _ { c } \left( T - T _ { a m b } \right)
$$

where $h _ { \mathrm { c } }$ is the nonradiative heat coefficient considering both convective and conductive heat exchange, and $T$ is the working temperature. Here, we assume $h _ { \mathrm { c } }$ as $6 ~ \mathrm { W ~ m ^ { - 2 } ~ K ^ { - 1 } }$ , which corresponds to the most common cas.7,33 $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ is the radiative cooling power that determines the passive cooling ability of FOsCs, which is calculated by the hemispheric integral of emitted irradiance following the equation of

$$
P _ { r a d } ( T ) = \int \mathop { d \Omega } c o s \theta \overset { \infty } { \underset { 0 } { \int } } d \lambda I _ { B B } \left( T , \lambda \right) \varepsilon ( \lambda , \Omega )
$$

According to Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation, the emissivity is equal to the absorptivity of a bulk emitting and absorbing thermal radiation in thermodynamic equilibrium. When $P _ { \mathrm { h e a t } }$ equals zero, the system is supposed in thermal equilibrium. The working temperature $( T )$ of FOSCs can be thus derived from these equations.

As determined by the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra, the functional films in FOsCs exhibit the significant difference in $\varepsilon ( \lambda , \varOmega )$ (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Therefore, the $\varepsilon ( \lambda , \varOmega )$ of a substrate is the decisive parameter to maximize $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ of FOsCs. Furthermore, high infrared absorption of a substrate determines that most of the infrared light emitted from the active layer and transport layers will be absorbed by the substrate. The thermal energy is finally coupled from the substrate into the air through the optical-thermal radiation transfer. Analogous to light-emitting diodes, only partial of the infrared radiation from FOsCs can be coupled into the air and the residual is mainly trapped in substrate and waveguide modes due to the refractive index mismatch at the interfaces.34-37] The infrared radiation will be constantly absorbed and re-emitted, and finally dissipated through heat exchange. The presence of light trapping in this energy transfer process retards the out-coupling of thermal radiation and thus the cooling of FOSCs.[38]

According to Equation (7), a high emissivity of $\varepsilon ( \lambda , \varOmega )$ in a wide infrared band has a constructive impact on the cooling ability, because the working temperature of FOsCs usually exceeds 70 or even $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under continuous solar irradiance. Therefore, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMs) was chosen as the substrate due to its relatively high infrared emissivity as compared to the other commonly used flexible substrates such as polyethylene glycol terephthalate and polyimide (Figure S2,

Supporting Information). Particularly, PDMS exhibits the highest emissivity in the spectral region of 8 to $1 3 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ , where the atmosphere rarely emits radiation. Therefore, PDMS promises the good radiative cooling effect. By using a simplified substrate/Ag structure, the angle-dependent emissivity of the substrate can be calculated (see the details in Supporting Information). Accordingly, a transmitted radiation power $P _ { r a d } ( T )$ of $5 2 9 . 7 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ can be calculated for PDMS at a working temperature of $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ which is higher than 508.6 and $5 2 2 . 1 \mathrm { \ W \ m } ^ { - 2 }$ for polyethylene glycol terephthalate and polyimide, respectively.

The angle-resolved emissivity of FOsCs was calculated with a device structure of PDMS/silver nanowires $( \mathrm { A g N W s } ) /$ $\mathrm { Z n O { : } C s _ { 2 } C O _ { 3 } / D 1 8 { : } Y 6 { : } P C _ { 7 1 } B M / M o O _ { 3 } / A g }$ (see Experimental Section for the layer thickness and fabrication details of each functional layer). Figure 1b,c shows the calculated angle-dependent radiative power of FOsCs with and without interface reflection in the infrared region. The flexible substrate exhibits a highly angle-dependent radiation when considering the interface reflection. The radiation power rapidly declines with emission angle and decreases to nearly $0 \ \mathrm { \ W \ c m ^ { - 2 } \ \mu m ^ { - 1 } }$ over $5 0 ^ { \circ }$ . These results indicates that infrared radiation is severely trapped inside the film due to interface reflection, leading to great suppression of radiative cooling ability. In contrast, Figure 1c demonstrates that high radiative power could be achieved in all directions when interface reflection is neglected. The extractions of 8.9 and $1 2 . 1 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ radiation at large emission angles result from low refractive index of PDMS, which is close to or even lower than 1 (Figure S3, Supporting Information). It is worth noting that the infrared emission from a FOsC is slightly dependent on the emission angle when neglecting the interface reflection in the calculation (Figure 1c).

To investigate the influence of interface reflection on opticalthermal radiative transfer efficiency, $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ and $P _ { \mathrm { a t m } } ( T _ { \mathrm { a m b } } )$ were calculated on a basis of Equations (3) and (7). For a hypothetical situation with $T _ { \mathrm { a m b } }$ of $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $T$ of $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ and $P _ { \mathrm { a t m } } ( T _ { \mathrm { a m b } } )$ of FOSCs are calculated to be 256.2 and $2 2 8 . 3 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ respectively. As a comparison, the neglection of interface reflection changes $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ and $P _ { \mathrm { a t m } } ( T _ { \mathrm { a m b } } )$ to 529.7 and $2 5 0 . 8 \ \mathrm { W } \ \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ respectively. As a result, the interface reflection reduces the radiation cooling power from $2 7 8 . 9$ to $2 7 . 9 \mathrm { ~ W ~ m } ^ { - 2 }$ , revealing that a large amount of thermal energy is trapped inside the substrate and the heat dissipation is impeded. Compared to $P _ { \mathrm { c o n d + c o n v } }$ with a value of $1 8 0 \mathrm { ~ W ~ m ~ } ^ { - 2 }$ the relatively higher radiative cooling power indicates an important contribution of optical-thermal infrared radiation transfer to heat dissipation. The balanced working temperature of FOsCs can be calculated by substituting approximate $P _ { \mathrm { s u n } }$ of $6 4 0 \mathrm { ~ W ~ m ~ } ^ { - 2 }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { M P P } }$ of $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { W ~ m ^ { - 2 } } }$ into Equation (1). The presence of interface reflection increases the working temperature of FOsCs from 60.3 to $8 6 . 6 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ when the absorption spectrum in the wavelength region below $1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ is assumed to be unchanged. These results illustrate the important role of infrared light manipulation on the infrared emissivity and working temperature of FOsCs. In fact, the trapped infrared radiation will be recycled as heat and then be re-emitted as infrared radiation again until the thermal energy is dissipated. The presence of light trapping retards the out-coupling of thermal radiation, and the subsequent opticalthermal radiative energy transfer is reduced. It is thus speculated that the optical manipulation for boosting the infrared emission could improve the cooling efficiency of FOsCs and contribute to the improved operational lifetime.

To boost the optical-thermal radiative transfer and improve the thermal dissipation in FOSCs, a SSCS design is integrated into fexible PDMS substrate. As schematically illustrated in Figure 2a, the SsCS is composed of a periodic hemisphere pattern and a DBR. As shown in Figure S4 (Supporting Information), the periodic hemisphere pattern was fabricated on the PDMS substrate via soft nanoimprint lithography. Figure 2b displays the scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the periodic hemisphere pattern, indicating that the morphology of the $5 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ polystyrene (PS) spheres was completely transferred to the PDMS substrate with high accuracy. Figure 2c shows the absorption (1-R-T) spectra of FOSCs with and without different optical structures. Considering the weak solar radiation at the spectral region of below $0 . 3 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ and beyond $2 . 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ which barely contribute to $P _ { \mathrm { s u n } } .$ the characterization wavelength was set from 0.3 to $2 . 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . Due to anti-reflective effect and light diffraction of the periodic hemisphere pattern, FOsCs obtained a broadband absorption enhancement in the absorption region of the active layer as obtained with finite difference time domain (FDTD) method (Figure S5a,b, Supporting Information). The subwavelength structure provided the gradient refractive index profiles at the interface, suppressing the interface reflection. Moreover, the subwavelength structure contributed to strong light scattering of incident solar radiation, thereby enhancing light harvesting of the organic active layer. This absorption enhancement is beneficial to the generation of photocurrent but will induce an increase in heat generation simultaneously. Moreover, light harvesting in the spectral region beyond the absorption edge of the active layer can be observed. The simulated absorption maps of FOsCs indicate that the enhanced absorption ${ \approx } 1 . 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ originates from the significant light extinction at the Ag interface (Figure S5c,d, Supporting Information). This part of optical energy will be all transferred to thermal energy since the photon energy is insufficient to generate excitons in the active layer. When substituting the absorption spectrum into the equations, it is found that enhanced light harvesting in the whole spectral region increases the $P _ { \mathrm { s u n } }$ from $6 4 2 . 6$ to $6 7 7 . 0 ~ \mathrm { W ~ m } ^ { - 2 }$

A slight increase in the emissivity is also observed after introducing periodic hemisphere pattern in the flat surface as evident by FTIR spectra (Figure 2d). The signal was collected at wavelength from 2.5 to $2 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ because this spectral region dominants the black-body radiation power of object with temperature ${ \approx } 6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Due to anti-reflection and light scattering effects of the subwavelength structure, the patterned substrate obtained improved emissivity. In specific, over 0.10 increase in emissivity was found at ${ \approx } 1 0 . 5$ and $1 6 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ and below $8 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ The presence of DBR further increase the emissivity of the substrate due to the absorption of $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { M g F } _ { 2 }$ in MIR region. According to the equations, the higher emissivity determines a higher $P _ { \mathrm { r a d } } ( T )$ which corresponds to device with higher cooling effect. Figure 2e,g shows the electric field distribution profiles of PDMS simulated by FDTD method. In the simulation setup, a $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ -thick substrate and $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ -thick air were included in the simulation zone along $\mathbf { X }$ and y axis. A dipole was set in the substrate to simulate the isotropous infrared radiation. After simulation, the electric field intensity along z direction $\left( \operatorname { E } _ { z } \right)$ was collected. The light trapping in a plane PDMS film leads to a weak electric field distribution in the air region. The construction of hemispheres suppresses the TIR and broke the waveguide mode, enhancing the electric field in the air region. The presence of subwavelength structure results in a 1.7 times stronger MIR emission. These results indicate that the construction of a $5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ hemisphere pattern can facilitate the escape of infrared emission into the air. The far-field optical power distribution of FOsCs was also simulated and shown in Figure $^ { 2 \mathrm { f , h } }$ These results confirm the weak optical out-coupling efficiency of infrared radiation at an emission angle larger than $3 0 ^ { \circ }$ for a flat PDMS substrate. In contrast, the introduction of a periodic hemisphere pattern enhances the optical out-coupling at large emission angles and boosts the infrared emission from FOSCs at a broadband spectral region as well.

To eliminate the heat generation caused by near infrared (NIR) light with energy lower than the optical bandgap of the active layer, a DBR consisting of the periodically stacked $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { M g F } _ { 2 }$ was further incorporated into the SsCs (Figure 2a). The cross sectional SEM image of DBR is shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information). The onset edge of the dominant reflection peak of DBR was tuned to ${ \approx } 1 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , which can block the infrared absorption without significantly scarifying the photocurrent generation for FOsCs. The absorption spectrum of a SSCS-based FOsC is plotted in Figure 2c. The additional high reflection beyond $1 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ and low reflection in the broad spectral region result in the decreased absorption of FOsCs in the whole spectral region, yielding a decreased $P _ { \mathrm { s u n } }$ of $6 1 2 . 6 ~ \mathrm { W } ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$

Therefore, it can be concluded that the SsCS is promising in prolonging the operational lifetime of FOSCs without sacrificing the device efficiency.

Operational stability of FOsCs. a) Schematic and photograph of thermal box. b) Infrared thermal images of various FOsCs measured outdoor and derived temperatures of the marked points and areas. c) Timeresolved working temperatures of various FOsCs. d) Efficiency decay of FOSCs working outdoors.

Figure 4c plots the tracking of the working temperature of FOSCs throughout the day. It is noted that the working temperatures of all FOsCs reach their highest value at noon when the ambient temperature keeps a small change. It is thus inferred that the trend of the device working temperature throughout the day is highly dependent on the variation in the illumination angle and $P _ { \mathrm { s u n } }$ of sunlight, and the radiative energy exchange plays a more important role rather than the nonradiative heat exchange.

The operational stability of FOsCs was further examined to clarify the cooling effect of the SSCS-based optical structures. To avoid the moisture- and oxygen-induced degradation of FOSCs as much as possible, a flexible encapsulation layer of parylene was deposited onto the devices. Figure 4d shows the efficiency tracking of FOsCs monitored in air. After 44 days of continuous outdoor sunlight illumination, the efficiencies of reference and SSCS-based FOSCs decayed to ${ \approx } 8 9 . 1 \%$ and $9 4 . 3 \%$ of their initial values, respectively. As extrapolated by fitting a double exponential decay, the outdoor $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetimes of a reference device is estimated to be ${ \approx } 1 0 2$ days. On the contrary, the SsCs-based FOSC achieves a potential outdoor $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime of 350 days, and maintains a relatively stable efficiency afterward. It is noteworthy that the measurements were performed in summer with an average daily maximum temperature of over $3 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ It is found that the operational lifetime of FOsCs is rather sensitive to the working temperature, and the $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetimes of a reference device can be dramatically decreased from 1417 to 444 h under continuous illumination when the working temperature is increased from 55 to $6 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Considering the periodical change of solar irradiance and air temperature around the year, a much longer $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime of SSCS-based FOsCs at lower working temperatures is expected, which may approach a maximum outdoor $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime of over 3 years. The device performance of reference and SsCS-based FOsCs without encapsulation were also measured. The $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ lifetimes of the unencapsulated devices were far poorer than that of encapsulated devices (Figure S9a, Supporting Information), while a relative better stability was still observed for the device based on SSCS. As presented in Figure S9b (Supporting Information), the $J { - } V$ characteristics of the unencapsulated devices indicate that the introduction of sscs barely affect the device efficiency of FOsCs, which is consistence with results show in Figure 3a. These results validate the feasibility of improving the operational stability of FOsCs by enhancing the passive cooling performance with SSCS.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have explored a thermal regulation strategy to enhance the photothermal radiative transport for FOSCs operating outdoors. The integration of a spectrally selective coupling structure allows for the passive cooling effect on the FOsCs with the enhanced infrared radiation out-coupling and NIR light blocking for heat generation. An increase in cooling power up to $2 9 2 . 5 \textrm { \text W } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and a decrease in the working temperature by $9 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ have been achieved under continuous sunlight illumination because of the boosted optical-thermal radiative transfer. By retaining a PCE over $1 7 \%$ for FOSCs, the $\mathrm { T } _ { 8 0 }$ operational lifetime can be prolonged to 350 days under outdoor sunlight irradiation. This work represents a universal passive cooling method for enhancing the operational stability of FOSCs.

A thermal box was designed to measure the working temperature of flexible devices under real sunlight illumination. The box was fabricated with insulation foam covered by reflective aluminum foil. The atmospheric spectrum used in calculation was obtained from ATRAN modeling software (Based on Lord, S. D. 1992, NASA Technical Memor. 103 957).
