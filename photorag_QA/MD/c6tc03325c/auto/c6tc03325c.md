# Highly ordered bimolecular crystalline blends for low-noise and high-detectivity polymeric photodiodes†

Kyu Min Sim, Seongwon Yoon and Dae Sung Chung\*

Suppressing noise current while maintaining high quantum efficiency is essential for realizing highperformance photodiodes. Solution-processed polymeric photodiodes, however, often suffer from high noise current levels because of undesired charge injection in the reverse saturation regime or localized energy states, resulting from the presence of structural imperfection. In this study, we demonstrated that such structural disorder can be avoided by constructing active layers with bimolecular crystallites. X-ray analyses of poly(2,5-bis(3-tetradecyllthiophene-2-yl)thieno[3,2-b]thiophene):phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester blends confirmed low paracrystalline disorder and a high degree of orientation ordering of the active layers. The resulting optimized photodiodes with $80 \%$ PCBM content showed a high detectivity value of $1 . 9 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones, a high bandwidth of $1 \mathsf { k H z } ,$ and a high 94 dB linear dynamic range. This result implies that optimizing the structural perfectness of the active layers can be a decisive factor for low noise current and thus high detectivity of the solution-processed polymeric photodiodes.

# Introduction

Polymeric photodiodes (PPDs) have attracted considerable attention because of emerging interest in new functionalities of image sensors such as color selectivity and readability at extremely weak light intensity and attractive features such as being lightweight and flexi bile with a low fabrication cost.1,2 Polymeric semiconductors, a key component of PPDs as the light absorbing and charge generating layers, possess a unique characteristic of chemical tunability; therefore, that the abovementioned functionalities can be fulfilled. However, because PPDs largely rely on the solution-based fabrication process, defect formation in PPDs is inevitable, making PPDs prone to low performance or low reproducibility compared with conventional workhorse silicon photodetectors. The figure of merit for a photodiode is its specific detectivity $D ^ { \star }$ , which is defined as follows:

$$
D ^ { * } = { \frac { e \lambda { \sqrt { A } } \mathrm { E Q E } } { h c i _ { \mathrm { n } } } }
$$

where $e$ is the elementary charge, $\lambda$ is the illumination wavelength, $A$ is the area of the active layer, EQE is the external quantum efficiency, $h$ is Planck’s constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $i _ { \mathrm { n } }$ is the noise current.3–5 It can be observed from the equation that by normalizing the device area, a measurement of $D ^ { \star }$ enables direct comparisons of photodiode performance between different devices. Moreover, a high quantum efficiency and low noise current are clearly essential for high detectivity. While methodologies of enhancing quantum efficiency of photodiodes have been widely investigated in the field of photovoltaics, there have been only few research reports on suppressing noise current.6–8 Noise currents in photodiodes are known to have several sources such as shot noise, 1/f noise, and thermal noise. Among those, shot noise limit is usually closer to the actual noise level than the other noise sources.9–11 Therefore, in general cases, suppressing dark currents can be directly related to lower noise current levels. In this regards, various electron/hole blocking layers have been investigated to suppress the dark current11,12 because it is mainly determined by charge injection in the reverse saturation mode, e.g., electron injection from indium-tin-oxide and hole injection from Al. The use of electron/hole blocking layers leads to approximately suppressed dark current with marginal detectivity values. However, it has been reported that extremely low dark current and low noise current levels can also be realized in conventional diode structures such as (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate)/organic active layer/Al structures.13,14 This implies that optimizing the crystalline/ morphological characteristics of the organic active layers can be a more decisive factor for low noise current and thus high photodiode detectivity rather than additional blocking layers.

In this study, we tried to elucidate the effect of crystalline perfectness in polymeric active layers on dark current and noise current levels of the PPDs. For the model bulk heterojunction (BHJ) system, we used poly(2,5-bis(3-tetradecyllthiophene-2-yl)- thieno[3,2-b]thiophene) (pBTTT) as the donor and phenyl-C61- butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) as the acceptor. pBTTT has a natural high degree of crystallinity and orientation order than the other conjugated polymeric semiconductors because of the interdigitation of side chains from adjacent polymer chains.15–17 pBTTT:PCBM BHJ is known to form bimolecular crystalline structure when a eutectic composition $\sim 4 4 \%$ PCBM) is used because of the intercalation behavior of PCBM between the side chains of pBTTT.18–21 Furthermore, it is known that pure PCBM domain is formed in the case of an excess amount of PCBM of more than $4 4 \%$ , leading to percolating pathways for electrons.21,22 Therefore, by investigating the pBTTT:PCBM BHJ system, we can study the effect of the high crystalline nature of pBTTT and/or the effect of formation of electron percolation pathways on dark current and PPD detectivity.

# Results and discussion

To investigate the degree of crystallinity and crystalline orientation of the pBTTT:PCBM BHJ films, two-dimensional grazing incident X-ray diffraction (GIXD) analyses were conducted. The results of the analyses are shown in Fig. 1. All the BHJ films with varying weight ratio of the blend were annealed at $1 9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $5 \ \mathrm { ~ m i n ~ }$ to increase the intermolecular crystalline order. The GIXD patterns of the pristine pBTTT films were consistent with previous reports by showing $\left( m 0 0 \right)$ Bragg diffraction peak series up to (400) along the $q _ { z }$ axis, with a lamellar $d$ -spacing of $2 1 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } ^ { 2 3 }$ All the other GIXD patterns obtained from the pBTTT:PCBM BHJ films with various blend ratios showed increased lamellar $d$ -spacing of $3 0 . 2 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ , which is also in good agreement with the literature, confirming the intercalation behavior of PCBM between the side chains of pBTTT.24 Interestingly, despite the formation of a heterointerface, the GIXD patterns of pBTTT:PCBM BHJ also revealed highly ordered Bragg diffraction peaks, which could be attributed to the bimolecular crystalline structure.24 (A crystalline structure with two distinct chemical species arranged in an ordered fashion on a lattice.) For quantitative analyses of the crystalline perfectness, we calculated the paracrystalline distortion parameter $g$ and true paracrystal size $\bar { L } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ . Because the paracrystalline (lattice) disorder effect is typical for organic semiconductors,25–27 we can calculate $g ^ { \star }$ and $\bar { L } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ normal to the (n00) plane from the slope $( = g ^ { 2 } \pi ^ { 2 } / d ,$ where $d$ is the domain spacing) and intercept $\scriptstyle \left( = 1 / \bar { L } _ { \mathrm { c } } \right)$ of the $\delta b { - } m ^ { 2 }$ plot, where $\delta b$ is the integral width of the diffraction peaks and $m$ is the order of diffraction.28–30 The $\delta b { - } m ^ { 2 }$ plot of all the films extracted from the out-of-plane intensity profiles are presented in Fig. $1 ( \mathrm { g } )$ . The obtained crystallographic parameters for the pristine pBTTT and pBTTT:PCBM blends are summarized in Table 1. Interestingly, all the blend films revealed a low level of paracrystalline disorder $( g = \sim 3 \ – 4 \% )$ , which is even similar to that of the pristine pBTTT film $( g = 3 . 4 \% )$ . Although the $\sim 8 0 \%$ blend films showed slightly higher paracrystalline disorder $( g = 4 . 1 \% )$ , presumably caused by an excess amount of PCBM domains, the distortion parameter was closer to crystalline $( g < 1 \% )$ than to that of amorphous $\left( g = \sim 1 0 \ – 2 0 \% \right)$ structures.27 This implies that the bimolecular crystalline structure of the pBTTT:PCBM blend enabled it to minimize structural disorder of pBTTT even in the presence of excess amounts of PCBM. Moreover, as summarized in Table 1, the true paracrystal size $\bar { L } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ of pBTTT decreased as the PCBM contents increased in the blend film. The average numbers of diffraction planes $\bar { N } _ { ( m 0 0 ) }$ also showed the same tendency, implying that the growth of PCBM domains inhibited the formation of large bimolecular crystalline domains. This result is consistent with previous reports in which formation of percolating pathways for electrons was observed when an excess amount of PCBM (more than $4 4 \%$ ) was used in the blend.

In addition to the aforementioned quantitative analyses of crystalline perfectness, pole figure analyses can give a deeper insight into the crystalline orientation of the blend films. The pole figures for various blend films revised for the geometrical effect are summarized in Fig. 1(h). To correct for the geometrical effect, the intensity profile in the pole figure was normalized by the relative degree of crystallinity for an easy comparison between the samples, which can be calculated by integrating the relative population distribution with the consideration of the X-ray exposure time, beam footprint, sample thickness, and polymer volume fraction.31,32 To further analyze the obtained pole figure, the relative ratios of edge-on and face-on crystallites are plotted in Fig. 1(i) as a function of the PCBM content in the blend by introducing a new parameter $A _ { \mathrm { e d g e - o n } } / A _ { \mathrm { f a c e - o n } }$ , where $A _ { \mathrm { { e d g e - o n } } }$ and $A _ { \mathrm { f a c e - o n } }$ are defined as the areas integrated over the polar angle $\omega$ ranges from $- 3 0 ^ { \circ }$ to $0 ^ { \circ }$ and $6 0 ^ { \circ }$ to $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ (or $- 6 0 ^ { \circ }$ to $- 9 0 ^ { \circ } ]$ , respectively. Although the pristine pBTTT film had the highest value of $\sim 1$ , the values of $A _ { \mathrm { e d g e - o n } } / A _ { \mathrm { f a c e - o n } }$ obtained from all the other blend films also ranged from 0.8 to 0.9, indicating that preferential edge-on structures were retained even for an excess amount of PCBM. In summary, GIXD-based structural analyses showed that the pBTTT:PCBM blend films with a bimolecular crystalline structure can conserve a low level of lattice disorder and high degree of crystalline orientation even similar to those of the pristine pBTTT. Structural localization (caused by either lattice disorder or orientation disorder) gives rise to energetic disorder, and the resulting variations in energy levels across the entire semiconductor layer can lead to undesired charge injection in photodiode structures operated in the reverse saturation regime. Therefore, the obtained relatively low paracrystalline disorder $( g = \sim 3  – 4 \% )$ and high degree of orientation ordering of pBTTT:PCBM can lead to low dark current in photodiode structures compared with all of the other conjugated polymer-based blend systems.33

In order to investigate the actual dark current behavior of the pBTTT:PCBM-blend-based photodiodes, a conventional photodiode was constructed with a structural sequence of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { I T O / M o O 3 / }$ pBTTT:PCBM/Al. The measured dark current density curves for various blends are shown in Fig. 2(a). For consistency, the thickness of the active layer was set to be constant $( \sim 6 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ regardless of the blend ratio, which is typical for high-performance photodiodes.34 Interestingly, not only the eutectic blend $\sim 4 4 \%$ PCBM) but also all the other blend films yielded a significant low-dark-current behavior with a current density less than $4 \ \mathrm { \ n A } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ at $- 5 \mathrm { \textrm { V } }$ . This result is closely related with the aforementioned structural analyses, i.e., paracrystalline disorder and orientation disorder of the pBTTT:PCBM bimolecular crystallites could be suppressed even in the case of excess eutectic composition, eliminating the probability of dark charge injection into localized energy states. The energy diagram schemes describing dark current injection with and without localized energy states are depicted in the inset of Fig. 2(a). It is interesting to note that the obtained dark current level is similar to those of the recently reported highest-performing photodiodes,3–8,34,35 even though we have not utilized specially designed electron/hole blocking layers. Although low dark current values often imply a low shot noise limit of the photodiodes, the actual noise current can significantly deviate from the limit because of other possible noise sources. Therefore, we measured the noise current level of the optimized photodiode using a reported method.36–38 Fig. 2(b) shows the measured noise current, along with the shot noise and thermal noise levels, as a function of dark current for the optimized photodiode with eutectic blend composition. The noise current level was only a few times higher than the shot noise limit, and there was only a minimal contribution from thermal noise. While an increased amount of PCBM did not significantly alter the crystalline perfectness of pBTTT:PCBM and the evolution of the pure PCBM domain was apparent, as indirectly evidenced by increased $\bar { L } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ and $\bar { N } _ { ( l 0 0 ) }$ values. Therefore, the electron mobility can be also significantly enhanced as the PCBM content exceed the eutectic composition $( \sim 4 4 \% )$ .15,21,39 Consequently, the responsivity $A / W$ values of the photodiode were gradually increased as the PCBM content was increased, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Based on the obtained noise current level and responsivity, the $D ^ { \star }$ values of photodiodes with various blend compositions were calculated. The results as a function of illuminating wavelength are shown in Fig. 2(d). The photodiode with $8 0 \%$ PCBM content showed the highest $D ^ { \star }$ values of $1 . 9 5 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones at $- 5 \mathrm { V }$ and illumination wavelength of $4 3 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , because of low noise current and high responsivity accelerated by PCBM crystalline domain. For the photodiode with eutectic composition, non-optimized electron percolation pathway limited the $D ^ { \star }$ value to $3 . 9 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 }$ Jones, despite having the lowest dark current. The $D ^ { \star }$ value of $1 . 9 5 \times { { 1 0 } ^ { 1 2 } }$ Jones obtained from the simple diode structure only with an optimized pBTTT:PCBM blend composition was comparable to those of previous reports wherein complex diode structures such as hole/electron blocking layers are employed.40,41 Note that $D ^ { \star }$ values obtained from dark current could be slightly higher up to $4 . 3 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones (Fig. S1, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). Moreover, we measured the EQE values from responsivity and it can be observed in Fig. S2 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ . It is clearly seen that the measured EQE values were sharply increased as the PCBM ratio increased over the eutectic composition and this implies that the excess amount of PCBM formed the electron percolation pathways and they assisted the charge extraction from the active layer to the electrode.

We then investigated the frequency response of the optimized photodiode ( $\sim 8 0 \%$ PCBM contents). The bandwidth of the photodiode is defined as the frequency of input light modulation at which the photoresponse of the photodiode was $^ { - 3 }$ dB lower than the continuous wave response. Fig. 3(a) shows the modulation-frequency-dependent photoresponse of the photodiode for various applied bias values. As the reverse bias was increased to negative values, the 3 dB bandwidth tended to increase, implying that the bandwidth was transit-time limited. Note that the transit-time-limited bandwidth of the photodiode is defined as follows:

$$
f = \frac { 3 . 5 } { 2 \pi t _ { \mathrm { t r } } }
$$

where $t _ { \mathrm { t r } }$ is the carrier transit time.42,43 The calculated theoretical estimation of the bandwidth is $1 . 3 9 3 ~ \mathrm { k H z }$ , which is very close to the actually measured bandwidth value of $1 \ \mathrm { k H z }$ , implying near-ideal operation of the OPD. A maximum 3dB $1 \ \mathrm { k H z }$ bandwidth is enough to operate the photodiode for videoimaging applications. Finally, we measured the linear dynamic range (LDR), i.e., the range of the input light power (in units of dB) over which the responsivity was constant. The photocurrent versus input light power for the optimized photodiode at $4 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ wavelength is shown in Fig. 3(b). The LDR value can be calculated using the following formula:

$$
\mathrm { L D R } = 2 0 \log ( j _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } / j _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } )
$$

where $j _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ and $j _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ are the maximum and minimum detectable current densities, respectively.44 The LDR of the optimized photodiode was $\sim 9 4$ dB, which is close to the measured value obtained from Fig. 3(b). This LDR value approaches those of inorganic photodetectors such as GaN (100 dB) and InGaAs $( 1 3 2 \mathrm { d B } )$ photodetectors.45,46 It is also among the highest reported LDR values for solution-processed polymeric photodiodes.

# Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated low dark-current high-detectivity polymeric photodiodes using simple crystallinity optimization without the assistance of functional buffer layers. The pBTTT:PCBM BHJ blend system possessed low paracrystalline disorder and orientation disorder even with an excess amount of PCBM, as confirmed by GIXD analyses. The resulting low energetic disorder could lead to low dark current in pBTTT:PCBM BHJ blend photodiodes by suppressing undesired charge injection into the photodiode structure operated in the reverse saturation regime. Moreover, the excess amount of PCBM over eutectic composition yielded percolation pathways of electrons without sacrificing the defect-free nature. As a result, the optimized photodiode with $8 0 \%$ PCBM content showed a high detectivity of $1 . 9 5 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones, a high 1 kHz bandwidth, and high 94 dB LDR value. These results demonstrated the possibility of realizing simple-structured high-performance polymeric photodiodes without introducing hole/electron blocking layers.

# Experimental

# Materials

pBTTT and PCBM were purchased from Lumtec and NanoC, respectively, and were used without further purification.

# Device fabrication

The organic photodiodes were fabricated on pre-patterned ITO glass substrates with structure glass/ITO $/ \mathbf { M o O } _ { 3 } /$ /pBTTT:PCBM/ LiF/Al. The ITO-coated glass substrates were first cleaned with a detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol by ultrasonication.

The $\mathbf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ buffer films were deposited through a shadow mask with a thermal evaporator: deposition rate $= 0 . 1 { - } 0 . 2 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ ; vacuum pressure $\sim { 1 0 } ^ { - 6 }$ Torr. Blends of pBTTT and PCBM with various weight ratios were solubilized overnight in chlorobenzene (CB) at a total concentration of $4 0 \ \mathrm { \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ . The blended solutions were spin-coated at $2 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ for $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (thickness 580 to $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { m m } }$ ) on the $\mathbf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ buffer layer fabricated ITO glass. The LiF and Al cathodes were thermally deposited to thicknesses of 1 and $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ , respectively.

# Device characterization

The GIXD measurements were performed at the PLS-II 9A U-SAXS beamline in conjunction with 3C beamline of the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL) in Korea. The X-rays from the invacuum undulator (IVU) were monochromated $( E = 1 1 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { k e V } )$ using Si(111) double crystals and were focused at the detector position using a K–B focusing mirror system. The horizontal and vertical beam size were 300 $( H ) \mu \mathrm { m }$ and 30 (V) $\mu \mathrm { m }$ , respectively. The incidence angle $\left( \alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } \right)$ was adjusted to $0 . 1 3 ^ { \circ }$ , which was above the critical angle. GIXD patterns were recorded with a 2D CCD detector (Rayonix. SX-165). Diffraction angles were calibrated by precalibrated sucrose (Monoclinic, P21) and the sample-todetector distance was approximately $2 2 5 \mathrm { m m }$ . The current density– voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ characteristics were measured using a Keithley 2400 source measure unit under monochromatic illumination from a 150 W Xenon arc lamp assembled with a $1 / 8 ~ \mathrm { m }$ monochromator. The photocurrent spectra were obtained by synchronizing the monochromator with the source meter. The noise measurement was conducted with a SR 830 lock-In amplifier. For LDR measurement, a monochromator and $4 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ laser $( 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \mathrm { ~ W ~ c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ were used. To measure the $3 { \mathrm { ~ d B ~ } }$ frequency, the same laser was used with a TDS5052 digital phosphor oscilloscope (Tektronix) and the measured light intensity was $1 ~ \mu \mathrm { W } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ .
