# Dual Effect of ITO-Interlayer on Inverted Top-Illuminated Polymer Solar Cells: Wetting of Polyelectrolyte and Tuning of Cavity

Wan Jae Dong, Jae Yong Park, Juyoung Ham, Gwan Ho Jung, Illhwan Lee, and Jong-Lam Lee\*

Flexible inverted top-illuminated polymer solar cells (IT-PsCs) are fabricated by wetting of polyelectrolyte and designing a microcavity structure by laying an indium-tin-oxide (ITO) interlayer on top of an Ag reflector. The ITOcoated Ag makes the surface hydrophilic, thereby improving wettability of polyethyleneimine (PEIE). This increased wettability of PEIE yields a reflective cathode with low work function of 3.73 eV. The ITO layer also tunes the light absorption spectrum in the active layer. Finite-domain time-difference simulation provides evidence that the ITO layer played a role in both the shift in resonant wavelength in the microcavity and confinement of the electric field to the active layer. Time-dependent simulation suggests that the time to reach steady-state light absorption is longer (6.6 fs) when a microcavity is present than when it is not present (3.8 fs); i.e., the microcavity increases light absorption in the active layer. The designed IT-PSCs show a maximum photo-conversion efficiency of $6 . 4 \%$ on plastic film and $6 . 1 \%$ on opaque copper foil; these are the highest values obtained by top-illuminated PSCs on a metallic substrate. The IT-PSCs have excellent mechanical flexibility and more stable in air than conventional normal structured devices.

# 1. Introduction

Flexible polymer solar cells (PSCs) have possible applications in low cost roll-to-roll fabrication. $[ 1 - 4 ]$ Although plastic has been typically used as a flexible substrate,[5-9] the plastic substrates have a high water vapor permeation rate $[ 1 0 ^ { 1 }$ to $1 0 ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { g \ m } ^ { - 2 }$ per day at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ) ^ { [ 1 0 ] }$ which degrades the device lifetime. Such problems could be solved by using a flexible metallic substrate, but, metallic substrates are opaque. Therefore, flexible solar cells must consist of a reflective bottom electrode, a photo active layer, and a transparent top electrode, i.e., top-illumination structure.[11-15]

The structure of a top-illuminated PsC affects its characteristics. The normal structure consists of an active layer sandwiched between a poly (3,4-ethlylenedioxtthiphene) (PEDOT:PSS) coated Ag anode and a metal or cathode interlayer (e.g., Al, bathocuproine) that has a low work function.[1,13] However, these structures suffer from degradation due to corrosion of the cathode interlayer and diffusion of oxygen into the active layer through the acidic and hygroscopic PEDOT:PSS.[16,17] An inverted top-illuminated structure can extend lifetime by using a chemically stable metal oxide as a hole transport layer and polyelectrolyte as a cathode interlayer, but a polyelectrolyte that is only a few nanometer thick cannot function as a cathode interlayer on an Ag bottom reflector because the Ag layers are hydrophobic.18] Polyelectrolyte solution does not coat its surface uniformly. To obtain a hydrophilic Ag surface to enable wetting by the polyelectrolyte solution, surface treatments such as UV-ozone have been used. However, harsh surface treatment degrades the Ag layer by oxidation and agglomeration.19,20] Preventing the Ag electrode from a roughening is a key requirement to obtaining high absorption of photons in the active layer. Especially, to realize inverted top-illuminated polymer solar cells (IT-PsCs) with excellent air-stability, the reflective bottom electrode must have a low work function.

Moreover, a polymer active layer in PSCs absorbs only $40 \%$ of the incident photons because of its small absorption coefficient (e.g., PTB7, $\alpha { \approx } 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 3 } ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ at $\lambda = 5 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ .[21,22] Therefore, several papers have used nanostructures to improve the absorption of photons in top-illuminated PsCs. Plasmonic metal nanostructures induce light scattering, localized surface plasmons, and surface plasmon polaritons, all of which increase the number of photons absorbed by the active layer.[23-27] 1D Ag nanograting can contribute to the surface plasmon polaritons mode, thereby resulting in enhanced photo-conversion efficiency (PCE) of PTB7:PCBM PSCs from $7 . 2 0 \%$ to $7 . 7 3 \%$ [27] But, implementation of these nanopatterns has encountered some obstructions.28-30] This approach can cause a short path to electrical contact, exciton quenching, and long-term instability. Also, the processes entail complicated processing steps such as high-temperature annealing, inductively coupled plasma etching,[28] and lithography.[29,30] These fabrication methods cannot be used on top of the top-illuminated PsCs because the polymer active layer degrades during the patterning processes.

Several authors have attempted to use an optical microcavity to manipulate the interference of light within highly absorbing media.31,32] Photons that have the resonant wavelength are amplified in the space between two Ag mirrors. Under appropriate conditions, optical field and resonance effect can be maximized in the active layer of PsCs, thereby enhancing light absorption.33-38] Despite the progress made, problems remain to be solved before microcavity can be used in real devices. To tune the resonant wavelength, a cavity length (in which the hole transport layer, active layer, and the cathode interlayer are positioned) between the top and the bottom Ag electrodes must be controlled. However, the thicknesses of charge transport layer and active layer are too sensitive to electrical resistivity, so small changes in thickness significantly degrade the PCE. Moreover, control of the thickness in a solution process that uses spin-coating is extremely difficult because the spinning process involves strong centrifugal force. To prevent such problems, tuning of the cavity length requires a thin interlayer that has a precisely controllable thickness. In addition to the need for a method to precisely control the cavity length, optical simulation must be developed to fully explain the physics of the microcavity in a PSC. Previously, steady-state distribution of the electric field has been simulated to visualize the enhancement of light absorption at the resonant wavelength in the microcavity. However, steady-state simulation cannot describe dynamic properties such as multiple reflections and optical path length. Thus, to understand why microcavity structure affect PCE, the time-dependence of the field distribution of photons should be investigated.

Here, we demonstrated IT-PsCs that overcome the poor wettability of polyelectrolyte and the low light absorption of top-illuminated PSCs. Indium-tin-oxide (ITO) was used as the interlayer on top of the Ag reflector and coated with solutionprocessed polyethyleneimine ethoxylated (PEIE). The PEIEcoated $\sf { A g / I T O }$ reflector had low work function of $3 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { \ e V } .$ The ITO layer improved the wetting of PEIE, and contributed to light absorption, to the shift in resonant wavelength in the microcavity and to confinement of the electric field into the active layer. The decay time of electromagnetic (EM) waves in the microcavity was ${ \approx } 1 . 7$ times longer than that in the noncavity condition. The optimized microcavity structure demonstrated PCE of $6 . 4 \%$ in IT-PSCs and $6 . 1 \%$ in IT-PSCs fabricated on copper foil; these are the highest PCEs reported for top-illuminated PSCs on metallic substrate. The flexible IT-PSCs had excellent mechanical flexibility and air-stability.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Design of Microcavity in Inverted-Top Illuminated Polymer Solar Cells

To design the microcavity in a IT-PSCs, the device structure was (bottom to top) optimized: Ag reflector, ITO layer, PEIE cathode interlayer, PTB7:PCBM active layer, and inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { A g } /$ outer $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ (MAM) transparent anode (Figure 1a). The inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ PEIE, ITO, and PTB7:PCBM layers constitute the microcavity, and their thicknesses determine the cavity length. However, the thickness of PEIE and PTB7:PCBM layer cannot be controlled easily because they are applied using solution process such as spin-coating. Therefore, the thicknesses of PEIE and PTB7:PCBM is fixed at 3 and $8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively. The thickness of the inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ in the MAM electrode was controlled to provide a spacer layer for the cavity resonance.39] However, the thickness of inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ strongly affects the conductance of hole transport,[40] so, deviation of the thickness of this layer from the optimal thickness in the MAM structure causes degradation of electrical performance. When inverted-bottom illuminated PSCs with $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ layers (2, 5, 10, or $2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick) were fabricated, the highest PCE of $6 . 3 \%$ was achieved in PSC with $1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ layer (Table S1, Supporting Information). As the thickness of $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ increased to $2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ PCE decreased to $5 . 7 \%$ due to reduction of photocurrent and fill factor, which resulted from decreased electrical conductance in $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ .Thus, the $1 0 \ \mathrm { ~ n m }$ thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ was chosen here for the inner dielectric layer. The details on optimization of MAM structure considering not only electrical properties but also the optical properties are described in the Supporting Information (Figure S1 and Table S1). The MAM structure was determined to be $1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ thick inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ $1 2 \mathrm { n m }$ thick Ag, and $4 0 \mathrm { n m }$ thick outer $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$

To understand the effect of the microcavity on light absorption, finite-domain time-difference (FDTD) simulation was conducted, assuming illumination with Air Mass 1.5 spectrum from the surface normal direction. When the thickness of ITO was increased from 0 to $5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , the wavelength at which absorption was greatest shifted from 460 to $7 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 1b), because increased cavity length between two Ag mirrors increases the resonance wavelength of photons. To find the optimum thickness of ITO, the contour of total light absorption in PTB7:PCBM active layer was plotted versus thicknesses of ITO and $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ (Figure 1c). For quantitative analysis, we determined the total absorption by integrating each resonance spectrum over the range $4 0 0 \leq \lambda \leq 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . When the thickness of inner $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ was fixed at $1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , the highest light absorption of 330.2 W $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ was attained using a $1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick ITO layer. Although use of $2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ and $1 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick ITO gave the maximum light absorption of $3 3 2 . 3 \ \mathrm { W } \ \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ (Figure 1d), the $2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ reduced the fill factor and degraded PCE in PSCs due to reduced electrical conductivity. Therefore, we used $1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ and $1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick ITO layer. These results indicate that tuning the ITO thickness is the best way to achieve strong cavity resonance in MAM-based IT-PSCs.

# 2.2. Wetting of PEIE layer on Ag/ITO Reflector

To realize the reflective cathode in IT-PsCs, the cathode interlayer of PEIE must be coated uniformly on the Ag reflector. However, the PEIE layer cannot be uniformly spin-coated on the Ag layer due to its low surface energy.8] The average surface roughness $( R _ { \mathrm { a } } )$ of the Ag layer increased from 1.20 to $1 . 4 6 \mathrm { n m }$ after coating of PEIE (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). This result is evidence of dewetting of the PEIE layer from the Ag reflector. This problem can be solved by depositing an ITO thin-film on the $\mathrm { A g }$ reflector. The PEIE layer flattened the surface morphology of Ag/ITO reflector, and decreased $R _ { \mathrm { a } }$ from 1.21 to $1 . 0 1 \mathrm { n m }$ , which means that the PEIE layer was smoothly wetted onto the surface of Ag/ITO by filling the space between Ag grains (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). When water contact angle is measured as a function of the ITO thickness on the Ag $1 2 0 \mathrm { n m } _ { \cdot } ^ { \prime }$ reflector, the contact angle of rapidly decreased from $7 1 ^ { \circ }$ with the deposition of ITO, but nearly plateaued at $4 5 ^ { \circ }$ at the thickness of $2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 2a). To further reduce the contact angle and thereby improve the uniformity of PEIE layer on the Ag reflector, UV-ozone (UvO) treatment was used.

The treatment is effective to change the surfaces of both the Ag and the ITO-coated Ag reflectors to hydrophilic (Figure 2b), but UVO treatment can cause agglomeration of the Ag layer (inset of Figure 2b). Therefore, $R _ { \mathrm { a } }$ increased from 1.2 to $3 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 2c). This rough surface of UVO-treated Ag can degrade the performance of PSCs. In contrast, $\sf { A g / I T O }$ reflector did not show Ag agglomeration (Figure 2d); Smooth morphology $R _ { \mathrm { a } } \approx 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ was maintained after the UVO treatment.

Photographs of the Ag $\left[ 1 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m } \right]$ and $\mathrm { A g }$ $( 1 2 0 \mathrm { \ n m } ) / \mathrm { I T O }$ $( 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ reflectors were obtained after various UVO exposure times (Figure 3a). As the exposure time increased, the $\mathrm { A g }$ reflector is oxidized, so its color changes from silver to dark brown. Total reflectance was measured using an integrating sphere to detect the light scattered by the Ag reflector. The reflectance of Ag reflector started to decrease after $2 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of UVO exposure. Average reflectance in the visible spectrum $( 4 0 0 \leq \lambda \leq 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ gradually decreased from $9 8 \%$ to $30 \%$ as UVO treatment time increased from 0 to $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (Figure 3b). The reason for the decreased optical reflectance of oxidized $\mathrm { A g }$ layer is described in detail (Figure S3, Supporting Information). In contrast, the $\sf { A g / I T O }$ reflector maintained high reflectance of $9 2 \%$ regardless of UVO exposure (Figure 3c). These results indicate that the ITO layer prevents oxidation of the Ag reflector.

The surface chemical compositions of Ag and UvO-treated Ag were analyzed by taking Ag $3 \mathrm { d } _ { 5 / 2 }$ XPS spectra (Figure 4a), which can be deconvoluted with into Ag-Ag bonds $( 3 6 9 . 5 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ and Ag-O bonds $3 6 8 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ .[41] The ratio of $\mathsf { A g \mathrm { - } O }$ bonds to $\mathsf { A g \mathrm { - } A g }$ bond was 0.57 for the Ag reflector and increased to

1.54 after UvO treatment as a result of oxidation of the Ag layer. This result is evidence that the ozone molecules contributed to activation of the chemical reaction to form Ag oxide particles.

The UvO-treated Ag is not appropriate as a bottom reflector in a photoelectric device because of rough morphology and low optical reflectance. Therefore, XPS was used to quantify the wetting behavior of PEIE on the highly reflective Ag and UVO-treated $\sf { A g / I T O }$ electrodes (Figure 4b). The N1s corelevels (Figure 4b, inset) was observed in the $\mathrm { A g / I T O }$ reflector but not in the Ag reflector. This difference implies that nitrogen atoms in PEIE were chemisorbed onto the surface of the ITO. The conformation of the polymer chains of PEIE assembles spontaneously due to the electrostatic forces between the protonated nitrogen cations $( \mathrm { N ^ { + } } )$ and oxygen-related anions such as hydroxyl groups $( \mathrm { - O H } )$ on the ITO surface,42-44 so strong interfacial dipoles formed between $\mathrm { O ^ { - } }$ and $\mathrm { N ^ { + } }$ (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The molecular dipole moments associated with the electrostatic force between neutral amine groups and $\mathrm { N ^ { + } }$ in PEIE molecules contributes some of the dipole at the ITO/PEIE interface.45] Consequently, the surface dipole moment down-shifts the vacuum level, resulting and thereby reduces the work function $( \Delta \Phi = - 0 . 4 7 \ \mathrm { e V } _  \it$ (Figure 4c). In contrast, the PEIE-coated Ag films shows little change in work function $( \Delta \Phi = - 0 . 0 8 \ \mathrm { e V } _ { \cdot }$ , because the PEIE layer cannot coat the hydrophobic Ag surface completely. These results confirm that the dielectric interlayer of ITO can solve the problems of wetting of polyelectrolyte on Ag, and thus, a reflective cathode with low work function (3.73 eV) was demonstrated.

# 2.3. Device Performance of Inverted Top-Illuminated Polymer Solar Cells

The energy diagram of each layer in IT-PsCs (Figure 5a, Supporting Information) was illustrated by using measured work function of Ag/ITO reflector $( 4 . 2 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ and PEIE $( 3 . 7 3 ~ \mathrm { ~ e V } )$ (Figure 4c). The highest occupied molecular orbital and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital levels of PTB7, $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ and thin Ag electrode were obtained from the literature.[46] At the ITO/PEIE interface, the vacuum level shifted to lower energy level by $0 . 4 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ due to formation of a dipole in PEIE layer (Figure 4c). The shift in vacuum level induced the increase of built-in potential between the $\mathsf { A g / I T O }$ cathode and the thin $\mathrm { A g }$ anode, thereby leading to improvement of open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ and short-circuit current $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ . The energy diagram (Figure 5a) confirms that the energy levels could be well-aligned for efficient charge transportation in IT-PsCs. To verify the device performance of the designed microcavity structure, we fabricated IT-PsCs that use it. Current density-voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ characteristics of IT-PSCs with various ITO thickness were measured (Figure 5b), and the device parameters were obtained (Table 1). The cells on the Ag reflector had $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } = 0 . 6 1$ v, $\boldsymbol { J _ { \mathrm { s c } } } =$ $1 1 . 7 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , fill factor $( \mathrm { F F } ) = 4 8 . 7 \%$ , and $\mathrm { P C E } = 3 . 5 \%$ . The low PCE was attributed to the incomplete coverage of PEIE on the Ag reflector, which leads to low $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } .$ When the $\sf { A g / I T O }$ reflectors were used, PCE increased to $6 . 4 \%$ due to increased $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ (0.75 V) and $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ $( 1 3 . 5 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } } )$ that resulted from efficient charge transport and light trapping. When the thickness of the ITO layer exceeded $2 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ gradually decreased (e.g., $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ $= 9 . 0 \ \mathrm { m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ at $5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ ) because of insufficient light absorption in the absence of microcavity. The dependence of device performance on wavelength was measured by incident photonto-current efficiency (IPCE) (Figure 5c). As the ITO thickness increased, the IPCE spectrum shifted to longer wavelength due to increased cavity length. Integral of IPCE curves are almost identical to $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ from $J { - } V$ measurements, but slight little smaller because the IPCE does not include $\lambda < 4 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ .We plotted measured and used optical simulation to calculate $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ as a function of ITO thickness (Figure 5d). The measured $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ agreed well with calculated $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } ,$ i.e., experimental results match the theoretical value well.

# 2.4. Flexible Applications: Metallic Substrate, Flexibility, and Stability

Flexible normal bottom-illumination polymer solar cells (NB-PsCs) and inverted top-illumination polymer solar cells (IT-PsCs) were fabricated (Figure 6a) and their parameters were measured (Table 2). The NB-PSCs were composed of PET/ITO $( 1 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ substrate, PEDOT:PSS hole transport layer $( 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ , PTB7:PCBM active layer $( 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ and $\mathrm { B C P } ( 1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m } ) /$ $\mathrm { A g } ( 1 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ reflector. IT-PSCs were fabricated on both PET films and copper foil.

PSC structure affected PCE. NB-PSCs on PET/ITO film had low PCE of $5 . 3 \%$ (Figure 6b), as a result of the large sheet resistance $( { \approx } 6 0 \Omega \ \mathrm { s q } ^ { - 1 } )$ of transparent ITO electrode deposited on PET film. IT-PSCs fabricated on PET film had high PCE of $6 . 4 \%$ due to both efficient charge transport and light trapping by the microcavity. IT-PSCs fabricated on copper foil had PCE $( 6 . 1 \% )$ slightly lower than the cells on PET films, but this is currently the highest PCE of top-illuminated solar cells on metallic substrate.

To determine why light absorption was spectrally tuned, we simulated 2D FDTD based on numerical analysis of the electric field (Figure S6, Supporting Information) and light absorption distribution in polymer solar cells (Figure 7a). As ITO thickness was increased from 0 to $5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , the maximum of light absorption shifted from 460 to $5 6 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and the contour moved toward the bottom $\mathsf { A g / I T O }$ reflector. In $1 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick ITO, the most broadband $( 4 0 0 \leq \lambda \leq 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ absorption occurred at the center of the active layer because the resonant wavelength in the microcavity is well-matched to the absorbance spectrum of PTB7:PCBM (Figure S7a, Supporting Information). When the ITO was thicker than $2 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , noncavity region (dark region) scattered across the active layer and the peak shifted to $\lambda > 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ . A large amount of light diverged from the active layer, resulting in the decrease in light absorption. The contour of external quantum efficiency (EQE) (Figure 7b) was determined by multiplying the calculated absorption by the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) (Figure S7b, Supporting Information) of PTB7:PCBM. On the visible spectrum range $( \lambda = 4 0 0 – 7 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , IQE was about $80 \%$ and independent of wavelength. Therefore, the distribution of EQE was almost identical to the light absorption. However, the IQE of PTB7:PCBM drops rapidly at $\lambda > 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ : therefore, the photons absorbed at this range of $\lambda$ cannot efficiently generate photocurrent. Because the IT-PSCs with thick ITO $( > 2 5 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ layer absorb a large amount of light from the long wavelength region, EQE must further decreased even though light is absorbed.

Notably, the microcavity absorbed more photons than did the noncavity structure even though the active layer is only ${ \approx } 0 . 6$ times as thick as that of noncavity condition. This result confirms that length of the cavity between two mirrors has a strong effect on light trapping by solar cells.

For analysis of the dynamic optical properties of the microcavity, time-dependent optical simulation was conducted for both the microcavity condition and the noncavity one at resonant wavelength of $6 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . A plane electromagnetic (EM) wave was beamed continuously at on top of the device as a sinusoidal function of time. To investigate the time-evolution absorption characteristics, we calculated total absorption in the active layer (Figure 8b) by integrating the distribution of absorption in the active layer. As the illumination time increased, the total absorption in the active layer gradually increased. After a few femtoseconds, the total absorption reached a steady-state at which the intensity of waves escaping from the active layer was the same as that of incident waves. The time to reach the steady-state was defined as the intersection between the extrapolated line from the steady-state absorption and the straight onset in the absorption spectra. In the microcavity condition (Figure 8c), the absorption of light was strongest at the center of the active layer, due to amplification of the electric field by the constructive interference of resonating waves. A small evanescent field escaped from the active layer because a nodal point of electromagnetic wave occurred at the interface of Ag with PTB7:PCBM.[47] Thus, the reduction in the quantity of waves that escaped from the microcavity increased the onset time to 6.6 fs. In contrast, in noncavity structure (Figure 8d), most of the evanescent waves could easily escape from the active layer, so the onset time was short (3.8 fs). These results indicate that in the microcavity, the quantity of waves that remain in the active layer increases, and the duration for which they stay there increases. Consequently, the PSC with the microcavity absorbed three times as much light as the noncavity condition (Figure 8b).

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, this paper has demonstrated inverted top-illuminated polymer solar cells that have an ITO interlayer on top of an Ag reflector. The dielectric interlayer of ITO was proved to be a promising configuration to protect the Ag reflector from oxidation and to realize a hydrophilic surface without degrading the surface morphology or the optical refectance. The hydrophilic surface of the Ag/ITO reflector allowed the PEIE layer to wet its surface completely, thereby resulting in a reflective cathode with low work function $( 3 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ . Simultaneously, the ITO layer contributes to spectrally-tuned light absorption in the PTB7:PCBM active layer, because resonant wavelength in the microcavity is highly dependent on the thickness of ITO. FDTD simulation suggested that the ITO layer contributes both to the shift of resonant wavelength in the microcavity and to confinement of the electric field to the active layer. Timedependent simulation verified that enhanced light absorption was achieved by delaying the onset time of EM waves in the microcavity condition compared to the time required in noncavity structure. The optimized IT-PSCs showed PCE of $6 . 4 \%$ on PET film and $6 . 1 \%$ on $\mathrm { C u }$ foil, and were also flexible and stable in air. This configuration was also compatible with other conductive and low-work function materials such as ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ thinfilm. We believe this proposed configuration gives a general guideline for demonstrating highly efficient flexible optoelectronics on metallic substrate.

# 4. Experimental Section

Fabrication of Inverted Top-lluminated Polymer Solar Cells: The reflective $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ $1 2 0 \ n m )$ and ITO electrodes were deposited on glass, copper foil, and PET film by radio frequency sputter system under a base pressure of $1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ Torr. UV-ozone was conducted for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ followed by coating the PEIE dissolved in 2-methoxyethanol at 5000 rpm for $3 0 ~ \mathsf { s }$ and dried at $1 1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ . After cathode interlayer coating, samples were transferred to an $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ filled glove box. PTB7 (purchased from 1-materials) was first dissolved in chlorobenzene, to make a $1 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ solution, followed by blending with $\mathsf { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathsf { B M }$ , in a 2:3 weight ratio. The active layer was spin coated and thickness is measured as $8 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ by Alpha step 500 surface profiler. The devices were dried on a hot plate in a glove box at ${ 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C }$ for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ The transparent anode, consisted of ${ \mathsf { M o O } } _ { 3 }$ $\mathsf { o O } _ { 3 } \mathrm { ~ ( } \mathsf { I O ~ n m ) } / \mathsf { A g } \mathrm { ~ ( } \mathsf { I 2 ~ n m ) } / \mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ $( 4 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ , was deposited using the thermal evaporation method.

Fabrication of Normal Bottom-Illuminated Polymer Solar Cells: PET coated by ITO ( $1 5 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ thick, $\approx 6 0 ~ \Omega ~ { \sf s q } ^ { - 1 }$ was used as the starting substrate. UV-ozone is conducted for 10 min prior to the spin-coating of PEDOT:PSS $( 4 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ . After PEDOT:PSS coating, the ${ 8 0 } \mathsf { n m }$ thick $\mathsf { P T B 7 : P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathsf { B M }$ is spincoated in the $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ filled glove box. The reflective cathode consist of BCP $( 7 5 \ : \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathsf { A g }$ $1 2 0 ~ \mathsf { n m } )$ was deposited by thermal evaporation.

Simulation: The 2D FDTD-based simulation (Fullwave, Synopsys Inc.) was performed for numerical analysis of the light absorption and electric field distribution in polymer solar cells. The optical constant of each organic layers were measured by ellipsometry. Boundary conditions for the FDTD simulation were set to be periodic in horizontal direction and perfect matched layer (PML) in vertical direction to avoid unwanted reflection at the edge of the structure. The light is emitted by plane wave on the surface normal direction with air mass 1.5 spectrum. The source type was set to be continuous wave (CW). The transverse electric (TE) waves were presented since TE and transverse magnetic (TM) waves are identical in plane geometry at normal incidence. The grid size was fixed to $2 \mathsf { n m }$ . The simulations were performed during the light propagation over a distance of $1 0 \ \mathsf { u m }$ in a free space to reach steady state. When the steady state is reached, the electric field distribution does not change over $0 . 1 \%$ . The cross-sectional discrete Fourier transformation (DFT) monitor was used to obtain spatial electric field and absorption distributions at steady state. The integrated absorption in the active layer was recorded by using fast Fourier transformation (FFT) monitor. The number of reflection was calculated by dividing light propagation distance by optical thickness of the active layer.

Characterizations: The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPs) was measured using a synchrotron radiation photoemission spectroscopy with the incident X-ray source at $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and a base pressure of $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 0 }$ Torr at the 4D beam line, Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL). The chemical bonding states included in the spectra were separated by a suitable combination of Gaussian and Lorentzian function. The $J { - } V$ curves were measured under air ambient using a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit. The photocurrent was measured under $\mathsf { A M 7 . 5 C \ 1 0 0 \ m w \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ illumination from an Oriel $7 5 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ w ~ }$ solar simulator. The light intensity was determined using a mono-silicon detector calibrated by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). For accurate measurement of the device performance, only the active area $( 0 . 0 6 ~ \mathsf { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ of the samples was exposed. The incident photon to current efficiency (IPCE) is measured using QEXi0 Solar Cell Quantum Efficiency Measurement System (PV Measurements, Inc.). The reflection measurement is performed with Agilent Technologies Cary 400o UV-vis spectrometer. The SEM was done using a PHILIPs XL30S with an accelerating voltage of $\mathsf { 1 0 ~ k V }$ and a working distance of $6 \textrm { m m }$ . AFM images were obtained in tapping mode on a Veeco Nanoscope III. The bending test was measured under ambient condition without encapsulation by using automatic bending testing system. In case of the PsCs, bending test was carried out for short period of time (=1 h, 500 cycles) in order to minimize the degradation by chemical reaction with oxygen and moisture. For the $\mathsf { A g } / | \mathsf { T } \mathsf { O }$ reflector, the sheet resistance was measured for 50 000 bending cycles $( \approx ] 0 0 \ \mathrm { h } )$
