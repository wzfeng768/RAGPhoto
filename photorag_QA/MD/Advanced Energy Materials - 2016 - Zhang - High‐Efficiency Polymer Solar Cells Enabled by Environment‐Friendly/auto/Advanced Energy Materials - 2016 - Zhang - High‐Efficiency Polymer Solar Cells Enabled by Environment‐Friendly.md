# High-Efficiency Polymer Solar Cells Enabled by Environment-Friendly Single-Solvent Processing

Hao Zhang, Huifeng Yao, Wenchao Zhao, Long Ye, and Jianhui Hou\*

In bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) polymer solar cells (PSCs), the donor and acceptor materials form a bicontinuous interpenetrating network with nanoscale phase separation, which plays a key role in facilitating exciton diffusion, charge separation, and transport in their BHJ active layers.[1-6] Since the BHJ active layers are fabricated by solution coating process, morphologies of the BHJ active layers can be effectively modulated by selecting various processing solvents.7-11] Moreover, some other methods such as using high-boiling-point additive,[12-14] thermal annealing,[15] and solvent vapor treatment[16] have been applied to tune the morphologies, and thereby the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of PSC devices have been boosted up to 10%.[17-19]

What calls for special attention is that when high-boilingpoint additives, thermal annealing, and/or solvent vapor treatment are involved for fabricating a PsC device, tedious optimizing processes must be carried out to get an optimal device fabrication condition. The detailed conditions including the feed ratio of additive, the temperature, and the time for thermal annealing or solvent vapor treatment and so on must be fully scanned. On the other hand, the reproducibility of device performance can also be reduced due to the complicated treatment conditions. For instance, the PSC devices processed with highboiling-point additive have problem in realizing reproducible photovoltaic performance, because the uncontrollable drying process of the additive possibly affect surface composition and/ or bulk morphology of the BHJ blends.[20] In addition, the high temperature annealing may not be compatible with the flexible substrates, such as poly(ethylene terephthalate).[21] Lately, some researchers had designed some donor and acceptor materials with considerable photovoltaic performance under additiveand/or posttreatment-free conditions.22,23] For examples, Jung et al. designed a novel fluorine-substituted polymer acceptor to fabricate all-polymer solar cells and obtained a PCE of $6 . 7 1 \%$ by using an additive-free solution.24] Yue et al. used a new polymer donor in PSCs and realized a PCE of $9 . 1 \%$ without using additives and posttreatments.[25] Overall, the development of simple processing method is of significance in advancing the PSC technology to realize the advantages in making light-weight, flexible, and large-scale solar panel by solution coating method.26]

From the aspect of mass production, fabrication process of PsC must meet the environment protection requirements. However, as a technology to produce green energy, the PSCs with the state-of-art efficiencies were commonly fabricated by using hazardous organic solvents, [16-18] such as chlorobenzene (CB), $o \cdot$ dichlorobenzene (DCB), chloroform, diiodooctane (DIO) and so on. Since these chlorinated solvents may cause persistent pollution to environment and accumulative damage to animal and human health, researchers devoted great efforts to developing PSCs processed by environment-friendly solvents.27-30] For instances, Chueh et al. used two nonchlorinated solvents, o-xylene (xylene) and trimethylbenzene, to process the polymer:fullerene derivative blend and obtained a PCE of $7 . 3 \%$ [31] Chen et al. employed a halogen-free and aromatic-free solvent, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran to fabricate PsC devices and realized a PCE of $5 . 1 \% ^ { [ 3 2 ] }$ Our group found that the blend of xylene and $N$ -methylpyrrolidone (NMP) could replace the function of the blend of DCB and DIO in device fabrication and realized over $9 \%$ efficiency;[29] more recently, we used the blend of anisole and diphenyl ether as the processing solvent to fabricate the PSCs and obtained a PCE of $8 . 3 7 \%$ [33] According to these reported works, it can be concluded that although highboiling-point solvents or other treatments were used to modulate active layer morphology, efficiencies of PSCs processed by environment-friendly solvents are still lower than the state-ofart efficiency obtained from the PsCs processed by chlorinated solvents. Therefore, it is still a great challenge to fabricate highly efficient PSCs by employing a single environment-friendly solvent without any further treatment.

Low bandgap copolymers based on benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']dithiophene (BDT) and thieno[3,4-b]thiophene (TT) units, namely PBDTT'Ts, have been successfully applied in fabricating highly efficient PSCs.34 46] However, it is worth noting that all highly efficient PBDTTTs-based devices with PCE beyond $9 \%$ were achieved by using binary solvents. In this work, we first proposed a novel solvent, 2-methylanisole (MA), as the processing solvent to fabricate PSCs based on PBDT-TS1,[37] a derivative of PBDTTTs, and realized a PCE of $9 . 6 7 \%$ without using any additives or posttreatments. As far as we know, this outstanding efficiency is the highest among those of PSCs fabricated under additive- and posttreatment-free circumstance, and as similar as the reported results obtained from the halogenated DCB:DIO or the nonhalogenated xylene:NMP binary-solvent systems. More importantly, MA is a nonhalogenated, biodegradable chemical with low toxicity, and much safer than CB or DCB for mass production. For example, according to the hazards identification on the Material Safety Data Sheet, there is only one hazard based active layers processed by the aforementioned solvents, i.e., xylene, MA, and DCB, were characterized and compared, and the results indicted the phase separation and domain size in the MA-processed active layer are favorable to charge generation and transport. Therefore, an outstanding efficiency was realized by using this environment-friendly solvent solely without any posttreatments.

In this work, we employed PBDT-TS1 as donor material and [6,6]-phenyl-C71-butyric acid methyl ester $( \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } )$ as acceptor material. As shown in Figure 1b, the conventional device architecture (ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer/ $\mathrm { | M g / A l | }$ is adopted to fabricated PSCs; wherein ITO is indium tin oxide, and PEDOT:PSS is  poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate). Herein, MA was used as the processing solvent to make the solution of the donor and acceptor materials. As reported, over $9 \%$ efficiencies could be realized by employing the binary solvent, i.e., the blend of DCB:DIO (97:3, $\mathrm { v } / \mathrm { v } ) ^ { [ 3 7 ] }$ or xylene:NMP (98:2, v/v).[29] In order to make clear comparison, we also prepared the devices by using DCB and xylene solely, respectively. All the devices were fabricated under the same conditions according to the reference. The $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio $( \mathrm { P B D T \cdot T S 1 : P C _ { 7 1 } B M }$ wt/wt) was 1:1.5. The concentration of the solution used here was $1 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ for the donor polymer, and the thickness of the PBDT- $\mathrm { T S 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films was ${ \approx } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . The representative current density-voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ curve of the device is shown in Figure 2a, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are collected in Table 1. The PSC devices prepared from xylene and DCB are $2 . 9 1 \%$ and $6 . 8 5 \%$ , respectively, which are much lower. than the devices prepared by xylene:NMP and DCB:DIO. The PSC device fabricated from MA exhibits an open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ of $0 . 7 9 \mathrm { V } ,$ a short-circuit current $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ of $1 7 . 3 9 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ a fill factor (FF) of 0.704, and an overall PCE of $9 . 6 7 \%$ . Moreover, the efficiencies of MA-processed devices exhibited considerable tolerance of the film thickness (see Figure S1 and Table S1, Supporting Information), which is another advantage for large-area printing technologies. External quantum efficiency (EQE) curves are depicted in Figure 2b. The integrated current density of MA-processed PSC calculated from EQE curve is $1 6 . 6 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , which is coincident with the result from $J { - } V$ measurement $( \approx 5 \%$ deviation). Besides that, the EQE curve demonstrates that in the whole range of absorption spectrum, the EQE of MA-processed device is much higher than that of the other two single-solvent-processed devices. What's more, another well-known polymer, PTB7-Th, was employed to fabricated PSC devices through MA solution, and a PCE of ${ \approx } 9 \%$ was obtained in the conventional devices architecture, which is comparable to the results obtained from binary-solvent system.[29] More details can be found in the Supporting Information.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to characterize the surface and bulk morphology of the blend films, and the corresponding images are shown in Figure 3. Besides the films processed by the aforementioned three single solvents, the blend films processed by the binary solvents, i.e., spin coated from DCB:DIO and xylene:NMP, are present as well. On the basis of the topography and phase images from the AFM measurements, the blend film processed by xylene shows sever phase separation and very large domain size; $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ forms huge spheres with hundreds of nanometers in diameter (which can also be clearly observed in the TEM image), and also the root mean square roughness $( R _ { \mathrm { q } } )$ reaches $1 8 . 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , which is a big value for $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick film. In contrast, the blend film spin coated from DCB is very uniform, i.e., the blend shows a $R _ { \mathfrak { q } }$ of $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { n m }$ , and in AFM phase and TEM images, the phase separation is too weak to be clearly distinguished. For PSCs, very small aggregation size in the BHJ blend is good for facilitating charge separation but unfavorable for forming bicontinuous channels for charge transport, and hence the morphology of the DCB-processed blend film may cause bimolecular recombination. Although the morphological properties in the xyleneand DCB-processed blend films are not suitable for achieving high photovoltaic performance, the morphologies in these two films can be improved by using NMP and DIO as additives, respectively. As observed in Figure 3, the MA-, xylene:NMP-, and DCB:DIO-processed films show very similar surface roughness in AFM and almost same phase separation morphology in TEM, implying that these three types of processing solvent systems served the same role in forming a nanoscale phase separation in PBDT-TS1 $\mathrm { \therefore P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films. Therefore, as observed in $J { - } V$ measurements, the PCEs of the three corresponding devices were very similar.

We employed the photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy to evaluate exciton quenching effects in the MA- and the other two solvents-processed films. Figure 4a shows the emission spectra of a pristine PBDT-TS1 film and the blend films. Taking the absorption spectrum and fluorescence intensity into account, the excitation wavelength was fixed on $5 9 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . The intense peak at $7 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in the pristine PBDT-TS1 film attributes to PL emission of the polymer. The xylene-processed film possesses severe phase separation, which is unfavorable for exciton quenching and thus limits charge separation in the blend film. In the DCB- and MA-processed films, the polymers are well mixed with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and hence the PL can be fully quenched, which indicates the efficient charge separation process.

Furthermore, as shown in Figure 4b, photocurrent density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } \right)$ -effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ plots were extracted from $J { - } V$ curves to determine the excitons dissociation efficiencies $( P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } } ) . ^ { [ 2 2 ] } J _ { \mathrm { p h } }$ is the difference between light and dark current density, and $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ is the difference betweeen. $V _ { \mathrm { a } }$ and $V _ { 0 }$ where $V _ { \mathrm { a } }$ is the applied voltage and $V _ { 0 }$ is the voltage when $J _ { \mathrm { p h } }$ is zero. $J _ { \mathrm { { p h } } }$ increases sharply when $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ is low and then saturates with the increase of $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } ,$ which suggests that most of the excitons have been dissociated with the help of large reverse bias. Therefore, we defined $P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } }$ as the ratio of $J _ { \mathrm { p h } }$ to $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ under short-circuit condition, where $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ is the saturated current density. The $P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } }$ calculated from the $J _ { \mathrm { p h } } { - } V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ plot are collected in Table 1. The $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ of $1 9 . 5 2 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } }$ of ${ \approx } 9 0 \%$ exhibited in MA processed devices are similar to the data acquired from binarysolvent system (see Figure S3, Supporting Information). We can conclude that the active layer morphology obtained from the MA is in favor of photoelectric conversion process from the standpoint of charge carriers generation and collection, which is similar to the optimized film processed by the binary-solvent system.

Hereby, it is reasonable to infer that the varied molecular aggregation effects caused by different solvents govern the blend film morphology. In previous reported works, Hansen solubility parameter was often used as the criterion to select processing solvent or to elucidate the thermodynamics mechanism for the fine-tuning of film morphology.[10,31,47] However, since the film formation is a very complicated dynamic process, it is very hard to make accurate prediction for the molecular aggregation effects in solution or in blend film. Therefore, we used UV-vis absorption spectroscopy to evaluate the aggregation behavior in both solution and blend.[8,48] Given the high solubility of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ in all these three solvent (which are well above the concentration of the processing solution, see Table S2, Supporting Information), and higher mobility as small molecules, the interaction between polymer chains is supposed to predominate the film morphology. Figure 5 shows the UV-vis absorption spectra of PBDT-TS1 in the state of concentrated solvents $( 1 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } } )$ and solid films. The absorption peaks in short-wavelength region $( \approx 3 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ should be assigned to localized $\pi - \pi ^ { * }$ transitions on 2D-conjugated BDT units while the main absorption peaks at $6 6 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ should be attributed to the intramolecular charge transfer between the electrondonating and electron-withdrawing conjugated components. The shoulder peak in the long wavelength is attributed to the aggregations of the polymer chains because the shoulder peak reduced and finally disappeared with the temperature increased from 20 to $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (see Figure S4, Supporting Information).[49] In the three solutions, the absorption intensity of the shoulder peak is much stronger than that of the main absorption peak, implying the polymer exhibits strong molecular aggregation effect. The comparison among the absorption spectra of the solutions indicates that the aggregation effect of the polymer in MA is slightly stronger than in xylene but obviously weaker than in DCB. It is interesting that the different aggregation effects observed in solutions can also be retained in solid films. In Figure 5b, DCB-processed film still shows a higher shoulder peak at long wavelength direction and the xylene-processed film exhibits a rather weak should peak, while the MA-processed film has a should peak with moderate absorption strength.

The mechanistic that the polymer aggregation has a great impact on the final blend morphology and photovoltaic performance has been reported in many literatures.18,49] In 2012, our group have studied and revealed the correlation between the polymer aggregation induced by the temperature variation and the final blend film morphology.[49] Gomez and co-workers proposed that polymer crystallization can be a key driving force for the polymer:fullerene partial miscibility and morphology, and the volume fraction of the pure fullerene phase should correlate to the crystallinity of P3HT in the mixture.[50] Combining the morphology images acquired from AFM and TEM with the absorption spectra, we can reasonably speculate that the interaction between polymer chains is relative intense in the DCB-processed film so $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ cannot grow up freely in the polymer network during the evaporation of solvent; therefore, a uniform and smooth film is formed with slight phase separation. In order to optimize the film morphology, DiO was added to promote phase separation. In contrast, the inter-chain interaction in the xylene solution is weaker so the $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ molecules can be easily separated from the blend and aggregated into large domains; in this solvent system, NMP was used to prevent the severe phase separation. Fortunately, as depicted in Figure 5c, the inter-chain interaction in the MA-processed solution is moderate, so the fullerene domains can grow up in a confined polymer network. Hence, the ideal phase separation morphology can be achieved by using MA as the processing solvent.

In conclusion, we used a single environment-friendly solvent to fabricate highly efficient PSC, and a recorded PCE as high as $9 . 6 7 \%$ was achieved from the MA-processed PBDT$\mathrm { T S 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ device without resorting to any additives or further treatments. It has to say the most significant role the additive approach and annealing methods played is the continuous fine-tuning of film morphology. The favorable morphology and good processing stability make them be widely used in many polymer:fullerene systems, while the advantages of MA are limited in both the capability of morphology-tuning and material varieties. However, the results clearly demonstrate that although we have to use binary solvents to achieve high PCEs in some of PSCs, there are great opportunities to realize high-performance devices with the state-of-art PCE by adopting an appropriate single environment-friendly solvent. Combining simple processing method with green solvent will bring PSCs' advantage of roll-to-roll printing into full play, and prospectively be the hot issue and trend in PSC research and manufacture.
