# Hot-Casting Strategy Empowers High-Boiling Solvent-Processed Organic Solar Cells with Over 18.5% Efficiency

Chucheng Yang, Mengyun Jiang, Shanshan Wang, Bao Zhang, Peng Mao, Han Young Woo, Fujun Zhang,\* Jin-liang Wang,\* and Qiaoshi An\*

Most top-rank organic solar cells (OsCs) are manufactured by the halogenated solvent chloroform, which possesses a narrow processing window due to its low-boiling point. Herein, based on two high-boiling solvents, halogenated solvent chlorobenzene (CB) and non-halogenated green solvent ortho-xylene (OX), preparing active layers with the hot solution is put forward to enhance the performance of the OsCs. In situ test and morphological characterization clarify that the hot-casting strategy assists in the fast and synchronous molecular assembly of both donor and acceptor in the active layer, contributing to preferable donor/acceptor ratio, vertical phase separation, and molecular stacking, which is beneficial to charge generation and extraction. Based on the PM6:BO-4Cl, the hot-casting OsCs with a wide processing window achieve efficiencies of $1 8 . 0 3 \%$ in CB and $1 8 . 1 2 \%$ in OX, which are much higher than the devices processed with room temperature solution. Moreover, the hot-casting devices with PM6:BTP-eC9 deliver a remarkable fill factor of $8 0 . 3 1 \%$ and efficiency of $1 8 . 5 2 \%$ in OX, representing the record value among binary devices with green solvent. This work demonstrates a facile strategy to manipulate the molecular distribution and arrangement for boosting the efficiency of OsCs with high-boiling solvents.

# 1. Introduction

Organic solar cells (OsCs) as hopeful renewable and clean energy devices have received extensive attention because of their solution processability, mechanical fexibility, multiple colors, lightweight, semitransparency, and high feasibility for low-cost commercialization.1-6] In recent years, the unceasing progress in device engineering and materials, particularly for the "Y-series" non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) and the conjugated polymer donors with temperature-dependent aggregation (TDA) behavior, has promoted the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of OSCs beyond $1 8 \%$ [7-16] Whereas, most of the first-class OsCs are fabricated with halogenated solvent chloroform (CF) under suitable preparation conditions, particularly for all-small-molecule devices, leading to a narrow processing window because of the low-boiling point $( 6 1 . 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ of CF.[17-23] Such a narrow processing window not only goes against the operational stability of device manufacture but also increases the risk and cost for its large-scale production in commercial applications, which makes it essential to develop strategies for constructing high-performance OsCs with high-boiling solvents.[24-26]

In comparison with low-boiling solvents, the solvents with high-boiling points can provide more operability and wide processing windows for constructing OsCs. However, the highboiling solvents are not very appreciated by the OSCs with star "Y-series" NFAs that only achieve a PCE of around $1 7 \%$ [27-31] It is widely known that solvents with different boiling points, Hansen solubility parameters, and saturation vapor pressure will result in vastly different film-formation kinetics and thus morphology of the active layer (e.g., phase separation, molecular stacking, domain size and purity). Considering the dissolving capacity and volatilization rate of the solvents correlate closely with the temperature, appropriate temperature control of substrate affords opportunities to manipulate the film morphology, which has been well demonstrated in various types of OsCs.[32-35] Management of solution temperature may be another resultful strategy to optimize molecular distribution and arrangement. Since "Y-series" NFAs-based OSCs with CF solvent can achieve excellent device performance, employing the hot solution (hot-casting strategy) with high-boiling solvent to imitate the film-formation kinetics with CF for preparing active layer should be a viable technique to yield efficient OSCs.

In this work, given the general applicability, operational stability, and eco-friendly manufacture, two high-boiling solvents, halogenated solvent chlorobenzene (CB) and non-halogenated green solvent ortho-xylene (OX), were employed to construct OSCs based on the routine polymer donor PM6 and two typical "Y-series" acceptors (BO-4Cl and BTP-eC9) for investigating the effect of the hot-casting strategy on the device performance.[17,36] The hot-casting strategy will increase the dissolving capacity and volatilization rate of solvents, and then manipulate the filmformation time and molecular assembly kinetics when the solution temperature drops from high to low during the spin-coating process, resulting in profitable molecular ratio, vertical phase separation, and more orderly molecular stacking in the active layer. The well-formed morphological structure of hot-casting blend films is conducive to enhancing photon harvesting, exciton dissociation, charge transport, and collection. Consequently, the hot-casting OSCs with PM6:BO-4Cl achieve PCEs of $1 8 . 0 3 \%$ in CB and $1 8 . 1 2 \%$ in OX, both of which are much higher than their counterparts that were processed with room temperature (RT, 25 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ solution. In addition, based on the PM6:BTP-eC9 matrix, the hot-casting OSCs produce remarkable PCEs of $1 8 . 3 4 \%$ in CB and $1 8 . 5 2 \%$ in OX, further confirming the positive impact of the hotcasting tactic on performance enhancement of the devices with high-boiling solvents.

# 2.2. Exciton and Charge Dynamics

Given the close PCE values of the hot-casting devices and similar phenomenon in two matrixes, two typical PM6:BO-4Cl-based OSCs, processing with 25 and $7 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ solutions, were employed to explore the impact of the hot-casting strategy on exciton and charge dynamics. Figure 3a and Figure S2, Supporting Information exhibit the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra, internal quantum efficiency (IQE), and integrated $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } } \mathrm { s }$ curves of different OSCs. The calculated $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } } \mathrm { \bf s }$ according to EQE spectra are in accord with the values acquired from the $J { - } V$ curves (within $2 \%$ error). The EQE spectra of OSCs with $7 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ solution display slightly larger EQE values in the whole wavelength region in comparison to the devices based on RT solution, which should be attributed to the increased light absorption, more effective excitation dissociation, and charge extraction in the hot-casting devices. The higher IQE values in the hot-casting OSCs further demonstrate the high-efficiency exciton utilization. To investigate the influence of the hot-casting strategy on the charge dynamics, the photocurrent density versus effective voltage $( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } - V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ curves were implemented (Figure 3b and Table S3, Supporting Information).43,41 It is generally assumed that the saturated photocurrent density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } \right)$ is positively associated with the light absorption of the devices under large $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ [45] When the $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ .s $3 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ the hot-casting OSCs deliver a slightly higher $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ of $2 7 . 9 2 \ \mathrm { m A }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ relative to the counterparts $( 2 7 . 6 7 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ , indicating stronger light absorption. Furthermore, the fast saturable $J _ { \mathrm { p h } }$ of the hot-casting OSCs manifests the more effective exciton dissociation and collection process, which can be ascertained by the $J _ { \mathrm { p h } } / J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ values under short-circuit circumstance $( \eta _ { \mathrm { d } } )$ and maximum power output circumstance $( \eta _ { \mathrm { c } } )$ , separately. Larger $\eta _ { \mathrm { d } }$ of $9 5 . 7 0 \%$ and $\eta _ { \mathrm { c } }$ of $8 7 . 9 3 \%$ are achieved in the hot-casting OsCs in comparison to the device prepared with RT solution $( \eta _ { \mathrm { d } } = 9 4 . 0 3 \%$ and $\eta _ { \mathrm { c } } = 8 6 . 3 7 \% )$ , which proves that hot-casting strategy is beneficial to optimizing exciton dissociation and extraction.

# 2.3. Morphology, Molecular Distribution, and Arrangement

Based on the ahead measurements and analysis, the promotion of $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } } \mathrm { s }$ and FFs mainly profits from effective charge generation and extraction, which has always been synonymous with molecular distribution and arrangement. The surface and bulk morphology of the blend films were characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure 4a,b, the hot-casting films with smoother surface morphology exhibit smaller root-mean-square roughness of $1 . 3 2 \pm 0 . 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ compared with RT-casting blend films (1.72 $\pm \ : 0 . 1 2 \ : \mathrm { n m } _ { \beta }$ . In terms of the AFM phase images (Figure 4c,d), alike homogeneous and prominent phase separations are observed from the two types of blend films. We further calculated the line profiles of the full-width half-maximum of the peaks in the AFM phase images to evaluate the fibril widths of the blends (Figure S5, Supporting Information).[58] The fibril width is ${ \approx } 1 6 . 5 \mathrm { n m }$ for hot-casting blends, which is slightly smaller than ${ \approx } 1 7 . 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for RT-casting blends, indicating that the hot-casting film possesses more suitable phase separation. As exhibited in Figure 4e,f, TEM images of the two blend films demonstrate even light and dark distribution, while more evident fibrillar structures can be seen from the hot-casting blend films (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The well-developed bi-continuous interpenetrating networks with nanofibrous in hot-casting blend films are conducive to elevating the exciton dissociation and charge transport. To gain more insight into the positive effect of the hot-casting strategy on performance enhancement at a molecular level, we perform the grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement.59-61] Consistent with the foregone reports, PM6 adopts hybrid face-on/edge-on orientation and BO-4Cl displays preferential face-on orientation relative to the substrate in pure films (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Both hot-casting and RT-casting blend films show predominant face-on orientation, while the hot-casting strategy contributes to steeper OOP (010) and IP (100) diffraction peaks and stronger intensities, proving the more well-organized molecular packing of the PM6 and BO-4Cl in hot-casting blend films (Figure 4g-i). Moreover, the hot-casting blend films emerge similar stacking distance while relatively large coherence length (CCL) values in both OOP and IP direction compared with the RT-casting blend films (Figure 4j and Table S5, Supporting Information). The enhanced crystallinity and larger CCL values help to facilitate charge transport in the hot-casting OSCs.

In order to gain a deeper perception of the effect of hotcasting on the multidimensional distribution of donor and acceptor, film-depth-dependent light absorption spectroscopy (FLAS) was carried out and the sublayer absorption spectra of the RTcasting and hot-casting blend films are displayed in Figures 5a,b, respectively.[62-64] Both two blend films exhibit hierarchical vertical phase segregation with a small degree of energetic disorder, while stronger absorption intensity of each sublayer is observed in the absorption spectra of hot-casting films.[65,66] We further calculated the distribution of exciton generation rate within the active layer according to FLAS and a modified transfer-matrix optical model. For the two types of blend films, most of the excitons are produced in the region between the center and bottom of the active layer (Figure 5c,d). It is worth noting that the hot-casting blend films deliver obviously higher exciton generation rates in the depth range of $5 0 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ with respect to RT-casting blend films (Figure 5e), which is in part responsible for the increase of EQE and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of hot-casting OSCs.

More detailed information about the vertical distribution of molecules in blend films can be obtained by performing time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) characterization.[67,68] As shown in Figure 5f,g, the F element is the unique element of the donor PM6 and the Cl element represents the acceptor BO-4Cl. It is apparent that the Cl element with almost the same intensity is gradually enhanced as the increase of sputtering time in both RT-casting and hot-casting blend films (Figure S8a, Supporting Information). On account of very similar Cl element intensity in two blend films, the much stronger intensity of the F element at different depths of hot-casting blend films proves the existence of higher PM6 proportion in PM6:BO-4Cl matrix than that of RT-casting blend films (Figure S8b, Supporting Information), which can be clearly and intuitively observed from 3D images. Moreover, the F element from the hot-casting blend films presents a more gentle decline with increasing sputtering time relative to the RT-casting blend films. The falling slope of the PM6 is ${ \approx } 0 . 1 6$ in hot-casting films from the surface to the bottom, which is smaller than that of RT-casting films $( \approx 0 . 2 5 )$ , suggesting more PM6 existence near the anode of the bottom. Since the more donors near the anode and the acceptor close to the cathode, the more conducive to the charge extraction, the formation of meliorative vertical phase separation in hot-casting blend films contributes to charge transport and collection. According to the above results, the hot-casting strategy can not only optimize the ratio of donor/acceptor but also can manipulate the vertical phase separation of donor and acceptor in blend films.

# 2.4. Film Forming Kinetics

To clarify the original effect mechanism of different temperatures on the molecular distribution and arrangement in the active layer, the in situ time-resolved UV-vis absorption spectra of blend films were monitored to investigate the dynamic process of film formation.13,69-71 Figure 6a,b display the time-dependent contour maps of absorption spectra for the RT-casting and hotcasting blend films. As expected, the hot-casting strategy results in a faster phase transition process from solution to solid states for the active layer in comparison with the RT-casting method. Moreover, the hot-casting film exhibits stronger light absorption intensity, which is consistent with the results from FLAS characterization (Figure 5a-e). For gaining more detailed information about the film-formation process, we extracted the representative absorption spectra of the samples with RT-casting and hot-casting strategies. As shown in Figure 6c,d, the intensity of the absorption spectra for both samples go down first and then enhanced along with the prolongation of the spin-coating time. The weakened intensity of the absorption spectra mainly originates from the decreased content of blend solution during the spin-coating process and the enhanced intensity with redshift absorption peaks represent molecular aggregation. The absorption spectra of the RT-casting sample show no obvious variation after spin-coating at ${ \approx } 4 . 0 ~ \mathrm { s }$ , while that of the hot-casting sample will not change after ${ \approx } 2 . 8 ~ \mathrm { s }$ , further proving the accelerated film formation with the hot-casting strategy. Since the absorption peak locations reflect the aggregation properties of molecules in solutions or films, the maximum absorption peaks of PM6 and BO4Cl were plotted to study the individual assembly process of the donor and acceptor. As displayed in Figure 6e, based on the RTcasting process, the peak location of BO-4Cl showed significant redshift at ${ \approx } 3 . 4 ~ \mathrm { s }$ while the absorption peak of the PM6 barely change at the same time, suggesting that acceptor aggregation come before the donor during the film formation. In terms of the sample with the hot-casting strategy (Figure 6f), apart from the rapid film-formation process, the donor and acceptor selforganization occurs simultaneously, demonstrated by the synchronously changed absorption peaks of PM6 and BO-4Cl. In contrast to the two strategies, the behindhand PM6 aggregation with the RT-casting method will lead to spinning out more PM6 and reduced donor/acceptor ratio in the final films. This result goes against the formation of profitable vertical phase separation for donor and acceptor, which provides convincing explanations for the phenomena observed from the TOF-SIMS test. Based on the above analysis, the film-forming process with different engineering is outlined in Figure 6g,h. The hot-casting strategy assists in the fast and synchronous assembly of both PM6 and BO-4Cl, contributing to preferable film morphology, which is beneficial to charge generation and extraction.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated the active impact of the hot-casting strategy on the performance enhancement of OSCs based on different high-boiling solvents and matrices. For the PM6:BO-4Cl matrix, the hot-casting OSCs generate much higher PCEs of $1 8 . 0 3 \%$ in CB and $1 8 . 1 2 \%$ in OX with respect to the RT-casting devices. Similar variation tendencies are observed in PM6:BTP-eC9-based OSCs, top-ranked PCEs of $1 8 . 3 4 \%$ in CB and $1 8 . 5 2 \%$ in OX are acquired by employing the hot-casting strategy. The performance improvement should be primarily attributed to effective charge generation and extraction, benefiting from the ameliorative donor/acceptor ratio, vertical phase separation, and molecular stacking, which result from the fast and synchronous molecular assembly of both donor and acceptor in the active layer with hot-casting strategy. This work is helpful for getting insight into the blend morphology evolution and constructing high-performance OSCs with high-boiling solvents.
