Yaqi Tang, Christopher R. McNeill

ABstrAct: All-polymer solar cells based on blends of the low band gap polymers poly{[4,8-bis[(2-ethylhexyl)oxy]benzo[1,2- b:4,5-b']dithiophene-2,6-diyl][3-fluoro-2-[(2-ethylhexyl)carbonyl] thieno[3,4-b]thiophenediyl]} (PTB7) and poly{[N,N-9-bis(2-octyldodecyl)-naphthalene-1,4,5,8-bis(dicarboximide)-2,6-diyl]-alt-5,59- (2,29-bithiophene)} (P(NDI2OD-T2)) are demonstrated. The use of the donor polymer PTB7 instead of poly(3-hexylthiophene) results in a higher open-circuit voltage and an overall spectral response better matched to the solar spectrum. A power conversion efficiency of $1 . 1 \%$ is reported with a peak external quantum efficiency of $18 \%$ at a wavelength of $6 8 0 ~ \mathsf { n m }$ . The microstructure of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends is also investigated using a combination of grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS), near-edge X-ray fine-structure (NEXAFS) spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy (AFM), and scanning transmission X-ray microscopy (STXM). GIWAXS measurements show that PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends contain P(NDI2OD-T2) crystallites with a (100) thickness of $9 . 5 \ \mathsf { n m }$ dispersed in an amorphous PTB7 matrix. STxM measurements indicate a lack of mesoscale phase separation, with AFM and NEXAFS measurements revealing a P(NDI2OD-T2)-rich top surface with fibrillar morphology. These results indicate that the pairing of low band gap polymers as both donor and acceptor polymers in all-polymer solar cells may be an effective strategy for realizing high-efficiency allpolymer solar cells. $\circledcirc$ 2012 Wiley Periodicals, Inc. INTRoDuctION Conjugated polymers have attracted much attention as potential materials for low-cost thin-film solar cells.1 The high binding energy of excitons that are the primary product of photoexcitation requires the use of heterojunctions to dissociate excitons into electron-hole pairs.? Such heterojunctions are formed at the interface between materials with different electron affinity and ionization potential. Thin-film blends are typically used in efficient polymer solar cells where heterojunctions are distributed throughout the film.3 A blend morphology nanostructured on the length-scale of the exciton diffusion length $( \sim 1 0 ~ \mathrm { \ n m } )$ enables the majority of excitons to diffuse to and dissociate at heterojunctions while allowing for films thick enough to absorb a sufficient amount of light.3

The most efficient polymer solar cells use a $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ or $\mathrm { C } _ { 7 0 }$ derivative as the high electron affinity material (the electron acceptor) in conjunction with a low electron affinity-conjugated polymer donor' The efficiency of polymer/fullerene blends has improved dramatically over the last 10 years with efficiencies over $9 \%$ now reported.4 The high electron affinity and high electron mobilities of functionalized fullerenes are often cited as the reasons for the high performance of polymer/fullerene blends.1

Conjugated polymers with high electron affinities can alternatively be used as the electron acceptor in so-called allpolymer solar cells. There are a number of potential advantages of using a conjugated polymer as the electron acceptor including superior optical absorption and greater synthetic flexibility in tuning material properties. The performance of all-polymer solar cells has not experienced the same dramatic increase in efficiency despite efficiencies of nearly $2 \%$ being demonstrated over 10 years ago. Efficiencies of ${ \sim } 2 \%$ however have been reported in a number of all-polymer systems? with a power conversion efficiency of $2 . 7 \%$ recently reported for a polythiophene/polyfluorene copolymer blend.7 A potential reason for the persistent low efficiency of allpolymer solar cells is a lack of synthetic effort devoted to the development of suitable polymers with high electron affinity. Indeed, the vast majority of conjugated polymers developed for polymer solar cells are developed for use with fullerene acceptors.

The polymer poly([N,N-9-bis(2-octyldodecyl)-naphthalene1,4,5,8-bis(dicarboximide)-2,6-diyl]-alt-5,59-(2,29-bithiophene)} (P(NDI2OD-T2), see Fig. 1) has emerged recently as an attractive acceptor material for all-polymer solar cells due to its high electron affinity, high electron mobility, and near-infrared absorption band. Initial solar cell studies, however, have been disappointing with efficiencies of $1 . 4 \%$ or below being realized in blends with poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) or poly[3-(4-noctyl)-phenylthiophene].9-12 Similar results have been achieved for perylene-based acceptor polymers combined with polythiophene derivatives.13-16 Here, we investigate the pairing of P(NDI20D-T2) with the low band gap copolymer poly{[4,8-bis[(2- ethylhexyl)oxy]benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']dithiophene-2,6-diyl][3-fluoro2-[(2-ethylhexyl)carbonyl]thieno[3,4-b]thiophenediyl]} (PTB7; see Fig. 1 for chemical structure). Polymer/fullerene blends using PTB7 as the electron donor exhibit the highest literature-reported efficiency of any solar cell to date,4 with PTB7- possessing properties that should make it a better-matched donor polymer for use with P(NDI2OD-T2) than P3HT. Specifically, PTB7 possesses a deeper highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) than P3HT that should enable higher opencircuit voltages. Furthermore the absorption spectrum of PTB7 is red-shifted with respect to P3HT enabling a better utilization of the solar spectrum. We also believe that the use of low bandgap polymers as both the electron donor and electron acceptor in all-polymer solar cells in general is a promising strategy for improving the efficiency of all-polymer solar cells.

# EXPERIMENTAL

# Materials

PTB7 was supplied by 1-Material (batch YY2-253) with P(NDI20D-T2) supplied by Polyera Corporation (N2200 lot #CZH-X-135-22). PTB7 had a molecular weight $( M _ { \mathrm { W } } )$ of 70.0 $\mathrm { k g ~ m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ and polydispersity index (PDI) of 2.2, whereas P(NDI2OD-T2) had a $M _ { \mathrm { W } }$ of $9 6 . 6 ~ \mathrm { k g ~ m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ and PDI of 4.0.

# Device Fabrication

Devices were fabricated by spin-coating the active blend layer onto conductive glass substrates and subsequently evaporating top metal electrodes. Indium-tin oxide (ITO)- coated glass substrates were first cleaned in acetone, isopropyl alcohol followed by oxygen plasma treatment. Subsequently a thin $( \sim 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ layer of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) poly(styrenesulfonate) (Baytron P VPAI 4083) was deposited by spin coating. The PEDOT:PSS-coated ITO glass substrates were then introduced into a nitrogen glovebox and annealed for $3 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends were prepared with a 1:1 weight ratio by dissolving in anhydrous chlorobenzene at $1 0 \mathrm { ~ g ~ L ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ and spin coating in the glovebox onto the preprepared substrates at $1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ to form films ${ \sim } 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick. (A $7 0 \mathrm { - n m }$ -thick active layer was found to be the optimum active layer thickness for 1:1 blends from chlorobenzene.) The samples were then transferred to a vacuum evaporator where the top electrodes were deposited through a shadow mask. A $\operatorname { C a } / \operatorname { A l }$ cathode structure was used with $2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ of Ca evaporated first followed by $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ of Al. Devices were encapsulated before removal from the glovebox and testing. The device active area was 4.5 mm?.

# Device Characterization

Cell performance under simulated sunlight was performed using a Photo Emission Tech. model ss50AAA solar simulator with the current-voltage curves measured with a Keithley 2635 source meter. The intensity of the solar simulator was set using a calibrated silicon reference cell with a KG3 glass filter (PV Measurements). The output of the solar simulator was characterized with a spectroradiometer (PV Measurements) with a spectral mismatch of 1.02 determined for PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) cells.

External quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured as a function of wavelength by dispersing light from a tungsten filament (Newport 250 w QTH) through a monochromator (Oriel Cornerstone 130) with a spot size smaller than the device active area. Light intensities of less than $1 \ \mathrm { m W } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ were used with short-circuit current recorded using a Keithley 2635 source measure unit. The system was calibrated by placing a calibrated photodiode (Thorlabs FDS-100cAL) in the device under test position and referencing the intensity measured to that of another silicon photodiode that samples a portion of the beam via a beamsplitter and serves to account for any intensity fluctuations.

# Microstructural Characterization

Surface topography was characterized with a Veeco Nanoscope $\mathrm { v }$ atomic force microscope (AFM) using ScanAsyst mode.

Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIwAXS) measurements were performed at the SAXs/wAxs beamline at the Australian Synchrotron. $1 2 ~ \mathrm { k e V }$ photons were used with 2D scattering patterns recorded on an MAR-165 CCD detector. The sample-to-detector distance was calibrated using a silver behenate standard. A grazing angle of $0 . 1 0 ^ { \circ }$ was used that is above the critical angle of the polymer films but below the critical angle of the substrate. Data acquisition times of 60s were used with no evidence found for beam damage when comparing data taken at shorter and longer acquisition times. GIwAxs data were analyzed using the software SAxs15ID version 3299. X-ray diffraction data are expressed as function of the scattering vector, q, that has a magnitude of $( 4 \pi / \lambda ) \mathrm { s i n } \Theta ,$ where 0 is half the scattering angle and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the incident radiation.

Surface-sensitive near-edge X-ray fine-structure (NEXAFS) spectroscopy at the carbon edge were recorded at the Soft Xray Spectroscopy Beamline at the Australian Synchrotron.17 Nearly perfectly linearly polarized photons $( P \approx 1 )$ from an undulator X-ray source with high spectral resolution of $E / \Delta E$ $\leq 1 0 { , } 0 0 0$ were focused into an ultrahigh vacuum chamber on ${ \sim } 0 . 4 \times 1 { \cdot } \mathrm { m m }$ sample area. X-ray absorption was measured via Auger electron yield (AEY) using a SPEcS Phoibos 150 Hemispherical Analyzer set to a kinetic energy of 230 eV. The recorded signal was normalized to the incident photon flux using the "stable monitor method" in which the sample signal is compared consecutively to a clean reference sample and the time variations in flux measured via a gold mesh.18 The normalized spectra were scaled by subtracting a background, which scales according to the atomic scattering factors of the material before the onset of the first feature setting (i.e., the absorption measured at $2 8 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and then normalizing to the value at $3 2 0 ~ \mathrm { e V } .$

STXM measurements were performed at the PolLux beamline at the Swiss Light Source.19,20 Samples were prepared by floating off onto TEM grids. An X-ray energy of $2 8 7 . 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ was used that was found to maximize materials contrast based on the difference spectrum of the two materials. Full details can be found in previous publications.10,21

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1 presents the chemical structures, HOmO and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies and optical absorption spectra of PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2). The HOMO and LUMO values were measured using cyclic voltammetry and reported previously8,22 and are useful in helping to establish the suitability of a particular donor/acceptor combination. From the HOMO/LUMO values the pairing of PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2) should result in a type-II heterojunction with electron transfer from PTB7 to P(NDI2OD-T2) and hole transfer from P(NDI2OD-T2) expected.

The optical absorption measurements in Figure 1 are of ${ \sim } 1 0 0 { \cdot } \mathrm { n m }$ -thick films and show that the absorption band of PTB7 at $6 0 0 { - } 7 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ is stronger than the near-IR absorption band of P(NDI2OD-T2). P(NDI2OD-T2) is expected to sensitize the near-IR region, however enhancing the strength of the near-IR absorption band of acceptor polymers in general should be considered as part of future materials design. The blend spectrum represents a linear combination of the neat absorption spectra with the spectral features of the neat materials reflected in the absorption spectrum of the blend.

FIGURE 3 AFM images (a,c,e) and GIWAXS pattrns (b,d,f) of neat PTB7 (a,b); neat P(NDI20D-T2) (c,d); and the PTB7:P(NDI20DT2) blend (e,f).

Figure 2 presents the device characteristics of a $7 0 \mathrm { - n m }$ -thick active layer ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2)/Ca/Al device (device data for different active layer thickness can be found in the Supporting Information). The EQE spectrum reflects absorption features of both PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2) indicating that photoinduced charge transfer from both PTB7 to P(NDI2OD-T2) and P(NDI2OD-T2) to PTB7 is occurring. The current-voltage characteristics under simulated AM1.5G show a short-circuit current of $3 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , an open-circuit voltage of $0 . 6 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } ,$ and a fill factor of 0.39. The measured short-circuit current of $3 . 4 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { - 2 }$ matches closely that predicted by integrating the EQE spectrum with the AM1.5G spectrum $( 3 . 3 7 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { - 2 \cdot }$ ) consistent with the low spectral mismatch factor of the measurement system. From these device parameters, a power conversion efficiency of ${ \sim } 1 . 1 \%$ is calculated.

Figure 3 presents AFM images and 2D GIWAXS scattering patterns of neat films and blend. The topography of the PTB7 film is smooth and essentially featureless while the surface of the P(NDI2OD-T2) exhibits characteristic fibrils.23 The surface of the blend film has a similar fibrillar morphology to the neat P(NDI2OD-T2) film suggesting a top surface that is rich in P(NDI2OD-T2). The fibrils on the surface of the blend film however are smaller than those on the surface of the neat P(NDI2OD-T2) film.

![](images/dff1051816ea0405149d4132e7625020630975e67d02ab92e5b2be3b1312239c.jpg)  
FIGURE 4 GIWAXS-scattering profiles along (a) $\mathsf { q } _ { x y }$ and (b) $\mathbf { q } _ { z } .$

The GIWAxS patterns of both $\mathrm { P T B 7 ^ { 2 4 } }$ and P(NDI2OD-T2)25 have been reported previously and the indexing of peaks here follows these previous reports. The GIwAxs pattern of the neat PTB7 film, Figure 3(b), shows only weakly scattering features. A (1o0) peak is observed at $\sim 0 . 3 2 \mathring { \mathrm { ~ A ~ } } ^ { - 1 }$ and a broad (010) $\pi - \pi$ stacking peak at ${ \sim } 1 . 5 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ (see also profiles along $\mathrm { q } _ { x y }$ and $\mathbf { q } _ { z }$ shown in Fig. 4). The (100) peak is found predominantly in the $\mathbf { q } _ { x y }$ scattering plane with an increased (010) scattering intensity along the $\mathbf { q } _ { z }$ direction. Taken together, these observations indicate a preference for the PTB7 lamella to lie face-on the substrate in agreement with the observations of Hammond et al.24 The neat P(NDI2OD-T2) film, Figure 3(d), exhibits more pronounced scattering features than the PTB7 film indicative of improved ordering. As-cast P(NDI2OD-T2) films also exhibit preferential face-on packing with (100) and (001) peaks clearly observable along $\mathrm { q } _ { x y }$ (see Fig. 4) and a (010) $\pi - \pi$ stacking peak along $\mathbf { q } _ { z } .$ Consistent with previous observations25 the (100) peak is found at ${ \sim } 0 . 2 6 \mathrm { ~ } \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ , corresponding to d-spacing of $2 . 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ with the (010) peak found at ${ \sim } 1 . 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ^ { - 1 } }$ corresponding to $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance of $0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$

The GIwAxS pattern of the blend, Figure 3(f), largely resembles that of P(NDI2OD-T2) with an absence of features seen in the neat PTB7 scattering pattern. Thus, face-on oriented P(NDI2OD-T2) crystals are present in the blend with PTB7 being essentially amorphous. There is a subtle shift in the position of the P(NDI2OD-T2) (100) peak from ${ \sim } 2 . 6 \mathrm { ~ } \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the neat film to ${ \sim } 2 . 8 \mathrm { ~ } \mathring \mathrm { \ A } ^ { - 1 }$ in the blend indicating a smaller $d$ spacing of $2 . 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The P(NDI2OD-T2) peaks in the blend film are broader than in neat P(NDI2OD-T2) films. In particular, the (1o0) peak has a full-width at half maximum (FWHM) in the blend of $0 . 0 6 6 \mathring { \mathrm { ~ A ~ } } ^ { - 1 }$ compared with a FWHM of $0 . 0 3 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the neat film. The broader peaks in the blend spectrum indicate that the P(NDI2OD-T2) crystals are smaller in the blend than in neat P(NDI2OD-T2). Calculation of crystallite thickness along the (1o0) direction using Scherrer analysis yields a thickness of $1 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for P(NDI2OD-T2) crystals in neat P(NDI2OD-T2) compared with $9 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in the blend. The observation of smaller crystallites in the blend from the GIwAXS measurements also seems consistent with the smaller-sized fibrils observed on the surface of the blend film by AFM.

To provide additional chemical information regarding the surface composition, surface-sensitive NEXAFS spectroscopy measurements were performed. Using AEY detection mode, sensitivity to the top ${ \sim } 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ is achieved. Figure 5 presents the NEXAFS spectra of neat PTB7, neat P(NDI2OD-T2) and the PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blend. Spectra were acquired at an X-ray angle of incidence of $5 5 ^ { \circ }$ -the so-called "magic-angle" to rule out any orientation effects. Full angle-dependent data can be found in the Supporting Information.

The NEXAFS spectra of neat PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2) match previously reported spectra.23,24 Peaks below $2 9 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ correspond to transitions from carbon 1s core states to unoccupied $\pi ^ { * }$ orbitals which are characteristic of each material. The broad peaks above $2 9 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ correspond to $\complement 1 s { - \sigma ^ { * } }$ transitions and are super-imposed on a step-like profile corresponding to ionization of C 1s electrons to the continuum. For further discussion regarding the features of NEXAFS spectra of organic semiconductors, the reader is directed to ref. 26. The NEXAFS spectrum of the blend largely resembles the neat P(NDI2OD-T2) spectrum suggesting a P(NDI2OD-T2)-rich surface layer. Singular value decomposition of the blend spectrum returns a surface composition of 68 wt $\%$ P(NDI2OD-T2) and 32 wt $\%$ PTB7. Thus, the surface of the blend is P(NDI2OD-T2)-rich consistent with the morphology observed with AFM; however, there is still a significant fraction of PTB7 present.

Finally, scanning transmission X-ray microscopy experiments were performed to examine the bulk morphology of the blend. Imaging at $2 8 7 . 4 \ \mathrm { ~ e V }$ where significant contrast exits between PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2) no features could be discerned larger than the spot size of the X-ray beam ( ${ \sim } 3 0$ nm), see Supporting Information. Thus, PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends appear to exhibit a lack of mesoscale phase separation that has been problematic for other polymer blend systems.27,28

Our initial measurements on PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends thus indicate that it is a promising materials system. Compared with P3HT:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends prepared with similar processing conditions,1o higher short-circuit currents and open-circuit voltages are obtained. Furthermore, the morphology and microstructure of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends appear to be more favorable compared with the P3HT:P(NDI2OD-T2) system. In particular, the P(NDI2ODT2) crystallite size of $9 . 5 \ \mathrm { ~ n m }$ found in as-cast PTB7: P(ND12OD-T2) blends is smaller than the $2 5 \mathrm { - n m }$ -thick crystals found in as-cast P3HT:P(ND12OD-T2) films.28 Thus, P(NDI2OD-T2) is able to crystallize in PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends but maintains a crystal size similar to the exciton diffusion length. Additionally, there is a lack of mesoscale phase separation in PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends that for other all-polymer systems has been difficult previously to control.27,28^The low fill factor of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends suggests that device efficiency may be limited by interfacial charge separation rather than a suboptimal microstructure.

We anticipate increases in the device efficiency of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends through further optimization. Our initial experiments with thermal annealing and the use of conventional solvent additives (e.g., diiodooctane) however were not found to result in improved device performance. The use of chloronaphthalene that has been found to be effective in improving the microstructure and device performance of P3HT:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends11 was also not found to be effective on the PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) system. This observation may be due to the fact that the P(NDI2ODT2) crystallite size is close to optimum as chloronaphthalene was found to be effective at preventing P(NDI20D-T2) aggregation. Promising strategies for future optimization of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) devices will be the optimizing of contacts through the use of interlayers,? exploring inverted architectures,11 the use of novel solvent blends and different molecular weights.

# CONCLUSIONS

All-polymer solar cells based on blends of the low band gap copolymers PTB7 and P(NDI2OD-T2) have been fabricated with an efficiency of $1 . 1 \%$ achieved. The use of PTB7 as the donor polymer results in a higher open-circuit voltage compared with P3HT:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends along with a spectral response better matched to the solar spectrum. Microstructural investigation of PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) blends reveals P(NDI20D-T2) crystallites with a (100) thickness of $9 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ dispersed in an amorphous PTB7 matrix. STXM measurements indicate a lack of mesoscale phase separation, whereas AFM and NEXAFS measurements show a P(NDI2OD-T2)-rich top surface exhibiting a fibrillar morphology. The initial device efficiency achieved and the favorable morphological properties indicate that the PTB7:P(NDI2OD-T2) system is a promising all-polymer system for further device optimization.
