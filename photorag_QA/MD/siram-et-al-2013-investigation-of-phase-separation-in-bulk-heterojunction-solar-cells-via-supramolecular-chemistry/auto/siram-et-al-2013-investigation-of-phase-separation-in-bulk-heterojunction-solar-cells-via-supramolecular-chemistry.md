# Investigation of Phase Separation in Bulk Heterojunction Solar Cells via Supramolecular Chemistry

Raja Bhaskar Kanth Siram, Meera Stephen, Farman Ali, and Satish Patil\*

Solid State and Structural Chemistry Unit, Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore 560012, India

Supporting Information

ABSTRACT: In this work, we have prepared two donor-acceptor-donor (D-A-D) - conjugated oligomers to investigate the effect of phase separation on the performance of bulk heterojunction (BHJ) solar cells. These charge transfer low band gap $\pi$ conjugated oligomers (TTB and NMeTTB) were synthesized by Knoevenagel condensation of terthiophenecarbaldehyde and barbiturate appended pyran derivative. The thin film morphology of both the oligomers and along with electron acceptor [6,6]-phenyl-C60- butyric acid methyl ester $\mathsf { \bar { ( P C _ { 6 1 } B M ) } }$ was investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The blend of NMeTTB and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ thin flm yield highly ordered thin flm, whereas there was clear phase separation between TTB and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ in thin film.The BHJ solar cell was fabricated using a blend of NMeTTB and TTB with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ acceptor in 1:1 ratio as active layer, and a power conversion efficiency of $1 . 8 \%$ was obtained. This device characteristic was compared with device having TTB: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as active layer, and large difrence is observed in photocurrents. This poor performance of TTB in BHJ devices was attributed to the difference in the nanoscale morphology of the corresponding derivatives. We rationalize our findings based on the low charge carrer mobility in organic field-fect transistors and miscibility/phase separation parameter of binary components (oligomers and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ,$ in the active layer of bulk heterojunction solar cells.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

In recent years, organic photovoltaics (OPV) is proving to be a promising technology to harvest solar energy.' OPV has number of advantages over conventional silicon-based solar cells such as low cost, ease of processability, and flexibility.? -4 The high power conversion efficiency of OPVs is established based on the concept of bulk heterojunction (BHJ) in which the active layer is composed of physical mixture of donor and acceptor, Both small molecules-11" and conjugated poly. mers" S12-20 have been used as p-type donor materials, and fullerene derivatives used as an acceptor in BHJ cells. The efficiency of an organic solar cell (OsC) critically depends on the dissociation of exciton into free charge carriers at the interface and efficient collection of free charge carriers at respective electrodes. After exciton formation, it is desirable to have a donor-acceptor (D/A) interface within the exciton diffusion length to facilitate exciton dissociation and then charge separation. These free charges require percolating pathways to reach the respective electrodes. In case of semiconducting polymers, the exciton diffusion length is in the order of $1 0 { - } 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } , ^ { 2 1 }$ and hence, the effciency of charge separation in BHJ solar cells is significantly influenced by length scales of phase separation. However, phase separation in the active layer of BHJ is not limited to $2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ due to inherent thermodynamic instability of the blend phase. The differences in crystallization tendency of the fullerene derivative and conjugated polymer drive them to form macroscopic phase separated structures larger than $2 0 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ .The unoptimized morphology accounts for low power conversion efficiency (PCE) in many novel semiconducting polymers with near ideal molecular orbital energies. The film morphology can be finetuned by controlling phase separation during the flm formation, and various strategies such as introduction of additives, post annealing, and so forth have been employed in this regard.22 The other strategy which can be employed to tune the microstructure in BHJ is via supramolecular chemistry.23 This approach requires complementary functional groups which can undergo hydrogen bonding and form microstructures. In this work, we intend to study phase separation in a donor/acceptor blend via supramolecular chemistry approach. We prepared two oligomers (TTB and NMeTTB) by coupling donor-acceptor-donor (D-A-D) by vinylene spacer. TTB has a functional group which undergoes self-assembly by hydrogen bonding, whereas we disrupted the self-assembly by introducing a methyl group in NMeTTB. We chose barbiturate as a functional group for intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Barbiturate with its acidic proton (N-H) and electronegative oxygen $\scriptstyle \left( { \mathrm { C } } = { \mathrm { O } } \right)$ atoms presents itself as a suitable candidate to study the H-bonding effect on the microstructure and PCE in BHJ. Moreover, barbiturate has high electron affinity and can be coupled to electron rich donor moieties to prepare low band gap materials.

Targeting on this aspect and exploiting the donoracceptor-donor (D-A-D) concept to manipulate the electronic structure, we synthesized terthiophene barbiturate (TTB) which showed self-assembled nanoribbon morphology as we have discussed in our earlier report.?4 This nanoribbon morphology did not give good PCE in the BHJ device. It is also not clear whether the low PCE was due to barbiturate or if it was the effect of self-assembly within the donor moieties which leads to phase separation with large microcrystals with grain boundaries. To study it further, we synthesized NMeTTB where the self-assembly of TTB was disrupted by methyl substitution at nitrogen of barbituric acid to give NMeTTB(Figure 1). Herein, we present our findings of a new small molecule NMeTTB as a donor for BHJ device and the results are compared with TTB. The spin-casted film of NMeTTB and its blend with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ yield uniform thin flm without any aggregation. Organic solar cell devices were fabricated using a blend of both TTB and NMeTTB, with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ,$ in 1:1 ratio as active layer, in similar conditions. The results show poor current-voltage characteristics for the device with TTB: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as active layer, whereas device with NMeTTB $\mathrm { . P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ blend as the active layer rendered higher photocurrents and resulted in an efficient device. This could be due to the fact that TTB, even after having a well ordered morphology within itself, failed to form the interpenetrating networks when blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and was observed to be completely phase separated with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ These results highlight the importance of the nanostructured morphology of the active layer in BHJ devices.

3.4. Thermal Properties. The thermal behavior of the compounds was investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DsC) and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA). Both of the compounds are thermally stable with decomposition temperatures of more than $3 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Supporting Information Figure S2a). DSC indicates phase change at 209 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ for TTB and at $1 7 5 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for NMeTTB (Supporting Information Figure S2b). These temperatures were matched with the melting temperature of the derivatives. The high melting temperature in the case of TTB is due to intermolecular H-bonding. In the cooling cycle, crystallization temperature was observed at 200 and $1 3 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for TTB and NMeTTB, respectively. The DSC peak in the cooling cycle is sharp for NMeTTB, and it was nearly smeared for TTB. The large energy change in the case of TTB is probably related to the formation of the crystalline phase whereas NMeTTB remains more amorphous. This was further confirmed by thin film X-ray diffraction (XRD; Supporting Information Figure S3).

3.8. Photoluminescence Quenching Studies. The HOMO-LUMO energy levels of donor and acceptor materials play an important role in exciton dissociation. For efficient exciton dissociation, an energy offset of about $0 . 3 ~ \mathrm { { \ e V } }$ is necessary between the LUMO of the donor and acceptor according to literature reports.,31,32 Figure 7a shows the HOMO-LUMO energy levels of TTB, NMeTTB, and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ The energy offset between LUMOs of the donor (oligmers) with respect to $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ is greater than $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ This suggests that $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ may favor exciton dissociation if blended with TTB or NMeTTB.

The excition dissociation and photoinduced electron transfer can be confrmed by studying fluorescence quenching in a donor-acceptor blend thin film. Figure 7b shows the fluorescence of neat film of TTB, NMeTTB, and their blend film with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ with a ratio of 1:1. The photoluminescence of the donor material (oligomer) is completely quenched when blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as an acceptor. This result indicates that $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ is a suitable acceptor for TTB and NMeTTB to fabricate BHJ.

3.9. Photovoltaic Characteristics. The photovoltaic properties of TTB and NMeTTB were tested by blending with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ A device with $\mathrm { P 3 H T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ as active layer was also prepared as a control. The ratio of donor and acceptor in the active layer was 1:1 by wt $\%$ . For the sake of convenience, the devices are named as A, B, and C. The device architectures are as follows: (1) Device A: ITO/PEDOT:PSS/ NMeTTB: $\mathrm { P C _ { 6 1 } B M / C a / A l }$ (2) Device B: ITO/PEDOT:PSS/ TTB: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M / C a / A l } .$ (3) Device C ITO/PEDOT:PSS/ P3HT: $\mathrm { P C _ { 6 1 } B M / C a / A l }$

Figure 8 shows the $J { - } V$ characteristics of BHJ devices, and the photovoltaic properties are summarized in Table 1. In the control device C, the measured PCE was $2 . 2 \%$ with a fill factor of O.58. Device A has shown an improved performance over device B. The measured PCE of device A is $1 . 8 \%$ which is an order of magnitude higher than that of device B. The low efficiency for TTB based devices could be due to various factors including low charge carrier mobility, phase separation between TTB nanoribbons, and PCBM with large grain boundaries. We have investigated in detail these factors for low efficiency of device B. The open circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ for device A is $0 . 8 1 \mathrm { V } ,$ and the short-circuit current density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \right)$ is $5 . 4 3 ~ \mathrm { m A } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ , both of which are significantly larger when compared with $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ (0.32 V) and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ $( 0 . { \overset { \sim } { 9 } } 7 ~ \mathrm { m A } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ of device B. The low $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ in device B may be due to the formation of an insulating layer at the interface between the cathode (calcium) and active layer (TTB). Because, in TTB, the acidic amide proton is very active and may react with calcium to form an insulating layer between the active layer and the cathode. This is further confirmed by fabrication of an OSC device with aluminium (Al) as cathode, and we obtained a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of 0.43 which is higher than that for device $\mathbf { B } . ^ { 2 4 }$ The larger $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ in device A is attributed to an increased interconnectivity between NMeTTB and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ moieties as well as good charge carrier mobility of NMeTTB. To verify this, TEM studies were done on the blend film of NMeTTB and TTB with acceptor $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ TTB: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend film indicates clear phase separation between the TTB nanoribbons and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ clusters (Figure 9a). This does not facilitate exciton dissociation in the TTB: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend as is evident from device characteristics. However, spin-casted NMeTTB $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend film was uniform, and no phase segregation could be seen (Figure 9b).

# 4. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we have shown that the phase separation between donor/acceptor in BHJ devices significantly influences the performance of the cell. The cooperative hydrogen bonding in TTB yields well-defined structures and upon mixing with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ leads to clear phase separation between these two materials. We developed another sample NMeTTB to compare the data with TTB. The device based NMeTTB showed better photocurrents and was much more efficient when compared to the one with a blend of self-assembled TTB with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as the active layer. The results reported here proved that disrupting the self-assembly within the donor moieties helped in developing a better interdigitating network between donor and acceptor moieties which in turn enhanced the photovoltaic characteristics of corresponding barbiturate condensed pyran derivative. This clearly suggest that PCE is strongly influenced by the phase morphology of the active layer, and the insight given by this work further motivates use of the interface supramolecular chemistry approach in organic solar cells.
