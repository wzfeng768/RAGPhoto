# Narrowband Organic Afterglow via Phosphorescence Forster Resonance Energy Transfer for Multifunctional Applications

Xin Zou, Nan Gan, Mengyang Dong, Wenguang Huo, Anqi Lv, Xiaokang Yao, Chengzhu Yin, Ziyang Wang, Yuanyuan Zhang, Huan Chen, Huili Ma, Long Gu,\* Zhongfu An,\* and Wei Huang\*

Achieving multicolor organic afterglow materials with narrowband emission and high color purity is important in various optoelectronic fields but remains a great challenge. Here, an efficient strategy is presented to obtain narrowband organic afterglow materials via Forster resonance energy transfer from long-lived phosphorescence donors to narrowband fluorescence acceptors in a polyvinyl alcohol matrix. The resulting materials exhibit narrowband emission with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) as small as ${ 2 3 \ n m }$ and the longest lifetime of 721.22 ms. Meanwhile, by pairing the appropriate donors and acceptors, multicolor and high color purity afterglow ranging from green to red with the maximum photoluminescence quantum yield of $6 7 . 1 \%$ are achieved. Moreover, given their long luminescence lifetime, high color purity, and flexibility, the potential applications are demonstrated in high-resolution afterglow displays and dynamic and quick information identification in low-light conditions. This work provides a facile approach for developing multicolor and narrowband afterglow materials as well as expands the features of organic afterglow.

# 1. Introduction

Luminescent materials exhibiting narrowband emission and high color purity features are gaining increasing attention in fundamental science and practical applications, including light-emitting diodes (LEDs), lasers, high-resolution bioimaging, and so on.1-3] Currently, the mainstream narrowband emission materials can be roughly divided into quantum dots (QDs), perovskite-based emitters, and purely organic chromophores.4,5] Compared with the inorganic narrowband luminescent materials, organic counterparts exhibit their merits in biocompatibility, low cost, easy modification, and large-area fabrication.[6,7] Nevertheless, most organic luminescent materials generally suffer from broadband emission with large full width at half-maximum $\mathrm { ( F W H M > 6 0 ~ \ n m ) }$ , resulting in poor color purity.[8-10] The emission broadening of organic chromophores is ascribed to their intrinsic vibronic coupling between the ground and excited states, and structural relaxation at the excited states.11,12] Therefore, synthesizing organic chromophores with rigid and symmetrical structures as well as suppressing the intermolecular charger transfer and aggregation is favorable to solve the above issues for narrowband emission.[6]

Following these principles, recently, diverse organic narrowband emitters with unique multi-resonance boron-nitrogen (BN) frameworks featuring high color purity have been successfully exploited, which are further widely applied in light-emitting devices, biolabeling, and imaging.[13-22] Despite the achievement of narrowband fluorescence or thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) with short excited-state lifetimes in the range of nanosecond to microsecond, the organic afterglow materials (lifetime over 100 milliseconds) featuring narrowband emission and high color purity have been rarely explored.

Ultralong organic phosphorescence (UOP) and organic long persistent luminescence (LPL) materials are two types of emerging organic afterglow materials and have sparked considerable attention because of their advantages in long-lived excitons, large Stokes shifts, and rich excited state properties, showing promising applications ranging from information encryption and displays to sensing and bioimaging.[23-31] Nevertheless, according to their luminescent mechanism, the long-lived phosphorescence mainly originates from the spin-forbidden radiative transition of triplet excitons that arise from singlet excitons through an efficient intersystem crossing (ISC) process.[32] The persistent luminescence is attributed to the excited complex emission upon the recombination of long-lived charge-separated states.[29] During these processes, the chromophores inevitably undergo a large geometrical distortion and strong structural relaxations, as well as form the exciplexes between donor and acceptor, resulting in the materials displaying a large Stokes shift and broadband emission. Owing to these intrinsic characteristics, it remains a formidable challenge to develop an efficient strategy for achieving narrowband afterglow from purely organic materials.

Forster resonance energy transfer (FRET) has been demonstrated as one of the simplest and most accessible strategies to fabricate multifunctional afterglow materials via the combined merits of long-lived phosphorescent donors and functionalized fuorescent acceptors. Based on this approach, a series of smart responsive, multicolor emissive, and near-infrared organic afterglow materials have been developed.[33-40] Notably, FRET is a process of non-radiative energy transfer between the energy donor and acceptor through dipole-dipole interactions occurring at a long distance of 10-100 A.[41] It can efficiently avoid the formation of intermolecular charge transfer and complex between the donor and acceptor, providing a new platform to achieve narrowband afterglow. Meanwhile, the polymer matrix can well disperse the doped donor and acceptor, benefitting the occurrence of FRET. Hence, we reason that the synergy of long-lived phosphorescent donor and narrowband emission fluorescent acceptor through FRET in the polymer matrix may be an effective pathway to obtain narrowband organic afterglow (Figure 1a,b; Figure S1, Supporting Information).

To validate our hypothesis, we chose the appropriate phosphorescent donors and fluorescent acceptors with large emissionabsorption spectral overlap and co-doped in the polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix. Based on the efficient FRET, a series of narrowband polymeric afterglow materials with high color purity were obtained. The resulting organic afterglow materials exhibited FWHMs as small as $2 3 \mathrm { ~ n m }$ and the longest lifetime of $7 2 1 . 2 2 ~ \mathrm { m s }$ . Moreover, the afterglow colors can be widely regulated from green to red by optimizing the donor-acceptor pairs. This work not only provides a facile strategy for developing narrowband and multicolor afterglow materials but also opens a new way to expand the features of organic afterglow.

This illustration was further proved by the excitation-phosphorescence spectra of BP-PVA under ambient conditions. As shown in Figure 2d, with the excitation wavelengths varying from 230 to $3 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , the BPPVA film displayed a stable emission band with a maximum peak at $4 8 6 \mathrm { n m }$ . As for the energy acceptor BCA, the photoluminescence spectrum showed a green sharp fluorescent peak at $5 1 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ with a lifetime of 6.35 ns in dilute ethanol solution $( 1 0 ^ { - 5 } ~ \mathrm { M } )$ (Figure 2e; Figures S16 and S17, Supporting Information). It is worth noting that the absorption and emission spectra of BCA presented a good mirror-image relationship with a small Stokes shift of $8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , and the FWHM of the emission band was as small as $2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , demonstrating the narrowband emission feature. This could be ascribed to the negligible geometrical distortion between the ground and singlet excited states. In addition, the CIE coordinate (0.216, 0.708) of BCA in dilute solution was close to the green emission (0.170, 0.797) in ITU-R BT. 2020 standard, proving the high color purity luminescence (Figure 2b). The afterglow color of the co-doped PVA film was similar to the fluorescence of BCA in a dilute solution, indicating that the energy was successfully transferred from BP to BCA. # 2.2. The Mechanism of Narrowband Organic Afterglow via Phosphorescence FRET Process

To systematically investigate the phosphorescence FRET process between BP and BCA, we further fabricated a set of co-doped PVA films containing 0.1 wt. $\%$ BP and various weight concentrations of BCA from $0 . 0 0 2 \ \mathrm { w t } . \%$ to $0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { w t } . \%$ (S1-S6), respectively. As shown in Figure 3a, the S1 film displayed dual-band emissions at 350 and $5 1 8 \mathrm { n m }$ with a shoulder peak at $4 8 6 \mathrm { n m }$ , which were attributed to the fluorescence bands of BP and BCA, and the phosphorescence of BP, respectively. Nevertheless, with the doping concentration of BCA increased from S2 to S6, the emission intensities at 350 and $4 8 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ were gradually decreased in both steady-state and delayed photoluminescence spectra. In contrast, the emission intensities at $5 1 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ were significantly enhanced with the longest lifetime of $1 0 5 0 . 8 9 \mathrm { m s }$ , accompanied by the afterglow color changing from blue-green to green (Figure 3a; Figure S21, Supporting Information). Moreover, the time-resolved delayed photoluminescence spectra demonstrated that the afterglow mostly derived from BCA while with a negligible contribution of BP (Figure S22, Supporting Information). We also collected the delayed excitation spectra of BP-PVA and S1-S6 films at 486 and $5 1 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively. As shown in Figure S23 (Supporting Information), the S1-S6 films showed nearly identical spectral profiles with BP-PVA, indicating the afterglow of S1-S6 and the phosphorescence of BP originated from the same excited states. These results suggested that the efficient FRET process occurred between BP and BCA, which was further confirmed by measuring the lifetime profiles of these hybrid films. As depicted in Figure 3b, as the doping concentration of BCA increased, the lifetimes at $4 8 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ of S1-S6 exhibited an obvious descent trend from 1128.62 to $7 3 3 . 4 9 ~ \mathrm { m s }$ which matches well with the typical phosphorescent FRET feature.[41] Furthermore, the concentration increases of BCA resulted in the enhanced phosphorescent FRET efficiency $( \Phi _ { \mathrm { F R E T } } )$ from $1 . 3 4 \%$ to $3 5 . 8 8 \%$ between BP and BCA (Figure 3c). Meanwhile, the energy transfer distances (R) between BP and BCA in S1-S6 were also calculated ranging from 49.4 to $2 6 . 6 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ (Figure 3c), which all met the requirements of energy transfer within 10 to $1 0 0 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ Additionally, the rate constant of FRET $( k _ { \mathrm { F R E T } } )$ from BP to BCA was calculated as $0 . 4 9 { \mathrm { s } } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is nearly ten times larger than the rate constant of phosphorescence $( k _ { \mathrm { P } } )$ of BP $( 0 . 0 4 3 \ \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , proving the validity of phosphorescence FRET between BP and BCA (Table S2, Supporting Information). The oscillator strength $( f )$ for the triplet state of BP was also calculated to be 0.01, which is expected to be large enough for the dipole-dipole coupling with BCA (Table S2, Supporting Information). These results provide a possibility for achieving the energy transfer from phosphorescent BP to fluorescent BCA via a non-radiative FRET process (Figure 3c; Tables S2 and S3, Supporting Information). Impressively, with the aid of the efficient phosphorescence FRET, the FWHMs of afterglow emission bands in films S1-S6 were gradually narrowed. Particularly, the film of S6 showed the narrowest FWHM of $2 3 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , along with high color purity afterglow with the CIE coordinate (0.233, 0.666) (Figure 3d; Figure S24 and Table S4, Supporting Information).

To gain a deeper insight into the mechanism of the narrowband afterglow of purely organic materials, theoretical calculations on the monomers BP and BCA were carried out. Firstly, the optimized geometrical structures of BP and BCA were simulated. Compared to the BP with a larger variation of triplet excited state conformation from $0 . 0 5 ^ { \circ }$ to $3 5 . 9 1 ^ { \circ }$ , the fluorescence molecule BCA displayed a negligible geometrical distortion between $\mathrm { S } _ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ states (Figures S25-s27, Supporting Information), leading to the narrowband emission accompanied by the small Stokes shift. These calculated data were well consistent with the corresponding experimental results. Then, the Huang. Rhys (HR) factors were also calculated to quantitively describe the coupling strength between the structural change and vibration mode, which is proportional to the reorganization energy. As shown in Figure 3e and Figures S28 and S29 (Supporting Information), BCA showed one dominant mode at low frequency $( < 1 5 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } ) _ { \mathrm { ~ } }$ with an HR factor of about 0.13, much less than that of BP $( \approx 1 6 )$ . The reorganization energies of BCA in the ground and excited states were 328.60 and $3 2 0 . 5 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ A which were also significantly lower than the BP of 6857.44 and $1 5 5 9 9 . 0 9 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , indicating the molecule BCA possesses a smaller structural relaxation at excited states than the BP (Table S5, Supporting Information). These results further verified that the green narrowband afterglow originates from the BCA via phosphorescence FRET from the long-lived phosphorescence donor BP.

Taken together, we proposed a plausible mechanism for achieving narrowband afterglow from organic materials (Figure 4a). Under the UV lamp irradiation, the triplet excitons of the donor were populated from the singlet excitons through the ISC process in the co-doped PVA film. Benefitting from the rigid PVA matrix, the triplet excitons of the donor were well stabilized by forming multiple hydrogen bonds with PVA chains under ambient conditions (Figure s30, Supporting Information). Importantly, the donor and acceptor possess a large spectral overlap between the corresponding phosphorescence and absorption spectra, which leads to the efficient phosphorescence FRET from the long-lived phosphorescence donor to the narrowband fluorescence acceptor. Therefore, narrowband organic afterglow with high color purity was obtained after photoexcitation.

# 2.3. The General Strategy for Achieving Multicolor Narrowband Organic Afterglow

To validate the generality of our strategy, we then prepared a set of PVA hybrid films (E1-E5) featuring multicolor narrowband afterglow by choosing the appropriate long-lived phosphorescence donors and narrowband emission fluorescence acceptors. These donors and acceptors all possessed good spectral overlap between the phosphorescence and absorption spectra, favoring the occurrence of phosphorescence FRET (Figure S31, Supporting Information). As shown in Figures S32 and S33 (Supporting Information), under the irradiation of a UV lamp, these hybrid PVA films showed bright luminescence with dominant emission peaks at 512, 518, 537, 590, and $6 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for E1 to E5, respectively. Impressively, benefiting from the efficient phosphorescence FRET, the multicolor afterglow covering green to red with the FWHMs ranging from 25 to $4 1 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ could be achieved from these hybrid PVA films after switching off the UV lamp (Figure 4b; Figures S34 and S35, Supporting Information). Among the films E1-E5, the longest lifetime and maximum photoluminescence quantum yield were up to $7 2 1 . 2 2 ~ \mathrm { m s }$ and $6 7 . 1 \%$ , respectively (Figure 4c; Tables S6 and S7, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the corresponding CIE coordinates of E1-E5 were (0.206, 0.652), (0.225, 0.704), (0.344, 0.624), (0.562, 0.426), and (0.623, 0.373) at the vertex/edge of NTSC and ITU.R BT 2020 standards, respectively, demonstrating their high color purity (Figure 4d; Table S8, Supporting Information). To the best of our knowledge, compared with the reported purely organic materials, these hybrid PVA films simultaneously exhibited long lifetime and narrowband emission characteristics under ambient conditions (Figure S36, Supporting Information).

# 2.4. Applications of High Color Purity Afterglow Materials

Given the long-lived emission, high photoluminescence quantum yield, and high color purity features of these hybrid PVA materials, we demonstrated the potential applications in after glow displays, information encryption and identification, and so on. In principle, human eyes are more sensitive to the green region in the visible spectrum.l Therefore, green afterglow materials with high color purity and brightness are promising for safety signs under emergency scenarios. As presented in Figure 5a, for comparison, we first chose BP/BCA-PVA and the control film of NpH-PVA as the display materials, respectively. Although they have similar emission peaks at 518 and $5 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ the FWHMs exhibit absolute differences between BP/BCA-PVA $( 2 3 \mathrm { n m } )$ and NpH-PVA $( 9 1 \mathrm { n m } )$ films, resulting in distinct color. purity (Figure S37, Supporting Information). Then, photoelectric devices with BP/BCA-PVA and NpH-PVA films were fabricated based on light-emitting diode (LED) arrays via a circuit diagram (Figure S38, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the switch of LEDs arrays can be controlled by the relative computer programs.

As shown in Figure 5f, based on screen printing, a quick response (QR) code carrying the "IFE@NWPU" information was painted on a piece of paper using BP/BCA-PVA aqueous solution as inks. As the reported afterglow materials, the code can be identified by scanning the QR code when the excitation source was turned off due to the long emission lifetime feature. In contrast, the QR code made by the control polymer with low color purity and brightness afterglow could only be identified under the $3 1 0 \mathrm { n m } \mathrm { U V }$ lamp excitation requiring a long response time. After removing the UV lamp, the information cannot be identified by scanning anymore (Movie S4, Supporting Information). These results demonstrated the advantage of our narrowband organic afterglow materials with high brightness and resolution luminescence in dynamic and quick information identification in darkness over conventional organic afterglow materials.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have presented a facile strategy to achieve narrowband organic afterglow via phosphorescence FRET from polymer materials. The combination of the advantages of the long. lived phosphorescence donors and the narrowband emission fluorescence acceptor simultaneously endows the materials with long lifetime and high color purity luminescence features. The materials exhibit the narrowest afterglow FWHMs of $2 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ by adjusting the weight concentration of the acceptors, and the phosphorescence FRET efficiency can reach $3 5 . 8 8 \%$ . The good energy match between phosphorescence molecules BP or BNP and fluorescence BCA/BPH enables the long afterglow lifetime of 721.22 ms and the high photoluminescence quantum yield of $6 7 . 1 \%$ . Additionally, based on this efficient strategy, we achieved a series of multicolor narrow emission afterglow polymer materials ranging from green to red with high color purity by changing the appropriate donor-acceptor pairs. Given the unique features of materials in water-solubility, flexibility, high color purity, and long lifetime luminescence, the potential applications in high color purity afterglow displays, dynamic information encryption and identification were demonstrated. Our findings not only provide a facile approach for developing multicolor and narrowband afterglow materials but also open a new way to expand the features of organic afterglow for multifunctional applications.
