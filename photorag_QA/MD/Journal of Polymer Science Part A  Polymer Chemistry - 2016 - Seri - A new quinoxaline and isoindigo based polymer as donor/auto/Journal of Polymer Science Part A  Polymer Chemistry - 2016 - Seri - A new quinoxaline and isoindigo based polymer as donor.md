ABstrAct: A new semiconducting polymer based on two different electron deficient (quinoxaline and isoindigo) and electron rich (benzodithiophene) moieties is synthesized, characterized and used as donor material for photovoltaic devices. Bladecoated bulk heterojunction solar cells are fabricated in air by using chlorinated $o \mathrm { \cdot }$ -dichlorobenzene) and nonchlorinated (oxylene) solvents for the deposition of the active layer. The use of $o \mathrm { \cdot }$ -xylene allows a $\sim 1 0 \%$ improvement of the device efficiency in comparison to the analogous system processed from $^ o$ -dichlorobenzene. In addition, the evolution of the photovoltaic parameters of the resulting devices during thermal stress is monitored and compared, demonstrating a nearly identical resistance against temperature. The reported results not only highlight the promising properties of the new polymer in terms of environmental stability and compatibility with nonhalogenated solvents, but also show an easy and ecofriendly way to further improve the device performance without altering the corresponding thermal stability. $\circledcirc$ 2016 Wiley Periodicals, Inc. J. Polym. Sci., Part A: Polym. Chem. 2017, 55, 234-242

Keywords: BHJ solar cells; donor polymer; nonchlorinated processing solvent; thermal stability

INTRODUcTION Bulk heterojunction (BHJ) polymer solar cells (PSCs) feature properties as low-cost at large production, lightweight, flexibility and low environmental impact, that offer grounds to consider this renewable energy technology as one of the most promising in the emerging scenario. Over the years, the extensive growth of this technology led to PSCs with power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) approaching $1 2 \%$ 1 Particular efforts have been devoted for example in the synergic development of novel materials,? enhanced understanding of polymeric film microstructure, and meticulous device optimization.4 Despite the great achievements in terms of PCEs of lab-scale solar cells, other fundamental aspects such as processing conditions and stability of the resulting devices need to be considered for the upscale of PsCs toward industrialization and practical applications.

To date, most of the best performing PSCs are processed by spin-coating (often in inert atmosphere) from halogenated solvents, such as 1,2-dichlorobenzene ( $ ́ { o }$ -DCB) and chlorobenzene (CB). These solvents provide good solubility for both BHJ components and their relatively high boiling points allow an optimal self-organization and phase segregation of the polymer:fullerene blend through a slow evaporation during the deposition/drying processes. However, the use of halogenated-based solvents has an adverse impact on the human health and the environment, thus strongly limiting their employment for large-scale production. As a consequence, the choice of alternative ecofriendly solvents able to provide PsCs with equal and/or greater performance, in comparison to analogous reference systems processed from halogenated solutions, is of relevant importance.5

Recent works highlight the great effort of several research groups on the processing methods of PsCs, demonstrating for instance, that the replacement of common chlorinated processing solvents with halogen-free ones in combination to (laboratory-scale) deposition techniques fully compatible with roll-to-roll mass production, represent crucial aspects for organic solar cells to succeed as a technology.6

In agreement with this approach, we report here the synthesis and characterization of a new donor polymer PBDTQx-ii in which electron deficient (fluorinated quinoxaline and isoindigo) and electron rich (benzodithiophene) units, opportunely spaced by thiophene rings to extend the $\pi -$ conjugation and to limit steric interactions, are combined together. Interestingly, for this polymer we found that the replacement of the common halogenated processing solvent ( $ ́ { o }$ -DCB) with a nonchlorinated one ( $\cdot$ -xylene) allows the preparation of blade-coated BHJ solar cells (processed in air) with ${ \sim } 1 0 \%$ improved PCEs. Moreover, besides the evaluation of the initial performance, we also investigated, in comparison to the $o$ -DCB-processed reference system, the impact of the $o$ -xylene on the thermal stability of the corresponding devices. Thermal degradation tests at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ demonstrated that PBDTQx-ii based PSCs, processed from $o$ -DCB or $o$ -xylene, have a satisfying and nearly identical resistance against thermal stress, confirming the promising intrinsic properties of the new material, the general applicability of environmentally friendly processing conditions and highlighting the (poorly explored) effects/benefits in terms of device stability arising from the use of nonchlorinated solvents.

# OPV Device Fabrication and Characterization

All materials, PEDOT:PSs (poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(4-styrenesulfonate), Clevios P VP AI 4083, H.C. Starck), $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ ([6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester, Solenne BV) and anhydrous $o$ -dichlorobenzene $\cdot$ -DCB) was purchased from commercial sources (Sigma-Aldrich) and used without further purification. After the final sonication step, substrates were dried with a stream of Ar gas and then placed in an oxygen plasma chamber for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Next, a thin layer of ZnO $( \sim 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ was deposited by blade-coating technique in air on the top of the glass/ITO substrate and subsequently annealed at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $5 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ to remove residual traces of solvents. The active layers were prepared from solutions of $\mathrm { P 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ dissolved in $o$ -DCB or $o$ -xylene with or without $3 \%$ DIO with a total concentration of $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / m L }$ . The active solutions were deposited by blade-coating under ambient conditions. The processing conditions of each active layer were optimized (in terms of D:A ratio, thickness, deposition temperature and speed, annealing, etc...) to obtain and compare the best performing devices. The films were transferred inside a metal evaporator and kept under high vacuum $\approx 1$ $\times ~ 1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ Torr) for $^ { 7 2 \ \mathrm { h } , }$ to remove the residual amount of solvents and/or DIO. To complete the devices, $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ $\cdot 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ and $\mathrm { A g }$ $\mathrm { 9 0 ~ n m } )$ were subsequently deposited. The currentvoltage (I-V) characteristics of all devices were recorded by a Keithley 236 source-measure unit under $\mathrm { A M } 1 . 5 \mathrm { G }$ simulated solar irradiation, $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ (Abet Technologies Sun 2000 Solar Simulator). The light intensity was determined by a calibrated silicon solar cell fitted with a KG5 color glass filter to bring spectral mismatch to unity. The active area of the solar cell was exactly $6 \ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ . During testing, each cell was carefully masked, by calibrated mask, to prevent an excess photocurrent generated from the parasitic device regions outside the overlapped electrodes area. All solar cells were tested, without encapsulation, inside the glove box in oxygen and moisture free environment.

# Thin Film Characterization and Thermal Degradation

All thin-film characterizations were performed in air. Solution and film optical absorption spectra were recorded on a JASCO V-550 spectrophotometer. The thickness of the various films was measured by a profilometer (KLA Tencor, P-6). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were taken with a Solver Pro (NT-934 MDT) scanning probe microscope in tapping mode. The AFM images were recorded directly on tested devices. The thermal degradation tests were carried out inside the glove-box, by keeping the devices onto a hotplate at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ and monitoring their current-voltage (IV) characteristics.

# Photovoltaic Properties

To evaluate the potential and the impact of the processing conditions on the photovoltaic properties of the donor polymer P1, we prepared a set of BHJ solar cells using different solvents to process the active layer ( $\cdot$ -DCB and $o$ -xylene were found to be the best candidates in terms of solubility and chemico-physical properties). An inverted device configuration, glass/ITO/ZnO/active layer/ $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { A g } ,$ was employed to achieve the best performance and to perform thermal degradation tests (vide infra). Further details for the device fabrication and characterization are given in the experimental section. Importantly, all photovoltaic devices were fabricated (except the top electrode) by blade-coating in air, which represents an easy, robust, reproducible and fully scalable approach to a roll-to-roll process.15

Table 2 summarizes the photovoltaic response data, including open circuit voltage $\left( V _ { \mathrm { { O C } } } \right)$ , short-circuit current density $\mathrm { ( } J _ { \mathrm { S C } } )$ fill factor (FF), and power conversion efficiency (PCE), of the most efficient $\mathrm { P 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ based solar cells processed from the different solvents. The corresponding current density-voltage $\left[ \mathsf { J - } \mathsf { V } \right]$ plots and UV-vis absorption spectra are shown in Figure 2.

The optimal D:A ratio (wt/wt) for $\mathrm { P 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend was found to be 1:2 both for $o$ -DCB and $o$ -xylene processed films. This is the result of an appropriate and fine optimization in terms of processing conditions, thickness, D:A ratio, thermal treatments, different acceptor materials and device configurations. Moreover, we also proved that the halogenated solvent additive such as DI0,16 used here to improve the OPV performance, can be effectively replaced with nonhalogenated analogous, able to guarantee comparable benefits in terms of PCE (Supporting Information Table S1). By comparing the OPV responses of the optimized 1:2 (wt/wt) $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ based devices, it can be appreciated the slight enhancement of the PCE (from 4.0 to $4 . 5 \%$ J arising from the use of $o$ -xylene as a processing solvent. Figure 2(A) demonstrates that the main difference between the two systems is represented by the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ accompanied by a slight variation of the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ . In particular, the $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend processed form $o$ -DCB exhibits an average value of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } = 8 . 1 \ \mathrm { \ m A } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ and $V _ { 0 \mathrm { C } } = 0 . 7 8 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ while the values for the analogous system processed from $o$ -xylene are $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } = 8 . 9 ~ \mathrm { m A } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ and $V _ { 0 \mathrm { C } } = 0 . 7 9 \ \mathrm { V } .$

The unchanged FF $( 6 4 \% )$ indicates an optimal nanoscale morphology and charge transport property of both optimized P1 based blends.

The slight $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ improvement $( \sim 1 0 \% )$ of $o$ -xylene processed devices, in comparison to analogous $o$ -DCB based systems, can be ascribed to the increased intensity of the corresponding absorption profile [Fig. 2(B)]. The improved light harvesting, further contributing to exciton and free charge generation, reflects the different thickness of the optimized blends ( ${ \sim } 7 5$ and ${ \sim } 9 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ for $o$ -DCB and $o$ -xylene processed films, respectively, Table 2). To note, slight variations of the optimized thicknesses led, for both cases, to reduced device performance, mainly affecting the corresponding $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF values. Indeed, by further increasing the thickness of each blend, the gain in terms of light harvesting/generated current $\left( J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \right)$ is lower in comparison to the drop in FF, thus limiting the resulting PCE. On the other hand, for thinner films the reduction of the $\mathrm { J } _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ becomes predominant compared to the benefits in terms of charge transport (FF), still lowering the device performance.

Interestingly, the achievement of the best compromise between light harvesting (thickness), generated current, morphology and charge transport/collection for $o$ -xylene processed blends represents an important aspect that highlights the key role of the processing solvent on the selfassembly of the D:A blend.17 Indeed, the possibility to have a slightly thicker and more efficient $\mathrm { P 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend using oxylene as a processing solvent, is likely the result of a fine and ideal polymer entanglement and/or backbone orientation during the drying process. This in turn might further promote $\pi - \pi$ interchain interactions accompanied by a proper BHJ morphology, responsible for suitable charge transport properties with minimized recombination even for thicker films.

To further clarify the effect of different processing solvents on the self-organization of the D:A materials, we investigated the morphological differences of $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ blend films by tapping-mode AFM. Figure 3 shows the topographic images of optimized 1:2 (wt/wt) $\mathrm { P 1 } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ based cells processed from $o$ -DCB:DIO and $o$ xylene:DIO $[ 9 7 ; 3 ~ \mathrm { ~ v / v } ]$ [Fig. 3(A,B], respectively].

As expected, the surface morphology of the resulting films is quite similar, with morphological features that correlate well with the photovoltaic responses. The images reveal a proper BHJ morphology for both processing conditions, with defined domains suggesting the formation of relatively organized D:A nanostructures likely responsible for the generation of an optimal nanoscale phase separation, in agreement with the remarkable OPV performance. However, despite the absence of evident morphological differences, by carefully looking at the details further considerations and hypotheses can be argued. The topographic image of the blend processed from $o$ -DCB [Fig. 3(A)] is characterized by a more regular and homogeneous surface with relatively less defined domains likely suggesting a modest aggregation and/or ordering of the polymer chains, in accordance with the need of slightly thinner films to guarantee optimal charge transport properties and thus overall PCEs.

The same blend processed from $o$ -xylene seems to show a relatively rougher surface [Fig. 3(B)] with fine and structured domains which might indicate a higher degree of polymer aggregation/ordering in agreement with the above mentioned fine reorganization of the polymer and/or fullerene molecules, enabling thicker films and hence enhanced $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and PCEs. Moreover, the supposed enhanced selforganization of the BHJ blend processed from $o$ -xylene correlates well with the modest increase of the corresponding $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ (0.01 V) which is known to strongly depend on nanostructural factors of the BHJ blend such as D:A interfaces, molecular distances, aggregation, orientation/conformation of the molecules, etc.3(a),18

As a result, we found that $o$ -xylene is an alternative and optimal solvent to effectively process P1-based solar cells, allowing the deposition of relatively thicker films with a proper morphology able to provide BHJ devices with enhanced performance in comparison to the corresponding reference system processed from $o$ -DCB.

# Thermal Stability of BHJ Devices

Besides the evaluation of the initial PCEs of $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ based solar cells, we further investigated the impact of the processing solvent on the thermal stability of the resulting devices, by monitoring the evolution of the photovoltaic parameters during thermal degradation. We studied and compared solar cells with identical structures, therefore the relative differences between the devices can be mainly related to the different stability of the active blends, since internal interfaces and/or buffer layers are the same.

Thermal degradation tests were carried out in inert atmosphere (glove-box), by keeping the devices onto a hot plate at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ in dark, in accordance with the IsOs-D-2 standard testing protocol.19 In parallel, the shelf life of identical devices was also evaluated by monitoring the photovoltaic response of the cell stored at room temperature for $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ in glove box.

The resulting PCE drop was ${ < } 3 \%$ (data not reported), thus allowing to mainly ascribe the observed decrease of the photovoltaic performance to degradation processes induced by temperature.

The surface morphology of degraded films is similar to that of the fresh devices, maintaining the same relative features/ differences above mentioned. In particular, after thermal aging we still found that the aged $o$ -DCB-processed film [Fig. 5(A)-aged] shows a quite regular and homogeneous surface with the generation of bit more defined but apparently less connected domains, while the same blend prepared from oxylene [Fig. 5(B)-aged] exhibits finer and more textured nanostructures which might suggest a higher D:A intermixing, likely responsible for the increased internal resistances. The resulting BHJ films, compared to the reference system prepared from $o$ -DCB, not only allow a ${ \sim } 1 0 \%$ improvement of the initial device efficiency but also exhibit a comparable resistance against thermal degradation.

# CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we reported the synthesis and characterization of a new polymer PBDTQx-ii based on the combination of three different structural units: quinoxaline, isoindigo, and benzodithiophene based building-blocks. This polymer, blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and deposited by blade-coating (in air) using $o$ -xylene as processing solvent, led to improved solar cells with PCEs up to $4 . 7 \%$ . Interestingly, in comparison to the analogous reference devices processed from oDCB, we demonstrated that the use of a nonhalogenated solvent represents an easy and ecofriendly way to further improve the device performance without altering the corresponding thermal stability. The possibility to use cheaper, less toxic, and less harmful solvents to fabricate efficient and stable solar cells represents an important step forward for the upscale of this technology.
