ABSTRACT: Macromolecules that exhibit both electron transport and ionic mass transport (i.e., mixed conducting polymers) are ascendant with respect to both emerging application spaces and the elucidation of their fundamental physical principles. The unique coupling between the two modes of conduction puts these materials at the center of many next-generation organic electronic applications. The molecular details of this coupling are also at the epicenter of outstanding questions about how these materials function; how monomer and macromolecular chemistry dictates observable properties; and ultimately, how these macromolecular materials can be rationally designed, processed, and implemented into high-performance devices. Here, we focus on what is currently known about coupled ionic-electronic transport in these polymers and where there are open opportunities in the feld. These opportunities include the syntheses of designer macromolecules, the ned for significant simulation efforts that provide molecular-level insights into the mixed conduction mechanism, and the need for advanced characterization techniques for real-time monitoring of polymer morphology, as this iscitcal tocoupled ion-charge transport processes. Considering the early stage of this important subheld of polymer science, we also present our view of how the development of mixed conductors can benefit from the lessons learned from previous polymer. based electronic devices.

The rapid progress regarding these materials and devices has been driven by a strategy that leverages design rules from adjacent materials classes (e.g, semiconducting macromolecules and polymer electrolytes), and excellent reviews exist on these important classes of materials for readers interested in decoupled effects.12-i5 However, when both species are present, the ionic transport and electronic transport of organic mixed ionic-electronic conductors is modulated by the coupling between these two conduction mechanisms and leads to new design considerations that are not present in either polymer electrolytes or semiconductors that conduct only one of the two species. Here, we focus on the unique aspects of the pairing between ionic and electronic components in mixed macromolecular conductors in order to highlight both the promise of this emerging class of materials and the opportunities that exist for the rapidly growing community of polymer scientists and engineers, device physicists, and end users.

The logic motivating the rapid expansion of these mixed conducting polymers is rather straightforward if the history of the polymer electronics field is followed. That is, while there is always an effort to move forward in advancing the fundamental transport properties of soft materials, there are also clear application niches in which mixed ion-charge conducting polymers have a distinct advantage relative to their inorganic counterparts. For instance, organic electronic materials tend to have charge carrier mobility values that are less than what is achieved for many single crystal (or even polycrystalline thin film) inorganic materials; however, this concern is less of an issue for applications where ion and charge conduction are both required, as the rate of ion transport is significantly slower than that of charge transport. Moreover, the "soft' nature of polymeric conductors affords these materials the opportunity to interface well with liquid phases and allows volumetric absorption of electrolytic media into the conducting film; again, this contrasts with most "hard", inorganic semiconductors. Therefore, as the community identifies the key differences between inorganic and organic ion and charge conductors, it also has been able to move forward with respect to applications where mixed volumetric conduction is critical and where polymers can serve as the active layer components. In this way, the end-use application has helped drive materials selection. However, materials design toward these targeted applications (a few of which are highlighted below) has yet to occur in full, and this is one of the key areas where polymer scientists will play a defining role in moving the entire field forward.

That is, a paradigm shift in the manner in which polymers are designed for mixed-conduction applications needs to occur from one that is focused on end-use performance to one that is rooted in fundamental insights. To enable the rational design of new organic mixed conductors, to advance the fundamental physical paradigms that govern transport in these materials, and to improve the performance of functional devices relative to the state of the art, an elucidation of ion and electron coupling is required that ultimately connects polymer chemistry to application figures of merit. Thus, after providing an overview of the design of mixed organic conductors, we describe phenomena in mixed conductors where ionic-- electronic coupling plays a central role in (i) doping and dedoping mechanisms, (ii) morphological changes with respect to ion penetration, and (ii) ion-coupled charge transport. In each of these cases, the ion-conducting and charge-conducting macromolecular components cooperatively participate, which results in unique design considerations that are not present in each component considered separately. Finally, we highlight opportunities based on these central ideas that stress how developing a predictive understanding that ties macromolecular structure with nanoscale properties and ultimate performance would establish a clear foundation for sustainable progress in the field.

One of the key limitations in achieving both fundamental and applied aims in this arena is the relatively small number of materials explicitly designed for mixed conduction. To date, the syntheses of materials exhibiting mixed conduction typically have been achieved by combining known ionconducting and charge-conducting components into a single polymeric material. In this approach, polyelectrolyte species are introduced to facilitate ion transport, while electronically active moieties are introduced to facilitate charge transport (Figure 1). The means by which these different moieties can be introduced varies along many macromolecular design lines (e.g., as homopolymers with a charge-conducting backbone and ion-conducting side chains or as A-B diblock copolymers, where one of the two blocks is charge-conducting, while the other block is ion-conducting). This allows for the creation of bulk materials or thin flms that have relatively uniform distributions of the two types of conductors throughout or where specific domains are formed for a specific conduction task. Depending on the application, it may be desirable for only one or both conducting channels to macroscopically percolate the film, with the latter being the most common use case in organic electronic applications.

Following this type of modular approach, numerous organic materials have been synthesized that exhibit mixed ionicelectronic transport.6-18 To first order, the choice of chargeconducting component determines the range of compatible dopants, achievable charge density, and the electrical conductivity of the material, while the choice of ion conducting component determines electrolyte compatibility, ion adsorption, and the ionic conductivity of the material. As elaborated below, this description is too naive, as all these properties are influenced by ion-electron coupling. Because the dopants are often ions absorbed from a gated electrolyte, the ionconducting component mediates the doping of the chargeconducting moieties, as shown in the left-most panel of Figure 1a. The ion percolation additionally modifies the resulting polymer-ion configurations, while being itself controlled by these configurations (Figure 1a, center).20,21 Likewise, carriermobility and ion-mobility are potentially coupled with one another through electrostatic interactions and mutual trapping (Figure 1a, right).22,23

Given the potential modularity associated with a number of these design approaches, most of the chemical groups introduced for electronic and ionic conduction have been borrowed from adjacent fields in organic electronics and have not been designed specifically for the purpose of mixed ionelectron conduction. Of course, this is a reasonable first approach; however, it does show the significant opportunities that could be had for polymer chemists as most of the groundbreaking work in the field has relied on well-known, commercially sourced materials. For example, the most commonly used organic mixed conductor, poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) doped with poly(styrenesulfonate) (PE DOT:Pss), was originally developed as an antistatic coating, rather than for mixed conducting applications.2425 There is no doubt that PEDOT:PSS has served the early stages of this growing community well; however, it is clear that advanced macromolecular designs will be required in order to push the bounds of the field, both in terms of fundamental understanding and in terms of translational devices. This is not unlike other organic electronics applications where a champion material has helped establish the feasibility of soft materials in applications at the outset of the field; then, as time progresses, polymer chemists have joined the field in order to make significant contributions in terms of macromolecular design. For instance, other variants of dioxythiophene,26,27 thiophenes20,28,29 and thienothiophenes30 (groups that are wellrepresented in organic semiconducting and conducting materials) are extant in mixed conductors exhibiting p-type transport, and naphthalene diimide31,32 is prevalent in mixed conductors exhibiting n-type transport (see Figure 1 for specific chemical structures for oft-used mixed-conducting polymers). As with many end-use organic electronic applications, the final performance of the macromolecules in application are coupled, in a not-necessarily obvious fashion, to the design of the polymer backbone and the polymer side chain, and these macromolecular features are tied to the processing and (intentional or unintentional) doping within the operating platform.

In fact, this complicated and coupled relationship between charge and mass transport is a fundamental issue in many realms of organic electronics, but the subfield of mixed conduction brings this topic to the fore in a manner not previously discussed in detail. However, this is not to say that there is not a solid base from which to work. For instance, a longstanding observation in the polymer electronics community is the ability to manipulate the charge carrier density of macromolecular semiconductors through the introduction of molecular and ionic dopants.37-39 Of course, molecular and ionic doping efforts in the realm of conducting polymers continues with impressive results to this day, and excellent summaries of how to maximize electronic performance for these types of applications are present.12,19,40 Moreover, the past decade has highlighted device geometries that have allowed for the reversible doping and dedoping of polymer semiconductors in a relatively controlled fashion. At the fore of these studies was the work associated with lithium salt-doped poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) dielectric layers in organic fieldeffect transistor (OFET) test beds (Figure 2a). Importantly, these efforts highlighted that a common conjugated polymer, poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT), could achieve a metallic conduction mechanism through the proper inclusion of ionic dopants.41-43 This work was extended by changing the dielectric layer to one based on ionic liquids. In these ionic liquid-gated transistor test beds, large charge carrier mobility values, relatively fast switching speeds, and long-term device stability metrics were demonstrated; this made these types of devices interesting from an applied standpoint.44 From a fundamental perspective, a significant amount of electronic and spectroscopic evaluation was performed in order to elucidate the underlying mechanism associated with this high performance.45 While some debate exists as to the penetration depth of the ions into the semiconducting polymer active layer, it was clear that the main mechanism of doping in this case was based on electrostatic interactions associated with the ionic double layer that was formed at the ionic liquid-semiconducting polymer interface.46-49 Because of this fact, only the interface of the charge-transporting polymer interacted considerably with the ion-transporting regime; however, a significant opportunity exists for new transport properties to be achieved if a larger portion of the charge-conducting species interacted with the ion-containing phase.

In fact, ionic dopants recently have been added (usually in a reversible or quasi-reversible manner) intentionally to the active layer of myriad organic electronic platforms, and this has led to devices with impressive performance metrics. These ionic dopants often appear as small molecule materials, but the dopants can appear as polyelectrolytes as well.19,40,50,s1 In either instance, the advantage of this type of operating scheme is that the entirety of the charge-conducting moiety has the opportunity to interact with the ion-transporting regime as well (Figure la, left). A clear concern with this strategy is the possible irreversibility of the doping due to the potentially hindered transport of ions through a charged polymer matrix, and this concern caused much of the community to shy away from these types of mixed-conducting macromolecules and mixed-conducting blends for a number of years. Interestingly, while there is some hysteresis (i.e., macroscopically observable transport signatures that are often referred to as "break in" regimes) associated with coupled ion and charge transport in these types of systems, many of these mixed conducting systems demonstrate fairly impressive operational stability after this initial "break in" period is cleared.52 Therefore, there is a pressing need to push the bounds of how these materials can be implemented in practical devices, and a leading application space for mixed ion and charge conduction in macromolecular materials is the organic electrochemical transistor (OECT).

The geometry of an OECT is similar to that of bottom contact-top gate OFET, where the gate dielectric has been replaced by an ion-containing electrolyte that significantly penetrates into the volume of the active layer device (Figure 2b), and comprehensive reviews regarding the device physics and device operation of OECTs are readily available.17,18 From a macromolecular design perspective, a fragile balance must be struck that allows for (1) significant uptake of the electrolytic solution throughout a large portion of the polymer thin film, while preventing dissolution; (2) reversible transport of ions, even when the polymer is charged; (3) transporting charge while not allowing ionic dopants to serve as trap sites within the polymer film; and (4) either high or low sensitivity to the surrounding media (e.g, background electrolyte, analytes of interest, and confounding species), depending on the proposed end-use application of the OECT device. Fortunately, the commercially available polymer PEDOT:PSS fits the four criteria described above for many OECT applications, and this has allowed devices based on PEDOT:PSS to lead the charge in many high-end bioelectronic sensors.3,3 Moreover, it provides a useful handle from which to base molecular design principles for next-generation polymers as well. For instance, the inclusion of specific side chains and branch points for all repeat units or some repeat units (i.e., oligo(ether)-functionalized propylenedioxythiophene (ProDOT)-based copoly$\mathrm { m e r } ^ { 2 6 } .$ ) typically allows for the controlled uptake of a given electrolyte system. However, this simple rule of thumb does not capture the complex interactions associated with coupled ion and electron transport, nor does it adequately account for the changes to microstructure associated with these substitutions. Therefore, even in this specific mixed conductor application arena, there is much work to complete for general principles to be developed, and this is true both at the electronic level and at the structural level of the polymeric films as well. As such, we anticipate that, moving forward, advances in macromolecular design will play key roles in creating nextgeneration mixed conducting polymers.

Polymer morphology also plays a central role in all aspects of mixed conduction as it is both the cause and effect of ionelectron coupling in mixed conducting systems, and real-time monitoring of changes in morphology during device operation can prove challenging. That is, changes in morphology, even while keeping the polymer constitution fixed, can modulate both conducting channels by orders of magnitude and affect electrolyte absorption. Amorphous morphologies exhibit increased absorption of electrolytic media, which, in turn, can determine whether the material exhibits volumetric or interfacial doping with gated electrolytes.29 In this way, the morphology of the pristine material mediates ion-electron coupling through the spatial distribution of dopants and the degree of doping. Conversely, upon electrolyte absorption, these materials swell and can undergo qualitative morphological changes. This is vividly illustrated by break-in effects that occur during the first few (de)doping cycles of functional devices.4 Because electron transport is particularly susceptible to structural changes, it is expected that electrolyte-driven changes in morphology will likewise modulate electronic transport (Figure 1a, center).2,55

The details of morphological rearrangement during operation and the characteristics that constitute "favorable" morphologies are still being explored, and there is little doubt that significant insights from polymer physicists will be required in order to address this critical issue. The baseline expectation is that amorphous morphologies favor ion transport while crystalline morphologies favor electron transport. This has led some studies to consider whether there is an 49 intrinsic optimum that balances these two considerations. However, the existence of many examples of high mobility amorphous semiconductors suggests that this trade-off is not intrinsic and can be overcome through polymer design.56-58 Alternatively, when employing electron-conducting units that require crystallinity, it is also possible to engineer morphologies that exhibit both high crystallinity and sufficient porosity to facilitate ion transport and doping.59,60

Of course, critical to the success in this area will be the results that arise due to synergistic interactions between experiment, computation, and theory. For instance, molecular simulations can play an important role in elucidating the mesoscale morphological changes that accompany absorption of the electrolyte and (de)doping. However, a challenge of these studies is the large simulation length scales and time scales necessary to address these phenomena. Coarse-grained (CG) models of mixed conductors are well-suited for this purpose, because they retain sufficient chemical detail to make predictions for real polymer chemistries while addressing mesoscopic length scales. Recent efforts have demonstrated the effect of oxidation levels and $\pi$ aggregation on PEDOT:tosylate and PEDOT:PSS films in the presence of electrolytes.61,62 In our own group, we have developed CG models of broader classes of mixed polymer conductors to characterize the effect of chemical changes on the swelled polymer morphology and ultimately to control these features through polymer design. In Figure 3, we present a subset of results from an ongoing study investigating the effect of side-chain hydrophilicity on the spatial distribution of the aqueous electrolyte phase within the morphology.

Here we have analyzed the degree of connected clusters in both the aqueous and electronically conducting components as a surrogate for ion and electron transport, respectively. Intuitively, we observe that increasing the hydrophilicity of side chains leads to intimate mixing of the aqueous and organic phases with concomitant breakup of the large conducting clusters. We also find that for balanced mixtures of polar and apolar side chains, aqueous channels can be established that retain high order within the conducting organic phase. Whether mesoscale aqueous/organic phase separation is desirable is currently unknown, as the relationship between morphology and application figures-of-merit (e.g., the carrier and ion mobilities and channel transconductance) are still under investigation. In addition to side-chain hydrophilicity, many other polymer characteristics must be systematically studied and connected with morphology during operation. For instance, the density and topology of side chains can also be varied to influence the spatial distribution of dopants. Likewise, the relative energies of $\pi - \pi$ stacking and side-chain ion interactions is expected to modulate the degree of polymer aggregation. These relationships are still hypothetical due to the limited materials exploration that has been performed to date and the shortage of experimental methods for characterizing functional morphologies at the molecular scale. We anticipate that simulation methodologies like this will play an important role moving forward in balancing subtle trade-offs in side-chain engineering to design mixed conductor morphologies; this, in turn, will impact the coupled transport in the electrochemically active macromolecules.

Mixed conductors are distinguished from organic semiconductors and organic electrolytes by the presence of mobile oppositely charged species both within the polymer and absorbed by the polymer, which presents the possibility of microscopic coupling between ion and electron transfer. Considering the analogous situation in doped organic semiconductors, where dopants are immobile relative to electronic diffusion, provides insights into the new physics still being examined in mixed conductors. In doped organic semiconductors, strong Coulomb interactions between the ionized dopant and the low dielectric semiconductor leads to a broadening of the electronic density of states and the creation of localized tail states.63-65 This phenomenon is well modeled by a random distribution of fixed point charge dopants, that produce a charge density dependent broadening of the electronic density of states. Depending on the material and dopant, this effect can initially lead to a reduction in mobility upon doping, followed by a dramatic increase in mobility at high charge carrier concentrations once traps are filled and the electrostatic influence of the dopants becomes homogeneous.

Importantly, this account must be modified in several critical aspects for mixed conductors. First, in mixed conductors, a stable percolating aqueous phase is established after the first few (de)doping cycles that substantially increases the average dielectric of the material, albeit with potentially large spatial variations on the mesoscopic length scale.32,49,s9,66 The increased screening from the aqueous phase is likely to mitigate the effect of Coulomb trapping between dopant ions and the semiconductor in comparison with organic semiconductors, but has yet to receive detailed characterization. Second, the phase separation between the aqueous and organic phases can also affect the degree of coupling between ions and charges. In PEDOT:PSS, mesoscopic phase separation between the PEDOT-rich and PSS-rich regimes exists, which potentially limits the spatial distribution of dopant ions to the exterior of PEDOT-rich clusters. As discussed above in the context of varying side-chain hydrophilicity and also in polymer design strategies, this phase segregation is potentially tunable,59,66 but detailed studies of its impact have not been performed. Third, in mixed conductors the dopant ion is, by design, mobile and capable of diffusing with complementary charges in the semiconductor (Figure 1a, right). For instance, PEDOT:PSS exhibits an ionic mobility of ${ \bar { \sim } } 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } ,$ which is comparable to the hole mobility of many PEDOT:PSS formulations.8 1 This is a significant modification from the account of doping in semiconductors where the dopant acts as a spatially fixed Coulomb trap. In mixed conductors, these "traps' can migrate with charges, although through pathways that are not necessarily congruent with the charge transport pathways. In the context of organic semiconductors, the recent concerted effort to characterize the energetic distribution of trap sites and the effects of dopants on the electronic density of states (DOs) as a function of charge concentration has clarified the role of ion-electron coupling in the low doping regime and raised new fundamental questions. A similar effort is required for mixed conductors in order to distinguish the interplay of charge trapping and ion-electron coupled transport. Likewise, the account of coupling between ions and charges in organic semiconductors must be supplemented to account for the unique features of mixed conductors.

As is the case in the experimental regime, a central challenge in simulating mixed conduction is the intrinsic coupling between the electronic and ionic transport. At the device scale, drift-diffusion modeling incorporates ionic and electronic coupling through macroscopic charge balance, and this method has been used to characterize OECT output and transfer curves and charge concentration profiles (Figure 4). Although drift-diffusion models are a critical tool for interpreting device behavior, they only incorporate the molecular details of ionelectron coupling phenomenologically, and they do not reveal the underlying charge transporting mechanisms. Many variations have been implemented to account for subtleties of ion-electron coupling. These include the treatment of ion and electron conducting channels as parallel phases,67,68 and single phase systems with varying assumptions on time-scale separation of ion-electron conduction and driving forces of conduction.69-71

To date, few molecular simulations have been performed on mixed conductors. One reason for this is that the intrinsic coupling between ionic and electronic degrees-of-freedom prohibits direct application of modeling methods that have been successful for characterizing organic semiconductors. For instance, master equation approaches, whereby electron transport is modeled as hopping between localized states of variable energy and coupling have been applied extensively to organic semiconductors. This methodology is attractive, because it scales well and the kinetic parameters can be derived in a chemically specific fashion based on a combination of atomistic molecular dynamics configurations and quantum chemistry calculations.72' However, this approach cannot be applied directly to mixed conductors, because electron transport is directly coupled to the motion of ions through electrostatic interactions, and ions can diffuse on a time-scale that is commensurate with charge motion within the polymer. Thus, additional methods development is required to treat these two transport mechanisms self-consistently or potentially in a mean-field approximation as a starting point. The molecular details of ion-coupled electron transfer and their influence on macroscopic device performance presents many outstanding questions that should motivate the development of new experimental and computational methods to study mesoscale transport phenomena. Ultimately, reconciling this molecular picture with device scale drift-diffusion modeling will be central to rationally designing the composition and processing conditions of mixed conducting materials for novel applications.

The field of coupled ion and charge transport in macromolecules is positioned to make rapid strides in the near future for a myriad of reasons. First among these is the significant body of literature that exists regarding decoupled ion and charge transport in macromolecular systems, as these studies provide a solid foothold from which to work. Perhaps less obvious is the fact that the polymer electronics community has begun to recognize the inherent limitations and potential advantages associated with previously studied macromolecules, and the device physics community has quickly honed on applications where these limitations can be minimized and the advantages can be exploited readily. In this way, the story of polymers capable of providing both ion and charge transport will not be one of continued promise but, instead, is likely to be one that allows for fundamental scientific advances in the field to be coupled to tangible outcomes and products that alter the technological landscape in the relatively near future. Thus, continuous and synergistic feedback will occur between the basic science and applied engineering communities such that both ends of the spectrum continue to push the bounds of what is known and what is possible in a rapid manner.

In order to achieve this lofty vision, the strong initial success of mixed polymer conductors should continue on multiple fronts. First, macroscopic descriptions of mixed conduction have outpaced molecular descriptions, leaving a knowledge gap that frustrates materials design. To address this gap, molecular simulation methodologies that respect the coupling of ionic and electronic transport, while also addressing mesoscopic scales, are required. Coarse-grained models of electron transfer could address this capability $\mathrm { g a p } ; { ^ { 7 3 } }$ likewise, recent improvements in the performance and accuracy of semiempirical quantum chemistry and the construction of tractable model Hamiltonians for the electronic degrees of freedom could be leveraged to provide a self-consistent treatment of both conduction mechanisms at relevant length scales.74-76 Second, real-time characterizations of morphology changes during operation and processing are still primitive, leaving little understood about how morphology affects specific mixed conductors, how dopants are distributed, and what aspects of morphology need to be optimized for specific applications. While typical X-ray scattering techniques provide information on some features of these materials (e.g., the average crystal spacings), the distinct pathways and structures (and the requisite number of these pathways and structures) that are relevant to electronic and ionic transport remain unknown at present. In this respect, research on mixed conductors will face similar challenges to organic semiconductors. Third, this field is still young and extremely few materials have been synthesized specifically for mixed conduction. That is, the community is in the phase where design rules are leveraged from adjacent applications, distinct materials are rarely studied by independent groups, and PEDOT:PSS is the primarily used material due to its commercial availability. Because of this dearth of materials diversity, general design rules have yet to develop, even for ascendant applications like OECTs. Thus, it is fair to say that several promising open opportunities exist for researchers with complementary skills sets to move the entirety of the field forward, and it is anticipated that in the near future these computational, theoretical, and experimental studies will occur in full. In this way, we anticipate that the links between fundamental polymer science and functional devices will occur relatively rapidly such that end-users will be able to exploit mixed conducting macromolecules in a number of nextgeneration application spaces in the near future.
