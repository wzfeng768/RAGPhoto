# Ionizing Radiation Detectors Based on Solution-Grown Organic Single Crystals

Beatrice Fraboni,\* Alessandro Fraleoni-Morgera, and Natalia Zaitseva

Organic single crystals (OsCs) have ideal qualities (well defined structure and morphology, lack of grain boundaries, high purity, 3D long range order, good electronic transport properties) for several technological applications, in particular as key components for electronic devices. It is only recently that OSCs have been considered as ionizing radiation detectors, and the latest develop. ments in this field are here reported. In the first section, various methods for OSC growth are described, with emphasis on cost-effective, solution-based approaches capable of delivering large volume, well performing crystals. The second section is focused on the use of solution-grown OsCs as scintillators (i.e., as high energy photon to UV-vis photon conversion), highlighting the ability of cm-scale OsCs to effectively detect neutrons and to carry out neutrons-gamma pulse-shape discrimination tasks. Finally, the third section describes the use of semiconducting, solution-grown OsCs as effective solid state direct detectors (i.e., directly converting high energy photons into charge carriers), evidencing extremely promising performances in terms of operability in environmental conditions (i.e., no need for encapsulation), radiation hardness, linear response and low operating voltage.

# 1. Introduction

The detection of ionizing radiation, either as high energy photons (X- and gamma-rays) or particles (neutrons or electrons) is a technological and socially relevant research feld. It covers a large number of activities, spanning from microelectronics to industrial quality control, from sanitization and sterilization to the oil industry, from nuclear waste monitoring to medicine, and to mining.11,2] Ionizing radiation can be detected with two different categories of functional materials-i.e., scintillators and semiconductors-via an indirect or direct detection mechanism, respectively. In the indirect detection, the incoming ionizing radiation is transduced into an electrical output signal in a two-steps process: the first step is performed by a first sensor (e.g., a scintillator that transforms the incoming radiation into a visible photon), and the second step is performed by a second sensor (e.g., a photodiode) that converts the signal from the first sensor into an electrical one (Figure 1a). In the direct detection, the incoming ionizing radiation is directly transduced into an electrical signal by applying an electric field to a semiconductor exposed to an ionizing radiation field: the directly generated charge carriers are hence separated and collected, giving rise to an electrical output (Figure 1b).

In direct radiation detectors, the up-todate best performing materials are based on inorganic semiconductors (e.g., Si,

CdTe, and CdZnTe), thanks to their favorable charge transport properties (high carrier mobility and low intrinsic carrier density), essential to achieve a low background current and a high sensitivity.[3,4]

On the other hand, scintillator detectors employ transparent fuorescent materials that don't have to be semiconducting, since they just have to produce flashes of light at the passage of an ionizing particle. Efficient inorganic scintillators, such as crystals of doped metal halides (e.g., NaI:Tl, CsI:Tl, LaBr3:Ce, and SrI:Eu) are widely used for detection of gamma radiation. The heavy-Z elements in the composition of inorganic scintillators provide high stopping power for electrons and high-energy photons, but not for neutrons that easily propagate through these materials. The more efficient interaction of neutrons with light nuclei, such as hydrogen, makes the use of hydrogen-rich organic crystals in scintillator detectors for high-energy neutrons particularly favorable.[3,5]

These two different detection mechanisms require specific material functionalities, such as a high absorption efficiency of the incident radiation (high stopping power) and limited exciton trapping and scattering effects (high material purity and uniformity). These properties should ideally be coupled to the possibility to grow the material into a large size to increase the radiation-material interaction volume.

However, inorganic materials satisfying these requirements (high purity and uniformity) are expensive when realized in sizes useful for practical applications. Therefore, novel

In this frame, organic small molecules and polymers are interesting candidates, thanks to their already demonstrated potential in flexible optoelectronic devices.[6,7] Organic materials can undergo several cost-effective processing technologies, as for example printing from solution at room temperature.[8]

In particular, organic semiconducting thin films can be considered an alternative to inorganic compounds for photon detection in the UV to the near infrared (NIR) radiation[9-11]. Organic materials were first suggested as indirect radiation detectors for higher energy photons (e.g., X- and gamma-rays) and particles (e.g., neutrons) in the early 1960s, when the primary interest in these materials was on their scintillating properties.12-15] They are presently widely used as scintillators in the indirect detection approach (Figure 1a), in the long-known form of molecules dispersed in either liquids[13] or in plastic matrices,[14,15] or as polycrystalline material.[16,17]

In general, single crystals have been key players in the development of semiconductor science and technology. Whether based on inorganic18,19] or organic[20,21] materials, single crystals present nearly perfect translational symmetry and exceptionally high chemical purity, coupled to superior charge transport properties[22-24] and the devices that rely on single crystals usually show the highest performance. In this view, the interest in organic single crystals (OsCs) as ionizing radiation detectors has been recently boosted by the development of the solutiongrowth processes, that allowed growing large and very good quality crystals, well apt for the detection of ionizing radiation both in the indirect and direct approach. With respect to their use as indirect detectors, efficient scintillators based on OSCs have been recently reported by Zaitseva et al.,[23,25] while the direct detection of ionizing radiation using OsCs has been reported for the first time in 2012 by Fraboni et al.[26] These findings, coupled with the very favorable processing possibilities typical of organic materials, suggest that OsCs should be considered as key components of technologically advanced and economically advantageous devices.

This review is hence focused on the most recent advances in the field of organic single crystals developed for ionizing radiation detection. In particular, the methods for OsCs growth, with a focus on solution-based strategies, are described (Section 2), as well as the application of OsCs as scintillators in the indirect detection approach (Section 3) and as solid state direct detectors (Section 4).

Vapor-deposited organic crystals can be grown at relatively low temperature (see Figure 2), and it allows fabrication of OSCs even on temperature-sensitive substrates like plastics[31] or paper.[32] Moreover, patterned substrate functionalization allows realization of very precise crystal arrangements, which can be beneficial for subsequent applications requiring, for example, electrical contacts with the grown crystals.33] Finally, OSCs grown by PVD can also be made so thin to be actually flexible, still effective in working devices.[20,34] However, for electronic applications the costs associated to this technique (heating, cooling, low yields) do not suggest meaningful advantages of PVD-grown OSCs with respect to the use of functionally analogue, but much more electronically performing, inorganic crystalline semiconductors. Moreover, the so-obtained crystals are usually rather small in size, rarely exceeding a few mm in length, and more often in the range of hundreds of microns to one mm. These characteristics limit the practical applicability of PVD growth for applications as scintillators for ionizing radiations detection, since in these cases the required sizes for crystals are in the range of several cm. In fact, efficient OsCs for scintillators-based neutron detection must have sizes in excess of $3 \mathrm { \ c m }$ , in order to provide enough probability for neutrons to deposit their energy in the scintillator volume. Among the techniques used previously for providing crystals of this size, growth from the melt was a by far preferred option. Typical approaches used Czochralski135] and Bridgman[36,37] techniques, although some crystals of low-melting-point organic materials have been also grown from supercooled melt.[38]

In the Bridgman technique, probably the most popular one for production of OsCs from the melt, the initial crystalline powder is placed into a quartz ampoule sealed under vacuum or inert gas. The ampoule is placed into a furnace and slowly moved through it until reaching a colder zone in which the melt starts to crystallize from the cold end of the ampoule (Figure 3a,b). The Bridgman technique, well-developed and successfully used for the production of large inorganic crystals of high symmetry (e.g., cubic NaI), is much less suited for the growth of aromatic crystals known to belong to the lowest symmetry groups (monoclinic, triclinic), due to the presence of aromatic rings and to the spatial asymmetry of their molecules. As a result of the high anisotropy of the physical properties, low symmetry crystals confined by the walls of crucibles frequently develop grain boundaries, mosaic structures and cracks, espe cially pronounced at increasing sizes.

The Czochralski method is based on the immersion of a crucible containing the organic material (usually in the form of a powder) in a furnace, so to melt all the material. A small crystal seed is then placed into the melt from above (see Figure 3c) and slowly pulled out, under a slight but steady rotation. In this way the crystal solidifies around the seed, conserving the seed's crystal orientation. Since the crucible used in this technique has an open end (needed to introduce the seed from which the crystal is grown), this technique is applicable only to materials that do not sublimate too easily, like for example benzophenone[35,40] and benzil (1,2-diphenylethane-1,2-dione).[41] Moreover, and more in general, growth from the melt of OSCs is often precluded by thermally induced decomposition or deg radation of the material. A more viable approach to large OSCs fabrication is represented, for soluble materials, by solution growth, which can be carried out in several ways. The most used ones are: solvent evaporation, in which a solution of the desired compound is allowed to slowly evaporate in a vessel until crystals are formed at its bottom (Figure 4a); temperature reduction, in which a solution is gradually cooled until occurring supersaturation generates crystals, recovered from the bottom of the vessel (Figure 4b); antisolvent vapor diffusion, in which the solution including the compound to be crystallized is placed in an open vessel, into which vapors of an appropriate antisolvent for the considered compound are allowed to diffuse (Figure 4c); and antisolvent liquid diffusion, in which an appropriate antisolvent is slowly and carefully added to the desired solution, causing progressive lowering of the solubility of the compound to be crystallized, hence its crystallization (Figure 4d). Each of these methods can be carried out also in presence of crystal seeds, to accelerate and/or improve the crystal growth process.

To obtain larger OsCs (sizes in the tens of cm), a carefully refined version of the temperature reduction solution growth method, developed previously for production of large size inorganic crystals,[42] and then modified for growth of aromatic crystals from organic solvents,[23,43] has to be applied. In this technique, a suitable crystallizer is placed in an external thermostated water bath (Figure 5). A solution of an organic compound in an appropriate solvent (typically toluene, anisole or different alcohols) is hence prepared, prior to the growth run, with a certain saturation temperature (around $5 0 { - } 6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , then filtered, overheated, and transferred to the crystallizer. To allow a uniform fow of material to the growing facets of the crystals, the crystallization equipment can include means for circulating the solution, like for example a rotating platform moving alternatively in opposite directions with fixed periods (see Figure 3c for an example of such an equipment). This method allowed for the first reported production of several cm-large, high optical quality OsCs out of 1,3,5-triphenylbenzene,[23] 1,1,4,4-tetraphenyl-1,3-butadiene, 9,10-diphenylanthracene or diphenylacetylene,[43] opening new opportunities for both further development of solution growth and potential studies of a large number of new organic materials.

While large OsCs are needed for scintillating purposes, smaller crystals (a few mm in size) can be effectively used in direct detection approaches.20] In this frame, it is interesting to review the evolution of solution-based growth methods to obtain OSCs of such size with demonstrated good electronic transport capabilities. First attempts of growing semiconducting OSCs from solution were based on slow solvent evaporation, and have been carried out from soluble precursors; i.e., molecules having a core semiconducting group and additional functional groups designed only to make the final material soluble. These groups can be removed via heat or light after compound deposition, in order to improve the overall electronic performances of the resulting material, but this process usually impedes the formation of actual single crystals, delivering instead polycrys talline films.44] This approach was soon abandoned for natively soluble semiconducting materials able to directly form crystals upon deposition. One of the first successful examples in this sense was the growth from solution, again via slow solvent evaporation, of TIPS-pentacene, a pentacene derivative characterized by two alkylsylilic chains that impart the molecule a notable solubility without negatively impacting on its electronic performances.45] However, also in this case the obtained material was not composed by single crystals, but rather by several merged microcrystals, forming large, aligned domains with grain sizes in the range of $1 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ [46] Bao et al. devised in 2008 an approach based on the use of two solvents (actually, one solvent and one anti-solvent for the considered molecules), so to selectively grow microcrystals (with sizes in the range of $1 0 { - } 1 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } } _ { \ r }$ from solutions onto electrodes patterned onto $\mathrm { S i } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { x }$ chips.[47] The antisolvent liquid diffusion strategy for growing semiconducting OsCs directly onto electrodes was followed later on also by Minemawari et al., who in 2011 showed that is possible to develop matrices of mm-sized crystals by inkjet printing a combination of a solution containing the molecule to be crystallized (C8-BTBT, a benzothiophene derivative) and an antisolvent able to promote its crystallization,[48] though up to now that work does not appear to have been replicated yet. In another case, Anthony et al. used a combination of two co-solvents, combined with a surface treatment based on octadecylsilane, to obtain about $1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ -sized single crystals of TIPS-pentacene.!49] To grow larger crystals, in the range of mm sizes, the slow solvent evaporation method was proven to be effective enough to deliver in 2008 the first example of macroscopic semiconducting OsCs, based on 4-hydroxycyanobenzene (4HCB).[50] These crystals can be easily and quickly grown from widely available solvents like ethylic ether, and their size is tunable simply changing the growth conditions. For example, moving from 2 to $3 ~ \mathrm { { m g } ~ \mathrm { { m L ^ { - 1 } } } }$ in concentration allows to increase the crystal size from about 2 to about $6 \mathrm { m m }$ , while lowering the temperature from 25 to $6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ results in more than doubling the crystals size.[51] This facility to tune the crystal size allowed to design crystals with geometrical features suitable for probing the 3D charge transport anisotropy,[52] and later on to lead to the first documented occurrence of direct detection of X-rays by an OsC.[20]

# 3. Organic Single Crystals as Scintillators for Indirect lonizing Radiation Detection

Efficient neutron detectors are required in many areas of nonproliferation and monitoring of special nuclear materials, such as plutonium or highly enriched uranium, which emit highenergy (fast) neutrons. Detection of these neutrons by conventional technologies based on $^ 3 \mathrm { H e }$ involves an intermediate step of moderation that slows down the initial neutrons to thermal energy, leading to a partial loss of information about the origin and kinetic energy of the emitted neutrons. Therefore, the detection of fast neutrons is commonly performed by liquid scintillators, which contain fluorescent dyes dissolved in aromatic solvents. However, $^ 3 \mathrm { H e }$ supplies can experience shortage problems, and liquid scintillators have issues related to the difficulty of handling and safety concerns, as well as costs associated to the need for an effective sealing of the liquid to prevent leakages, oxidation and aggregation of the organic materials within the liquid. Therefore, organic crystals can be considered as promising alternatives for fast neutron detection, especially if enhanced scintillation performance can be obtained due to the specific properties of organic materials, linked to their high hydrogen content. Among the requirements important for neutron detectors are high efficiency, ability to determine neutron energy, fast response, and low cost when large-area detectors are needed. Neutrons do not have electrical charge, which determines the mechanisms of interactions for electrons and other charged particles.[3,5] Instead, neutrons interact with nuclei of the absorbing material atoms. The interaction can occur via a neutron-induced nuclear reaction or by elastic collision of neutrons on light nuclei, such as hydrogen. The probability of neutron interaction depends on the neutron energy and composition of the detecting material. Low-energy (thermal) neutrons, that at room temperature have energy of about $0 . 0 2 5 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ can be detected through the radiative capture reactions with materials containing nuclei like $^ 6 \mathrm { L i }$ or $^ { 1 0 } \mathrm { { B } }$ , but the probability of such reactions drops off rapidly with increasing neutron energy. For high energy (fast) neutrons, that can transfer more energy in collisions with hydrogen atoms, the process of elastic scattering becomes more likely. In this case organic aromatic scintillators with high hydrogen content and fuorescent properties are the most suitable materials for detecting fast neutrons, delivering fluorescence (scintillation) pulses that can be detected by photo multiplying tubes (PMT) and converted into an electrical signal.

Since neutrons emitted by fission materials are always accompanied by strong gamma radiation, the ability of detector material to discriminate between neutron events and gammarays is one of the most important features required to high performance devices. Neutron/gamma discrimination properties were found in some hydrocarbon-based aromatic materials many decades ago.53,54] The mechanism of the discrimination process relates to the fact that most aromatic crystals exhibit two-decay fluorescence, in which, in addition to the main component decaying exponentially (prompt fluorescence), there is a slower component which decays non-exponentially over the longer time (delayed emission).5] This is due to the fact that the interaction of aromatic matter with the incident radiation results in formation of the singlet and triplet states. The fast component results from the direct de-excitation of individual molecules in excited singlet states $( \mathsf { S } _ { 1 } )$ , while the slow emission is produced by interaction of two molecules in the excited $\pi$ triplet states $( \mathrm { T } _ { 1 } )$ . The process is illustrated by Figure 6a. Excited triplets can migrate in organic matter until two of them collide and annihilate with the formation of one singlet excited state: $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } + \mathrm { T } _ { 1 }  \mathrm { S } _ { 0 } + \mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ The decay of this delayed light depends on the rate of $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } { - } \mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ collisions. The heavier, slow moving protons produce a more localized excitation with higher concentration of excited triplets compared to gamma interactions, which result in the formation of triplets separated by a larger distance. This leads to the higher intensity of the delayed emission in neutron-induced pulses compared to the pulses produced by gamma excitations (Figure 6b). The difference in the shapes of the scintillation pulses induced by different type of particles gives rise to the pulse shape discrimination (PSD)[55] techniques used for the separation of neutron events from the accompanying gamma-radiation background (Figure 6c,d).

From a general point of view, a high concentration of hydrogen and the presence of phenyl rings for efficient fluorescence are sufficient conditions for PSD in organic scintillators. However, the relevance of other specific properties of organic materials ( such as physical state, perfection of crystallographic structures, or presence of impurities) for the final scintillator performances has been little considered until the recent time, likely due to the limited number of organic materials historically investigated for their PSD properties. Studies conducted with larger number and variations of organic crystals[23,25,56] showed that a certain degree of PSD properties may be typical for many aromatic crystals. High degree of neutron/gamma discriminations have been obtained with a number of different poly-phenyl compounds.57] while many of these crystals are still subject to intensive studies related to scintillation and PSD phenomena, additional time is required to determine their potential applications in real detection systems.

Faster development of newly discovered materials may be impeded by different factors, such as high cost or low purity of available materials, as well as problems related to the crystal growth. Availability of large crystals produced by the solution growth technique makes it possible to consider these factors in relation to their properties and scintillation performance as was done with 9,10-diphenylanthracene (DPA) or 1,1,4,4-tetraphenyl-1,3-butadiene (TPB), already known as efficient scintillation dyes used in liquid and plastic scintillators, but never investigated as large single crystals until recently (Figure 7).[23,43] For example, as a result of insufficient purity, crystals that exhibit excellent PSD at small scale may lose it at larger sizes, as is found in DPA (Figure 7a). In this case, the deterioration of PSD in large-scale DPA crystals[56] occurs due to the increasing selfabsorption caused by the yellow color, most likely introduced by the presence of impurities hardly separable from the basic compound. Another possible problem in producing large single crystals is due to the formation of specific defects like needles, different and misaligned domains, or twins, as is clearly seen in a TPB crystal shown in Figure 7b.

The difficulties related to purification and growth problems have been overcome for crystals of trans-stilbene, known for many decades as one of the best organic scintillators. Transstibene (diphenylethylene, briefly stilbene) is a molecular organic crystal constituted by aromatic hydrocarbon molecules, $\mathrm { C _ { 1 4 } H _ { 1 2 } }$ . Stilbene single crystals were used in initial studies that led to development of the PSD technique.58,59] Further works reporting on crystals incorporated into radiation detectors[53,60-62] have proven that stilbene crystals are better suited for fast neutron spectroscopy and neutron-gamma discrimination than any other organic scintillators. However, in spite of more than a six-decade history of development, stilbene single crystals, traditionally grown by melt (Bridgman) techniques, have been rarely produced at sizes exceeding $6 { - } 8 ~ \mathrm { c m }$ [63] A recent publica$\mathrm { t i o n } ^ { [ 6 4 ] }$ described the successful growth of $1 0 \ \mathrm { c m }$ scale stilbene crystals of high optical quality from organic solutions, in which anisole was used as a solvent. Investigation of the scintillation properties of these crystals[65] showed that they exhibit $2 0 { - } 5 0 \%$ higher light output (LO) and better PSD in comparison with the same size crystals grown by Bridgman techniques (Figure 8). This difference, especially pronounced at larger sizes, is attributed to the higher structural perfection of crystals grown by solution technique. It is known that melt-grown stilbene crystals are characterized by a mosaic structure, with varying degrees of block disorder,[66] common for low-symmetry monoclinic crystals grown by the Bridgman techniques. The mosaic structure of the melt-grown stilbene can be clearly seen by the naked eye because of the light scatter from the mis-oriented blocks and grain boundaries. Such blocks are not typical for solution growth that proceeds mainly by dislocation mechanisms, forming more homogeneous single crystal structures (Figure 9). Considerable scatter by the grain boundaries leads to longer paths of the scintillation light that loses a fraction of its intensity because of the resulting additional self-absorption. Due to the absence of similar grain boundaries, the effects of scatter and light loss are less pronounced in solution-grown crystals, explaining the higher LO. Moreover, lattice defects in crystals work as absorption or exciton trapping sites, leading to the reduction of the excitation life times. Also the presence of grain boundaries acting as traps could lead to a partial loss of excitation that is more pronounced for the long-living triplet states than for the shorter lived excited singlets. In organic scintillators, the initial singlet excitations produce the majority of the light output, while the number and kinetics of the excited triplet states control the amount of the delayed light in the scintillation pulses. There. fore, the increasing proportion of the triplet excitations lost on the structural defects leads to a larger decrease of the delayed light fraction, explaining the more pronounced deterioration of PSD in melt-grown crystals with respect to higher perfection stilbene crystals grown from solution.

Comparison of melt- and solution-grown stilbene that revealed the effects of structural imperfection was made with crystals grown from high-purity material, since much greater effects can be introduced by chemical impurities. For example, studies conducted with a model system of mixed dipheylacetylene (DPAC)-stilbene crystals[67] showed that very small addition of impurities may lead to almost complete suppression of the delayed light needed for PSD (Figure 10). A condition for such suppression is determined by the lower bandgap of the impurity in comparison to the bandgap of the host matrix. It has been well-established that impurity molecules having lower energies of the excited states may act as traps for both singlet and triplet excitations of the host molecules. In the mixed DPAC-stilbene crystals, stilbene added as an impurity has a smaller triplet excitation energy (2.4 eV) than DPAC (2.7 eV). This leads to an efficient energy transfer from DPAC to stilbene molecules. As a result, at low concentration, when stilbene molecules are separated by large distances, they act as traps holding the triplet excitations that cannot annihilate to produce delayed light ("no PSD" region of Figure 10). A larger stilbene concentration leads to a higher trap density and increased probability of stilbene network formation. When the distance between stilbene molecules becomes small enough for the efficient $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } { - } \mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ interaction, a sharply increased probability of the triplet annihilation results in a sudden rise of PSD $( \approx 2 0 \%$ region in Figure 10).

These discovered phenomena led to development of new materials with a controlled fraction of delayed light. First plastic scintillators with efficient PSD were produced by incorporation of high loads of fluorescent dyes into aromatic polymer matrices.[68] Sufficiently small distances between the dye molecules dissolved in the aromatic polymer matrix at high concentrations (typically ${ > } 2 0$ wt $\%$ lead to efficient $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } { - } \mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ interactions, enhancing the delayed light fractions. On the other hand, organic crystals with the suppressed delayed light have been shown to be useful for the applications as low afterglow scintillators. Such scintillators, having a low fraction of the delayed light in their scintillation pulses, can be used for ion and neutron spectrometry at high intensity pulsed laser facilities, where separation of temporally close signals is required in the cases when the delayed signals are significantly smaller than the initial ones. One such high profile application includes a neutron diagnostic instrument designed to measure fusion neutrons produced from deuterium-tritium target implosions in the National Ignition Facility (NIF), the world largest laser operating at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.[69,70] The quantity of interest in the NIF experiments is the ratio between the neutrons with the energy of $1 4 \ \mathrm { M e V }$ produced in the fusion reaction $\mathrm { D + T  4 H e + n ^ { 0 } }$ and the $1 0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 2 \ \mathrm { M e V }$ neutrons scattered in the compressed target. The separation of the neutrons with different energy is performed by the time-of-flight method, which uses different velocities of neutrons along a flight path. The problem in determining the ratio is that for a $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m }$ flight path $1 4 \ \mathrm { M e V }$ and $1 2 \ \mathrm { M e V }$ neutrons are separated by just 30 ns and the expected intensity of the $1 0 { - } 1 2 ~ \mathrm { M e V }$ neutrons produced as a result of scatter is a thousand times lower than that of the $1 4 ~ \mathrm { M e V }$ neutrons. This condition requires fast organic scintillators with a very low fraction of the delayed light not exceeding $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ . An initial solution to the problem was the use of liquid scintillators containing dissolved molecular oxygen which suppresses the delayed light via the efficient quenching of the excited triplet states.[71] The disadvantage of this method was that, even if the desired short decays were reached in the liquid scintillators, the presence of molecular oxygen led to the simultaneous suppression of both the delayed and the prompt component of fluorescence, resulting in the total decrease of the scintillation efficiency, essential for the high-resolution neutron spectrometry. Absence of PSD in the mixed DPAC-stilbene crystals is also caused by suppression of the delayed light that leads to a sharp decrease of the scintillation decay times. Similar short decays were detected in other doped crystals, such as, for example, bibenzyl (BB), that showed faster scintillation decays than any available oxygen-quenched liquid or scintillators or plastics (Figure 11). No decrease in the scintillation LO has been observed with these new crystals. On the contrary, addition of stilbene that has much higher scintillation quantum yield compared to the less efficient DPAC or BB led to almost an order of magnitude increase in the final scintillation LO.[67]

As shown by the above described results, in all cases of the LO or PSD comparison, single crystals exhibit a superior performance with respect to traditional liquid scintillators. There are, however, more specific differences that can be considered when comparing the application of single crystal as scintillators. Studies made with a number of melt-grown crystals that included anthracene and stilbenel73,74] have shown that the scintillation responses of crystals to heavy charged particles, such as recoil protons, can be anisotropic passing through maxima and minima along directions parallel to the crystallographic axes.5] The biggest directional dependence (up to $3 3 \%$ in LO for $1 \mathrm { M e V }$ protons) was reported for the anthracene crystal[73] that also has the largest pulse shape anisotropy of any known organic crystal. Trans-stilbene crystals show much less anisotropy for both LO and PSD, but quantitative results reported in early literature are less certain, because of the polycrystal structure of the measured crystals.[72] The magnitude of the directional effects may limit the energy resolution of anisotropic scintillators, unless adequate angle dependent correction functions are determined and applied.74] On the other hand, the anisotropic response of crystalline organic scintillators can find useful applications in the field of directional sensing and neutron imaging, as explored in the previous works and now continued with the new, more perfect organic crystals produced by solution technique.175] Characterization of the anisotropic properties of these materials will help achieving a deeper understanding of the physical mechanisms behind the directional dependence of the detection of ionizing radiation via scintillation effects.

# 4. Organic Semiconducting Single Crystals as Direct lonizing Radiation Detectors

Solid-state semiconductor detectors can directly detect high energy radiation (e.g., X-rays, gamma-rays, and other uncharged and charged particles), i.e. they can convert the incoming ionizing photon into an electrical signal (see Figure 1). The enormous advances in inorganic semiconductors science and technology and the strong market request of highly performing, low-cost solid-state radiation detectors dramatically improved their performance in the last 20 years. This allowed achievement of extremely good energy resolution (below $0 . 2 \% )$ with detectors based on inorganic semiconductors, such as Si, Ge and CdZnTe.

However, high-quality crystals are difficult to grow in largesize at low costs. This limits their use to very high-tech and specialized and restricted applications, e.g., in satellites and as pioneering medical diagnostic tools. Moreover, some of these materials are poorly available and can be toxic. These issues prompted the need to find alternative and novel semiconducting materials, possessing the main requirements for a good solid-state semiconductor detector: i) high resistivity $\left( > 1 0 ^ { 9 } \Omega \mathrm { c m } \right)$ and low leakage current to grant low noise operation; ii) small enough bandgap and electron-hole ionization energy $( < 5 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ to create a large number of charge carriers and increase the signal to noise ratio; iii) large radiation-semiconductor interaction volume; iv) high-purity, homogeneous, defect-free material, to ensure good charge transport properties; v) good carrier mobility-lifetime product $( \mu \tau )$ , to grant a good charge transport and collection within the material.

Organic semiconductor science and technology have recently experienced an impressive progress, as witnessed by the realization of large area and flexible optoelectronic devicesl?l by cost effective printing methods.] These advancements prompted the exploration of their application as solid-state direct detectors.

Most of the few reported examples refer to detectors based on organic semiconducting polymers[76-78] and small molecules thin films.[79] Conjugated polymers allow to easily cover large areas and can be nanostructured via wet-processing, thus increasing the detector active surface at a much lower cost.[80] However, their low carrier mobility and poor radiation tolerance represents a practical problem for the realization of effective direct detectors. Most of the above cited detectors, in fact, work by measuring the resistivity (conductivity) of the polymeric semiconductor, which decreases (increases) upon device exposure to the ionizing radiation, due to material degradation. Thus, such devices are not able to operate for prolonged times, nor they can be reliably repeatedly used, hence mostly per forming as disposable devices. This is exemplified by the first studies over these materials, that were performed on polyacetylene and polythiophene films exposed to neutron or alpha par ticle irradiation showing an increase in the film conductivity, proportional to the irradiation dose but irreversible.[76,81,82]

Because of the low atomic number $( Z )$ of their constituent atoms and of the small interaction volume, detectors based on organic thin films generally exhibit a limited detection sensitivity, due to their overall low attenuation efficiency. A significant improvement came recently, when diodes based on semiconducting polymer films with a thickness of approximately $5 { - } 2 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ (hence a rather thick film, to maximize radiation attenuation) showed a linear response to increasing X-ray irradiation up to dose rates of $6 0 \mathrm { m G y \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ [77-79,85]

Other attempts to overcome the low-Z limitation included the use of $\mathrm { B i l } _ { 3 }$ -nylon-11 high-Z composite thick films[83] to enhance the stopping power of the material with respect to X-rays, hence the overall performance of the detector. With the same purpose, high-Z nanoparticles (metallic tantalum[77] and insulating bismuth oxide[85]) have been added to semiconducting polymers.

Another reported approach consisted in improving the transport properties of the organic film via the inclusion of highmobility small molecules (TIPS-pentacene) in a blend with the polymer, leading to enhanced performances of the resulting detector.[78,79] These devices reached sensitivity values of $1 0 0 -$ $4 0 0 \mathrm { n C } \mathrm { m G y } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ , which are comparable to silicon detectors.

In addition to these attempts to develop organic-based direct detectors, a recent work[26] showed that solution-grown organic semiconducting single crystals (OssCs) are promising candidates to detect X-ray radiation, as they possess peculiar structural and electronic properties particularly apt to overcome most of the up to now discussed limitations inherent to organic materials. The long-range molecular packing order and lack of grain boundaries peculiar to organic single crystals impart them unique transport properties such as top carrier mobility among organic semiconductors (up to $2 0 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 [ 8 6 ] } )$ , transport anisotropy and long exciton diffusion length (up to $8 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } } ^ { \left[ 8 7 \right] }$ as opposed to the few nanometers of organic thin films and blends used in photovoltaic and photodetectors devices.10,11] Solution-grown OSsCs couple the possibility to tune the crystals dimensions up to several $\mathrm { m m } ^ { 3 }$ as described in more detail in the previous section, to a high resistivity and low dark currents, allowing easy fabrication of effective OssCs-based direct detectors. A meaningful example of the application potential of OssCs in this sense in given in Figure 12, where the X-ray induced photocurrent response of a 1,5-dinitronaphthalene (DNN, Figure 12a) single crystal is reported in Figure 12c as a current vs. time curve measured at room temperature and in ambient atmosphere when the crystals is exposed to an on/off switching X-ray beam from a Mo tube $( 3 5 \mathrm { \ k e V , \ 1 1 7 \ m G \ \ s ^ { - 1 } } )$ with two Ag electrodes $4 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ apart (Figure 12b).[88] The single crystal electrical photo-response in terms of $\Delta I = I _ { \mathrm { o n } } - I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ is reported in Figure 12d and demonstrates that it varies linearly with increasing radiation dose rate, assessing that it is possible to effectively use OssCs as reliable ionizing radiation dosimeters. The detector sensitivity, defined as $S = \Delta I / D$ , where D is the radiation dose rate, was evaluated to be about $6 ~ \mathrm { { n C } ~ \mathrm { { m G y } ^ { - 1 } } }$ at $1 0 \ \mathrm { V } .$ It is noteworthy that the sharp electrical response to ionizing radiation observable in Figure 12c was obtained with applied voltages as low as $2 \textrm { V }$ and that the low "off" current (below ${ 5 0 0 } \mathrm { p A }$ very good for radiation detectors) does not show any significant hysteresis or degradation effects after repeated exposures to X-rays.

The mobility of the polymeric films varies in the range $1 0 ^ { - 6 } – 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ i.e., 1-3 orders of magnitude lower than the mobility of OssCs tested as radiation detectors. Moreover, organic single crystals are characterized by the absence of grain boundaries and thus by a much lower density of traps, which is reflected in a higher carrier lifetime $\tau$ than in organic thin films, despite the fact that all the reported X-rays detectors based on OSsCs have been fabricated and operated en plein air. In other words, the above exposed considerations strongly suggest that in the case of OssCsbased detectors the intrinsic mobility-lifetime product $( \mu \tau )$ , a fundamental parameter that controls the charge collection efficiency of radiation detectors, could be more sensitive to $\tau$ than to $\mu$ when the carrier mobility is large enough to grant efficient charge collection.[90]

Another interesting observation relates to different timescale response to ionizing radiation of Rubrene-based devices. These observations hint at a correlation between the slow response of Rubrene-based detectors and the trap states possibly induced in Rubrene single crystals by the exposure to oxygen and visible light.[95,96]

The effects of varying the device geometry and electrodes configuration on the X-ray electrical response of OssCs detectors, by using both conventional X-rays sources and monochromatic synchrotron X-ray beams, working in air and at room temperature, have been also investigated.91] Device geometries that maximize the electrode/semiconductor interface active area (e.g., interdigitated electrodes with finger spacing of few tens of microns, Figure 15a) allow to significantly increase the X-ray electrical photo-response of OssCs and their charge collection efficiency up to $20 \%$ , i.e., an order of magnitude higher than the standard planar contact geometry.[26,91] By combining these findings with the tunable large dimensions of solutiongrown organic single crystals (see Section 2), the achieved net macroscopic effect is a strong increase of the output electrical signal (up to few microamperes) and a notable reduction of the operating bias voltage, down to a few volts (Figure 15b). # 5. Conclusions

Organic materials have been used in ionizing radiations detection since several decades, but only as scintillators in indirect detectors. However, thanks to the significant recent improvements in organic single crystals growth from solution processing, the ideal qualities of organic materials have been coupled to those of large volume single crystals (well defined structure and morphology, lack of grain boundaries, high purity, 3D long range order), allowing to develop novel and high-performing ionizing radiation detectors. Organic single crystals, in principle, can be taken as model systems for basic studies over the effect of ionizing radiations on organic matter. Moreover, organic single crystals can be grown taking advantage of a number of very low cost processing methods, which make them very attractive from a practical application point of view.

This review hence overviewed the latest developments in the field of organic single crystals for ionizing radiations detection, used both as scintillators (i.e., as high energy photon/particle to UV-vis photon conversion) and as solid state direct detectors (i.e., as high energy photon to charge carrier transducers), with an eye on economically advantageous organic crystal growth methods like solution growth, which has been found to be able to produce very effective materials for both scintillators and direct detectors, allowing tunability in the crystal size from a few mm to several tens of cm.

For indirect detection, OsCs can perform very well as scintillators, especially for the detection of neutrons and for neutronsgamma Pulse-Shape Discrimination tasks, overcoming traditional liquid scintillators in terms of final performances.

In direct detection, very recent findings show that semiconducting OSCs can perform extremely well for X-ray detection in terms of operability in normal ambient conditions (no need for cooling or modified atmosphere), radiation hardness, low voltage operations (down to a few volts), linear response and performance reproducibility, and intense work is ongoing to achieve a better insight into the mechanisms underlying the observed behavior and to extend the range of detectable energies and radiation types.

In conclusion, OsCs possess a number of desirable features for effective ionizing radiation detection: robustness, ability to detect low radiation levels and overall performances already approaching or (in some cases) even surpassing those of inorganics-based detectors. These characteristics, coupled with easy and low-cost fabrication routes, hold great promises for a bright future for these materials in such a technologically and socially relevant field.
