# Fabrication of Gradient and Patterned Organic Thin Films by Bipolar Electrolytic Micelle Disruption Using Redox-Active Surfactants

Yaqian Zhou, Naoki Shida, Ikuyoshi Tomita, and Shinsuke Inagi\*

Abstract: Bipolar electrochemistry could be regarded as a powerful approach for selective surface modification due to the beneficial feature that a wirelessly controllable potential distribution on bipolar electrodes (BPEs). Herein we report a bipolar electrolytic micelle disruption (BEMD) system for the preparation of shaped organic films. A $U$ shaped bipolar electrolytic system with a sigmoidal potential gradient on the BPE gave gradient-thin films including various interesting organic compounds, such as a polymerizable monomer, an organic pigment and aggregation induced emission (AIE) molecules. The gradient feature was characterized by UV-Vis absorption, thickness measurements and surface morphology analysis. Corresponding patterned films were also fabricated using a cylindrical bipolar electrolytic setup that enables siteselective application of the potential on the BPE. Such a facile BEMD approach will open a long-term perspective with respect to organic film preparation.

# Introduction

Bipolar electrochemistry has attracted a renewed interest in the last two decades due to its use within broad fields ranging from materials science[1] to analytical chemistry] and beyond.] The bipolar electrochemical technique involves electrochemical reactions in a wireless manner with respect to the classical electrochemical method.4l A general electrochemical setup includes a pair of driving electrodes connected to a power supply, and a bipolar electrode (BPE) embedded in an electrolytic solution. The driving electrodes involve not only electrochemical reaction on their surfaces, but also generate an electric field in the solution in a low concentration of supporting salts, which induces the BPE. The BPE surface can promote electrochemical reactions, even in the absence of a direct ohmic contact. The potential distribution along the BPE can be regulated by the applied voltage, the shape and the position of the driving electrodes, as well as the electrolytic cell design, considering the principles of bipolar electrochemistry.l In previous work, our group originally designed several electrolytic cells that achieved effective control of the potential distribution on the BPE.[6] One of the most attractive features for bipolar electrolysis is that the BPE can act as an in situ generated controllable template for gradient surface modification. We have achieved electrochemical chlorination across polythiophene films,l gradient electrochemical doping of conducting polymers,8] gradient electrochemical surface modification of a poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-based conducting polymer using an electroclick reactionl and the formation of a 3D gradient polymer brush by electrochemically mediated atom transfer radical polymerization.10 In addition, another electrolytic system with an insulating cylinder has also been investigated, in which the driving anode and cathode are separated by a plastic shielding cylinder, which generated a mountain-like potential distribution on a wireless BPE plate. The cylindrical setup was successfully used to perform bipolar site-selective patterning on a BPE.[1,11] These bipolar electrolytic setups provide a significantly facile and effective method to fabricate gradient and patterned polymeric films. Here, all gradient or patterned films prepared were subjected to electrochemical reaction during the film-formation process. In this context, the preparation of gradient or patterned thin films that maintain a consistent chemical composition after film formation represents a significant challenge that when achieved will extend the application range of the present wireless bipolar electrochemical technique.

The method for the preparation of organic thin films is significant because of its attractive potential applications in various devices such as photovoltaic cells,[12] transistors[13] and sensors.[4] Most of the methods used to prepare films of organic small molecules are dry processes, such as chemical vapor deposition (CvD) and physical vapor deposition (PVD).[15] However, these methods are time-consuming and require expensive equipment. On the other hand, Saji's group has developed a unique and practical wet process known as the electrolytic micelle disruption method, where thin films are deposited by the release of organic compounds during the oxidation of redox-active surfactants containing a ferrocene moiety.[16] This method is based on the breaking up of micelles formed by surfactants with a ferrocenyl group when the surfactants are electrochemically oxidized on an electrode surface. Solubilizate is released from the micelles and then deposited onto the electrode surface to form a film. This film formation technique is simple, cost-effective and can be applied to many compounds. It is not only suitable for organic compounds[16c17] but also for polymers[8] and fullerenes.19] Unlike general electrochemical film formation processes via electrochemical reactions where the starting compounds undergo reactions, this approach gives films of intact organic compounds carried by micelles. Moreover, this technique does not use organic solvents and is thus an environmentally preferred technology.

We demonstrate here with several experiments that the bipolar electrolytic technology can be ideally integrated with the electrolytic micelle disruption process, namely, the bipolar electrolytic micelle disruption (BEMD) approach. Using the BEMD method, the wireless preparation of thickness-gradient and patterned organic thin films consisting of a variety of compounds is possible. In our investigations, $N$ -vinylcarbazole (NVC) was solubilized in micelles composed of a cationic surfactant of (11-ferrocenylundecyl) trimethylammonium bromide (FTMA), while copper phthalocyanine (CuPc) and aggregation-induced emission (AIE) compounds were dispersed in micelles constructed from a nonionic surfactant of $\mathfrak { a }$ -(11-ferrocenylundecyl)--hydroxypoly(oxyethylene) (FPEG). When FTMA micelles solubilized with NVC and FPEG micelles including CuPc or AIE compounds were introduced to the BEMD system, NVC, CuPc and AIE compounds as film-forming materials produced the corresponding films. Cooperated organic compounds present were unfettered when these electroactive surfactants were electrochemically oxidized. The sigmoidal potential distribution in the U-shaped electrolytic cell and the mountain-like potential distribution in the cylindrical electrolytic cell were transcribed into the BPE surface, and the location and number of disrupted micelles were regulated, which afforded deposited films with gradient and patterned characteristics. Furthermore, the gradient film was available for post-functionalization. For example, the gradient NVC film was photopolymerized under UV irradiation. In this contribution, the methodology provides for the preparation of various film gradients and patterns, which is not limited to organic molecules, but could potentially expand the scope to other materials such as polymers and carbon materials.

# Results and Discussion

# Preparation of gradient Nvc films and post-functionalization.

A ferrocene-modified cationic surfactant of FTMA was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure (Figure S1a-c).[16c,20] FTMA as a redox-active surfactant formed a micelle and solubilized the well-studied compound NVC as a film-forming material (Figure 1a). The electrolytic solution was prepared by the addition of an excess amount of NVC to an aqueous micellar solution containing $2 . 0 \mathrm { m M }$ FTMA and $0 . 1 \mathbf { M }$ lithium sulfate $\left( \mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \right)$ . The suspension was sonicated for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ and then stirred continually for 5 days to attain a solubilization equilibrium. Undissolved NVC was removed by centrifugation $( 2 0 0 0 \mathrm { r p m } , 1 \mathrm { h } )$ and the remaining solution was used as the electrolyte. An in-house-built Ushaped electrolytic cell consisting of a pair of platinum (Pt) driving electrodes $( 2 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 2 0 \mathrm { m m }$ ) and an indium tin oxide (ITO) plate as a BPE ( $1 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 4 0 \mathrm { m m } ,$ was used for bipolar electrolysis (Figure S3a). Half of the BPE surface was covered with NITOFLON tape to facilitate the thickness measurement (Figure S5). In this NVC micellar system, oxidation of FTMA occurs on the BPE anode, while reduction of FTMA+ or $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ proceeds on the cathode to keep electrons in balance on the BPE. Considering the FTMA+ species generated at the surface of the driving anode electrophorized to the BPE cathode, the required minimum potential difference $( \Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n 1 } } )$ is ca. $0 . 1 5 { \mathrm { V } }$ [Figure S7a and Eq. (2)]. This electrolytic system was utilized in an aqueous medium; water, as the most abundant molecule, should not be ignored in the reduction part of the BPE where the potential difference is over $1 . 1 6 \mathrm { V }$ [Eq. (3)]. The U-shaped cell had a shielding wall between the driving electrodes, which controlled the intensity of the potential gradient on the BPE and induced a sigmoidal potential distribution on the BPE surface. The IR-drop around the BPE surface was measured using a reference electrode-array. The points near the ITO surface were measured at $5 \mathrm { m m }$ intervals (Figure S3b). COMSOL simulation was employed to gain a theoretical value for the interfacial potential distribution (Figure 1b). The experimental and simulation results showed good agreement with each other. This validation is significant, especially when other bipolar electrolytic systems that are difficult to estimate the potential distribution experimentally are used, such as the cylindrical setup presented later. In that case, simulation results reveal insights into the experimental phenomena. Combined with these results, the maximum potential difference generated on the ITO surface was over $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n 1 } }$ , which indicates that the potential difference generated was sufficient to drive redox reactions on the BPE under the experimental conditions employed. Once the BEMD process initiated, FTMA was oxidized to its cation at the anodic part of the BPE, followed by breaking of the FTMA micellar structure. Simultaneous release of NVC molecules from these micelles occurred in this process. As a result, free NVC was deposited on the local electrode surface. The potential difference at the BPE surface was sigmoidal; therefore, the different degree of FTMA oxidation at each position resulted in a gradient amount of deposited NVC along the BPE surface. Finally, a thickness-gradient NVC film was generated (Figure 1c).

NVC thin films were prepared using the BEMD approach under various conditions. The addition of an inorganic salt to an ionic surfactant generally decreases the critical micelle concentration, and thus influences micelle formation and solubilization.21] Therefore, to achieve fine-tuning of the film thickness, micellar solutions with different concentration of electrolyte salts $( C )$ are employed. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) results revealed that the mean diameter of the resultant micelles was ca. $1 3 \mathrm { n m }$ with a broad size distribution when $C$ was 50 mM $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ , while a narrower size distribution with a similar mean diameter (ca. $1 5 \mathrm { n m }$ ) was obtained with $\mathrm { 1 0 0 \ m M \ L i _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } }$ (Figure S6a). The size distribution width may be affected by the hydrophobic interactions of alkyl chains in a micelle and the electrostatic interactions around the surface of a micelle.22] When these micellar electrolytic solutions were employed for the BEMD system, NVC films were formed on BPE surfaces in all cases. All films exhibited UV-- Vis absorption peaks at ca. $3 3 6 \mathrm { n m }$ and $3 4 9 \mathrm { n m }$ , which were almost identical with the UV absorption peaks of NVC in dichloromethane. In the UV spectrometer, the width of the space window that can pass through film was $3 \mathrm { m m }$ ; therefore, starting from the edge of the anodic pole of the BPE, UV absorption spectra of the obtained film were recorded every $3 \mathrm { m m }$ . When $C$ was $5 0 \mathrm { m M }$ , the white NVC film fabricated at an applied voltage $( E )$ of $6 \mathrm { V }$ for an electrolysis time $( T )$ of $1 . 5 \mathrm { h }$ faded toward the edge of the cathodic pole of the BPE (Figure 2a). The maximum UV-Vis absorption was positioned at the very end of the anodic part. UV-Vis absorption of the contiguous area towards the right decreased, which suggested a gradient of the NVC film. A much thicker NVC film was obtained (Figure 2b) when the micellar solution with a higher $C \left( 1 0 0 \mathrm { m M } \right)$ was electrolyzed. This gradient film had a stronger UV absorption than the film in Figure 2a. Figure 2c shows that a lower $E$ caused the release of a small amount of NVC during the BEMD process, which resulted in a thinner film. In this case, UV characterization at each position gave a relatively weak absorbance, although the tendency to decrease was maintained. Therefore, the thickness of these gradient NVC films was tunable by simply changing the micellar solution and/or electrolytic conditions.

# Patterning of NVC circular films

Towards the goal of patterning organic thin films by the BEMD method in a wireless manner, we employed an electrolytic system with an insulating cylinder, in which the BPE is placed under the cylinder and a circular potential distribution is generated on it.20,21,23] The experimental configuration is shown in Figure 5a. A glass container was used as an electrolytic cell and the cylinder (diameter: $1 \mathrm { m m }$ was kept at $1 \mathrm { m m }$ distance from the ITO surface. The stainless steel driving anode and cathode were set outside and inside the cylinder, respectively. This setup induced a circular anodic surface in the region under the cylinder. Accompanied by micelle disruption after oxidation, NVC is released around the anodic area of the BPE surface, to form a circular film (Figure 5b). In this cylindrical setup, the driving anode was close to the BPE surface, and thus generated $\mathrm { F T M A ^ { + } }$ at the anodic spot of the BPE, which could easily migrate around the cathodic area of the BPE and act as a sacrificial species. Here, the required minimum potential difference of $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n 1 } }$ was kept consistent with that in the Ushaped cell. However, the space in this cylindrical setup is too narrow, which makes it difficult to measure potential differences in the solution. Therefore, the distributions of interfacial potential were numerically simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics, which presented a mountain-like potential distribution where the center of the covered area had the highest potential difference (Figure 5c). When $E$ was 6 V, the maximum potential difference was $0 . 2 \mathrm { V } ,$ which was sufficient to drive BMED $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n 1 } } =$ $0 . 1 5 \mathrm { V } ,$ . Consequently, a round white film with a diameter of $2 \mathrm { m m }$ appeared, which reflected the shape of the cylinder (Figure 5d). With an increase of $E$ $_ { 8 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ and $1 0 \mathrm { V }$ ), the diameter of the circular NVC film formed on the BPE became larger, which can be explained from the numerically simulated results. The general trend was that a higher $E$ induced a larger $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { B P E } }$ on the BPE surface, which resulted in an enlarged diameter of the circular NVC film.

# Preparation of gradient CuPc dye films.

Encouraged by the success of this BEMD method in preparing gradient and patterned NVC thin films, we aimed to broaden the scope using other organic compounds. Copper phthalocyanine (CuPc) as a colored macrocycle is known for its strong Q-band absorption $( 6 0 0 - 8 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ and excellent photoinduced charge generation efficiency, and is applied in optoelectronic devices, mainly as thin films. An organic thickness-gradient heterojunction based on $\mathrm { C u P c / C _ { 6 0 } }$ or CuPc/3,4,9,10-perylenetetracarboxylic bisbenzimidazole (PTCBI) has been prepared using a mechanically controlled shutter with gradual displacement along the longitudinal direction, and exhibited position sensing.[24] However, the preparation process required expensive instruments and rigorous experimental conditions. The approach presented in this study may open a new perspective to produce such sensors. Here, a nonionic surfactant of $\mathfrak { a }$ -(11-ferrocenylundecyl)--hydroxypoly(oxyethylene) (FPEG) containing a ferrocenyl moiety was synthesized (Figure S1a,b,d), which has better ability to disperse large size organic compounds, and improve their solubility in aqueous solution.11c] An aqueous solution containing $2 . 0 \mathrm { m M }$ FPEG, $0 . 1 \mathbf { M }$ $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ and $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ CuPc was sonicated for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ and stirred for 5 days to prepare an electrolytic solution. The supernatant was then separated for BEMD electrolysis. The size distribution of the FPEG micelles incorporating CuPc had a wide range from $1 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ to $4 4 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S6b). This redox system was firstly examined by cyclic voltammetry [Figure S7b and Eq. (4)]. The oxidation of FPEG occurred at the anodic part, while the reduction of $\mathrm { F P E G ^ { + } }$ and/or $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ proceeded on the cathodic part (Figure6a). A value of $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n } 2 }$ $( 0 . 1 2 \mathrm { V } )$ was obtained. According to the measured potential distribution in a Ushaped cell, the voltages applied to drive the electrodes ensure the redox reactions proceeded (Figure S4a). Consequently, the FPEG micelle structures were disrupted at the anodic part of the BPE in a gradient manner, in agreement with the potential distribution. The released CuPc was simultaneously deposited on the BPE surface to generate a gradient blue thin film (Figure 6b).

# Preparation of gradient and circular luminescent films having a variety of colors.

In subsequent studies, the aggregation induced emission (AIE) effect is favored in the solid state and is practically useful in optoelectronic and sensory systems;[25] therefore, the proposed approach was extended to the preparation of AIE thin films (Figure 6a). To our best knowledge, this is the first time that AIE molecules have been introduced into an electrochemically controlled micellar system. (TPE). Next, we explored a promising TPE derivative of tris(4-nitrophenyl)phenylethene (TPE3N), which can easily be structurally tailored by simple reactions from commercial TPE (Figure S2).[27] TPE3N exhibited a yellow emission rather than a blue emission because of electron-withdrawing nature of nitro groups. The height profile of the TPE3N film was lower than $2 . 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ within the $1 0 \mathrm { m m }$ length and then decreased to the end of the film (Figure 8b).

AIE luminogens have a diversity of structures. Another classic luminogen, hexaphenylsilole (HPs), emits sky-blue light.26a] An FPEG micellar solution containing HPS was electrolyzed for $1 . 5 \mathrm { h }$ $\mathbf { \Delta } E { = } 6 \mathbf { \Delta V } ,$ . The $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n } } s$ generated from the oxidation of FPEG and reduction of $\mathrm { F P E G ^ { + } }$ was $0 . 1 2 \mathrm { V }$ [Figure S7e and Eq. (10)]. The practical potential difference generated on the BPE of $1 . 5 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ was sufficient to initiate redox reaction (Figure S4d). The BEMD process resulted in a film composed of HPS with a bright sky-blue emission under UV irradiation. The length of the HPS film was ca. $2 2 \mathrm { m m }$ and the luminescent intensity gradually decreased from the anodic pole to the cathodic pole. The film thickness was then measured to reveal the luminescent intensity change at different positions; it remained at a steady level within $1 4 \mathrm { m m }$ and then decreased from $1 4 \mathrm { m m }$ to the end of film, which corresponded to the gradient luminescence of the aggregated HPS film (Figure 8c).

Finally, we attempted the fabrication of patterned luminescent AIE thin films using the cylindrical BPE electrolytic installation. This configuration induced a circular anodic surface under the cylinder. The oxidation of FPEG occurred at the anodic area, which resulted in stacking of the released AIE molecules as the FPEG micelles disrupted during the BEMD process (Figure 10a). Following the electrolysis under $6 \mathrm { V } .$ the successful formation of AIE thin films was confirmed by visual examination. Under UV irradiation ( $\lambda = 3 6 5 \mathrm { n m }$ ), these circular TPE, TPE3N and HPS films had blue, yellow and skyblue emissions, respectively, corresponding to their starting materials (Figure 10b). With a higher $E$ (8 V), the diameter of the round film was enlarged accordingly. It was clarified by our previous study that with a higher $E$ applied to driving electrodes in this setup, the potential difference within a larger region can satisfy $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { m i n } } .$ which gives a broader anodic area.[11b]

# Conclusion

# Acknowledgements

We successfully established a BEMD approach by the combination of bipolar electrochemistry with the electrochemically controlled micelle disruption process. Various organic compounds can be incorporated into micelles consisting of electroactive ferrocene-modified cationic and nonionic surfactants. When the surfactants were electrochemically oxidized, organic compounds were released onto the BPE surface as a thin film that corresponded to the potential distribution on the electrode surface. These organic compounds as film-forming materials do not undergo electrochemical reactions due to the electroactive surfactants containing a ferrocene moiety, having a low oxidation potential, more likely to be oxidized firstly. The main advantage of the BEMD method is related to the intrinsic features of bipolar electrochemistry, namely, a possible wireless operation in potential distribution regulation. In the case of the U-shaped bipolar electrolytic cell, the sigmoidal potential distribution contributed to the formation of gradient films. Modulation of the voltage applied to the driving electrodes or electrolytic solution allowed control of the film thickness. The cylindrical bipolar electrolytic setup yielded circular films due to the different anodic and cathodic regions. The size of these circular films could be easily adjusted by the supplied voltage from the external power source. In several proof-of-concept experiments, a variety of gradient and circular organic films were successfully obtained. NVC with an olefinic double bond was solubilized in micelles of FTMA cationic surfactant, which gave a gradient NVC film that further underwent solidstate photopolymerization. Patterned NVC films with a variety of sizes were also produced. An organic pigment of CuPc was well dispersed in a nonionic surfactant of FPEG, which produced gradient blue thin films. Three types of AIE molecules (TPE, TPE3N and HPS) were dispersed in FPEG micelles to generate gradient thin films that exhibited strong luminescence under UV irradiation. Bright and colorful circular AIE films were also fabricated. The variety of thin films produced confirmed the validity of the proposed BEMD method.

The straightforward approach can be generalized to a large variety of chemical molecules, as long as they can be solubilized or dispersed by electroactive surfactants. By slightly adapting the configuration of a bipolar electrolytic device and the electrolytic conditions would also enable the customization of gradient or patterned films. This is thus a promising tool for various applications, not only in the field of luminescence, but even for more sophisticated research, such as biosensor systems, due to the organic solvent-free and mild conditions employed, which would allow the design of position-sensitive thin films for sensors. Besides, personalized electrodes having various materials and sizes are possible to be flexibly adopted to deposit gradient or circular films, which could satisfy the practical demand in various situations.
