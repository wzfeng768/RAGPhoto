# Process-Aid Solid Engineering Triggers Delicately Modulation of Y-Series Non-Fullerene Acceptor for Efficient Organic Solar Cells

Xin Song,\* Kai Zhang, Renjun Guo, Kun Sun, Zhongxin Zhou, Shenglei Huang, Linus Huber, Manuel Reus, Jungui Zhou, Matthias Schwartzkopf, Stephan V. Roth, Wenzhu Liu, Yu Liu, Weiguo Zhu,\* and Peter Muller-Buschbaum

Volatile solids with symmetric -backbone are intensively implemented on manipulating the nanomorphology for improving the operability and stability of organic solar cells. However, due to the isotropic stacking, the announced solids with symmetric geometry cannot modify the microscopic phase separation and component distribution collaboratively, which will constrain the promotion of exciton splitting and charge collection efficiency. Inspired by the superiorities of asymmetric configuration, a novel process-aid solid (PAS) engineering is proposed. By coupling with BTP core unit in Y-series molecule, an asymmetric, volatile 1,3-dibromo-5-chlorobenzene solid can induce the anisotropic dipole direction, elevated dipole moment, and interlaminar interaction spontaneously. Due to the synergetic effects on the favorable phase separation and desired component distribution, the PAS-treated devices feature the evident improvement of exciton splitting, charge transport, and collection, accompanied by the suppressed trap-assisted recombination. Consequently, an impressive fill factor of $80 . 2 \%$ with maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) of $1 8 . 5 \%$ in the PAS-treated device is achieved. More strikingly, the PAStreated devices demonstrate a promising thickness-tolerance character, where a record PCE of $1 7 . 0 \%$ is yielded in PAS devices with a $3 0 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ thickness photoac tive layer, which represents the highest PCE for thick-film organic solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Organic solar cells (OsCs) with promising advantages, such as lightweight, flexible processability, and low toxicity, are considered as one of the most promising green-energy technologies.1-4] From the past several years, with the advents of start-of-the-art Y-series non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) (Y6, Y6-BO, BTIC-2Cl${ \mathcal { K } } \mathrm { F } _ { 3 }$ BTP-eC9, L8-BO, and so on) in terms of novel electron-deficient core, dithienothiophen[3.2-b]pyrrolobenzothiadiazole, BTP,[5-10] the recording power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) have exceeded $1 9 \%$ in single-junction configuration and over $20 \%$ certified PCE based on tandem solar cells has been reported recently.[11,12] As the exciton splitting, charge generation, transport, and extraction are highly dependent on the microstructure morphology and mesoscopic aggregation state in bulk heterojunction (BHJ) photoactive layer.13  Correspondingly,  considerable morphology control strategies have been proposed to enhance crystallinity and manipulate phase separation behaviors for the promotion of photovoltaic characteristics. For example, solvent additive engineering is a promising approach to fine-tune nanomorphology. Despite the increment of performance, such a strategy worsen the device reproducibility and long-term operational stability due to the low volatilization property.417) Besides, the ternary blend (introducing a third component into the pristine binary system) is another feasible strategy to manipulate the BHJ morphology. Molecular aggregation and phase separation behaviors would be re-assembled in ternary blend thereupon the further improvement of photovoltaic performance.[18-22]

Incorporation of volatile solids with $\pi$ backbone into the photoactive layer is a new emerging morphology control approach, which can break through the aforementioned bottlenecks.16,23-25] Up-to-now, the exploration of aromatic solid is mainly focused on the symmetric types. Unfortunately, collaborative optimization of phase separation in the lateral direction and component distribution in the vertical direction is complicated for symmetric solid due to its isotropic molecular packing and weak dipole interaction, which would induce the sacrifice of either exciton splitting or charge extraction efficiency, and further the photovoltaic performance. Therefore, it is essential and urgent to explore novel volatile solids with different conformation to synergetically manipulate the phase separation and component distribution, thereby boosting the performance.

Asymmetric geometry is comprehensively furnished in organic and perovskite optoelectronic fields (such as lightemitting diodes, solar cells) as it can provide distinct dipole effects and 3D spatial configurations.[26-31] In this work, a novel process-aid solid (PAs) engineering is proposed by applying asymmetric configuration on volatile solid, where a volatile 1,3-dibromo-5-chlorobenzene (DBCl) solid (shown in Figure 1a) with asymmetric halogen around benzene ring is selected to construct the high-performance organic solar cells. The key superiority of PAS engineering is the elevated dipole moment, unidentical dipole direction and evident interlaminar noncovalent interaction after coupling asymmetric DBCl with BTP core in Y-series small molecule. Therefore, this delicate manipulation of molecule conformation can significantly modify the mesoscopic molecule stacking arrangement, optimize the microstructure blend phase separation, and form gradient donor/acceptor distribution with the assistance of high-volatility of DBCl. The PAS-treated devices demonstrate an improved exciton splitting rate, a prolonged carrier lifetime, and an enhanced carrier extraction process. These systematic improvements suppressed the charge carrier recombination, leading to a champion PCE of $1 8 . 5 \%$ with a remarkable FF of $8 0 . 2 \%$ based on the PM6:L8-BO system (third-party organization certified result: $1 8 . 3 2 \% $ . More strikingly, a PCE of $1 7 . 0 \%$ together with FF of $7 4 . 1 \%$ is obtained when the thickness of the photoactive layer rises to over $3 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ which represents the highest efficiencies for the thick-film OsC so far.

# 2. Results and Discussion

As a proof-of-concept, the classical PM6: Y6 system is applied for the systematic study, where PM6 is the polymer donor, Y6 is on behalf of Y-series small molecules, with their structures illustrated in Figure 1a. As known, benzene is the simplest aromatic hydrocarbon and its derivates are the basic raw materials of the chemical industry. Thus, DBCl is price friendly (a gram is less than $\$ 0.10$ and highly accessible organic hydrocarbon. Notably, this material is irritating to a certain extent (especially to the eyes). However, under regulated operating conditions, it is an environmental-friendly material. Therefore, the application of this material provides a reliable way for the industrial application of organic solar cells in the future (such as large-scale printing or coating process).[32] Besides, as shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the DBCl molecule demonstrates an extremely low extinction coefficient $( 4 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ M ^ { - 1 } ~ c m ^ { - 1 } } )$ with absorption onset at $3 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , suggesting that the neat DBCl has a slight influence on the light-harvesting in the light-to-photon conversion process.

Meanwhile, to investigate whether DBCl would remain in the active layer after thermal annealing, the volatility of DBCl is systemically investigated by thermogravimetry analysis (TGA) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement. As shown in Figure 1b, DBCl shows a clear weight loss starting from ${ \approx } 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ associated with a $5 \%$ weight loss temperature $T _ { \mathrm { d } }$ at ${ \approx } 7 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Moreover, DBCl can be volatilized when the heating temperature increases to $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Notably, during the heating process, DBCl is completely disappeared without any residue in the crucible, indicating that this material is completely sublimated and not decomposed. The residues of DBCl after annealing treatment are further detected by XPS measurement, which can accurately examine atoms in trace amount (full survey shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information). Comparing the elemental compositions in three molecules (PM6, Y6, and DBCl), chlorine and bromine are two unique atoms in the DBCl molecule. Thus, by tracking these two atoms in the PM6:Y6:DBCl blend film after the thermal annealing treatment $( 7 5 ~ \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C } , 5 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , as shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information), there is no signal of Cl or Br in the blend film in the XPS profiles measured from neat PM6:Y6 and PM6:Y6:DBCl annealing film, implying that DBCl can be completely volatilized at this temperature. As illustrated in Figure S4 (Supporting Information), parts of white DBCl aggregates can directly be observed after depositing the DBCl solution on the silicon wafer. To inspect whether DBCl performs multifunctionality on the Y6 molecule, we first calculate the electrostatic potential surfaces (ESP) patterns of DBCl and Y6 based on

B3LYP-D3BJ/ $^ { \prime } 6 { \cdot } 3 1 \mathrm { G } ^ { { \ast } { \ast } }$ by Gaussian 09.D01 software package.[34] Compared to Y6, the DBCl molecule shows a weaker electrostatic potential due to its small benzene ring. Undoubtedly, based on the strong electronegativity of bromine and chlorine atoms around the benzene ring, the ESP pattern of DBCl exhibits a slightly negative distribution in the iso-surface. However, most of the iso-surfaces from the distribution in the Y6 configuration show positive ESP values, especially the BTP core unit, whereas the negative region is located at fluorine atoms and cyano in the end groups. Thus, due to the opposite polarity attraction theory,[35] we can speculate that a strong intermolecular interaction exists between DBCl and BTP core unit.

To verify this hypothesis, different adsorption locations between Y6 and DBCl are conducted to reveal the optimal geometry of the Y6:DBCl dimer. From the assembled structure in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the exoergic energy is only $- 8 . 5 8 \mathrm { \ k c a l \ m o l ^ { - 1 } }$ when DBCl is not close to Y6. Remarkably, the exoergic energy shows a continuous decrease from $- 2 2 . 0 7$ to $- 2 4 . 7 6 \mathrm { \ k c a l \ m o l ^ { - 1 } }$ when DBCl approaches gradually to the BTP core unit. The decreasing exoergic energy demonstrates that DBCl is more favorable to assembly or locate on the BTP core unit. Furthermore, a reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis based on the optimal geometry structure of Y6:DBCl dimer is finely executed to qualitatively study the noncovalent interactions between BTP core and DBCl.36] The green and blue regions of RDG analysis validate the presence of marked noncovalent attractive interaction between DBCl and BTP core unit. As reported by Yip et al.,[37] compared to other NFA counterparts, a vital factor for Y-series NFA is that the BTP core units would cluster between adjacent molecules to mold another unique $\pi - \pi$ stacking arrangement except the terminal group packing $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { J } }$ -aggregation). Thus, a special 3D high-efficiency bipolar transmission network is formed in Y-series molecular configuration. Taken above results together, the introduction of DBCl enhances the noncovalent force of the central core from our RDG simulation, which is beneficial for the tight molecular packing of the Y6 molecule (which will be discussed in the crystallinity part).

Another promising feature of DBCl is the asymmetric halogen atom distribution, which would induce the dipole effect due to the different electron-withdrawing characteristics of chlorine and bromine. Subsequently, as shown in Figure 1g, the dipole moment of the DBCl molecule as calculated by the DFT simulation is 0.19 Debye with a dipole direction from Cl to Br atom. As illustrated in Figure 1g, there exists a dipole moment of 1.01 Debye in the neat Y6 molecule with 3D vector of $x = 0$ $\gamma = 1$ $z = 0$ indicating the dipole direction is parallel to the plane direction of Y6 geometry. This observation is similar to the results reported by Zhu et al.[38] After coupling the DBCl on top of Y6, the diploe moment remarkably increases to 1.93 Debye, with an improvement of $9 1 . 1 \%$ in comparison with that of neat Y6. More specifically, the dipole direction in the Y6:DBCl dimer shows a vector direction: $x = 0 . 1 7$ $\gamma = 0 . 6 7$ $z = - 1 . 8 1$ with an upward sloping orientation in comparison with that of Y6. As reported, the asymmetric property could induce different relative orientations of the adjacent molecules.31] In addition, this exclusively asymmetric alignment can provide a larger compensation for overall structural relaxation than only one orientation arrangement, resulting in more potential to form metastable polymorphs for more compactly molecular packing. Thus, we can speculate that this DBCl introduction process could contribute to improving Y6 intermolecular packing.

The grazing incidence wide X-ray scattering (GIwAxs) is carried out to approve our speculation by evaluating the molecular ordering property. As shown in Figure 2a, the characteristic crystallization peak of Y6 is hard to monitor in both inplane (IP) and out-of-plane (OOP) direction of neat Y6 film, whereas the signal of OOP peak is obvious after the introduction of DBCl (highlighted in Figure 2b), inferring that the preferred face-on $\pi - \pi$ stacking orientation is constructed in the Y6:DBCl mixed film, which is beneficial for the efficient charge transport and charge collection.39] We further quantitatively analyze the crystallization behavior of Y6 and Y6:DBCl mixed film (IP and OOP cut-off profiles in Figure S7, Supporting Information). As demonstrated in Figure S7c (Supporting Information), the Y6:DBCl mixed film possesses a $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance of $3 . 6 7 \pm \ : 0 . 1 7$ A accompanying with the enhanced coherence length of 24.36 A according to the Scheer equation. In contrast, we cannot figure out the information from neat Y6 due to the moderate crystallinity. Besides, surface morphology and aggregation action are characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements. As illustrated in Figure 2c,d and Figure S8 (Supporting Information), the Y6:DBCl film shows a higher roughness (Y6: $1 . 0 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ Y6:DBCl: $2 . 3 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ value together with bigger domains regarded from the AFM 3D view. These circumstances from GIWAXS and AFM characterizations demonstrate that an obvious molecular packing existed in the Y6 film processed by DBCl addition, which can be ascribed to the enhanced dipole moment and the tunable dipole direction. Moreover, as illustrated in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), thanks to the enhanced crystallinity, the electron mobility is raised from $1 . 5 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in case of the control to $1 . 4 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in case of the Y6 film after the DBCl incorporation.

The tighter packing tested by GIWAXS can also be confirmed by tracking the maximum peak locations of Y6 and Y6:DBCl mixture through UV-vis absorption spectroscopy, respectively. As shown in Figure 2e of thin-film absorption patterns, a critical redshift trend from ${ \approx } 8 0 2$ to $8 2 7 \mathrm { n m }$ is exhibited after mixing DBCl and Y6. However, as shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information), there is no change in the solution state in Y6 according to our absorption measurement. A similar redshift trend is also observed in case of BTP-eC9 films, where the absorption peak redshifts from 813 to $8 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ after the incorporation of DBCl (see Figure S11, Supporting Information). Collectively, this bathochromic-shift effect only appears when the formation of aggregation states in the film because the diploe interaction can only have a markable influence in solid-state conditions. More essentially, due to the interaction in the BTP core unit, the broader absorption range and enhanced absorption shoulder (especially in the range of $6 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ are observed after the DBCl incorporation into Y6 and BTP-eC9. We reason that these features are beneficial for the light-harvesting and further improvement of current density.

To make our conclusions (coupling interaction between DBCl and BTP core) more convincing, the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurement is conducted to probe the intermolecular interaction between the DBCl and Y6. As illustrated in Figure 2f, the stretching vibrations peak in the range from 2800 to $3 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ corresponding to the $- \mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } -$ substituted in the BTP core unit can be investigated.[21]

Thus, all the above fundamental results confirm that DBCl molecule can be efficiently used for process-aid solid engineering. In the solution state, the DBCl and Y6 are dispersed in the chloroform without any intermolecular interaction. After forming the Y6:DBCl intermediate phase, the strong dipole interaction would attract the adjacent molecules due to the different dipole orientation and strong dipole moment. With the volatilization of DBCl, the large specific area in the Y6:DBCl intermediate phase would vanish. As a result, the molecular crystallinity and crystal orientation of Y6 are notably improved, which coincides with our morphological characterizations.

The improvement of photovoltaic performance after the PAS treatment is positively related to the charge carrier dynamic. First, as shown in Figure S16a (Supporting Information), the lower dark current density and higher rectification ratio in the dark $J { - } V$ curves manifest that lower charge recombination potential and higher charge extraction process are happening after the PAS treatment, which is in line with the higher FF in the corresponding devices. Moreover, we evaluate the exciton dissociation and charge collection processes by the dependence of the photocurrent density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } \right)$ on the effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ . As plotted in Figure S16b (Supporting Information), at $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } > 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } ,$ the saturation current density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } \right)$ values for control and PAS-treated device are 25.4 and $2 6 . 5 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , respectively, indicating that more charge carriers are generated in the PAS-based devices. The exciton dissociation efficiency $P ( E , T )$ values for PAS devices is $9 7 . 4 \%$ according to the ratio of $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } / J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } ,$ whereas the value for control devices is only $9 5 . 2 \%$ . Moreover, the PAS devices show a higher charge collection efficiency $( 9 4 . 1 \% )$ than that of the control devices $( 9 0 . 8 \% )$ , reflected by the current density in the maximum power point. These findings demonstrate a coherent improvement of exciton dissociation and charge collection process, which is in line with a higher $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ value in the corresponding device.

To investigate the fascinating FF in the PAS-treated devices, the energetic disorder in the corresponding devices is understood by extracting the Urbach energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { u } } \right)$ , which demonstrates a close relationship with charge transport loss.41] We calculate the $E _ { \mathrm { u } }$ by analyzing data obtained from the sensitive EQE (s-EQE) spectrum via Equation (1)

$$
\alpha ( E ) = \alpha _ { \circ } e ^ { \frac { E - E _ { \mathrm { g } } } { E _ { \mathrm { u } } } }
$$

where the $E$ is the incident photon energy, $\alpha ( E )$ is the optical absorption coefficient, $\alpha _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $E _ { \mathrm { g } }$ are the constants.

By the natural logarithm fitting s-EQE plots, the $E _ { \mathrm { u } }$ values based on control and PAs devices are 31.6 and $3 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { \ m e V } ,$ respectively (shown in Figure 3d), disclosing that enhanced crystallinity of PM6:Y6 blend would decrease the energetic disorder and facilitate the charge transport process after the PAS treatment. To verify this phenomenon, the carrier mobilities (hole and electron) are calculated by measuring the singlecarrier (hole and electron) devices with the space-charge limited current (SCLC) method. In this case, the hole and electron mobility of the control and PAS devices are extracted by fitting the dark $J { - } V$ curves with the SCLC method (shown in Figure S17, Supporting Information). As summarized in Table 1, the hole $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } )$ and electron $( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } )$ mobility are increased from $2 . 2 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $1 . 7 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ to $3 . 9 9 \times \mathrm { { 1 0 ^ { - 4 } } ~ c m ^ { 2 } ~ V ^ { - 1 } ~ s ^ { - 1 } }$ and $1 . 6 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively, together with a $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } / } \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ ratio increased from 0.076 to 0.42 with an increment of $4 5 2 . 6 \%$ We consider that this is possibly due to the difference in the interpercolated electron transport networks in the blends after the DBCl incorporation, which originates from the optimal arrangement and packing symmetry (confirmed by UV-vis and GIWAxs characterizations). Additionally, the energy disorder is decreased after the PAS treatment (seen from the sensitive EQE curves), which can improve the charge transport channels and enhance the charge transport process.

Except for the charge transport process, free charge carriers normally encounter the nongeminate recombination (bimolecular and trap-assisted recombination) behaviors, which is one of the dominant factors of the FF parameter. Thus, the $J { - } V$ curves as a function of light intensity $( I )$ are characterized to get an in-depth observation of the charge recombination kinetics. It is worth to mention that imbalanced hole/electron mobility or space charge can induce the $\alpha < 1$ (without necessarily showing strong bimolecular recombination).[42] As plotted in Figure S18 (Supporting Information), the slope $( \alpha )$ values extracted from the linear fitted $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } - I$ curves are all 0.99, suggesting that bimolecular recombination is not the dominant recombination mechanism. On the other hand, the correlation between $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ and I generally follows the equation: $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } \propto \ln ( I ) n k T / q$ where $k , q .$ and $T$ are the Boltzmann constant, elementary charge, and Kelvin temperature, respectively. The slope in the range from $k T / q$ to $2 \ k T / q$ is applied to distinguish whether the charge carrier transport is constrained by the trap-assisted recombination. Notably, if the slope is lower than $k T / q$ , surface trap-assisted recombination is present.43] As shown in Figure 3e, the plots of $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ versus logarithmic light intensity fit and calculate slopes of 1.31 and $1 . 1 4 ~ k T / q$ for control and PAS-treated device, respectively, showing that PAS strategy can efficiently eliminate the trap-assisted recombination.

To confirm the carrier recombination with this PAS treatment, transient photovoltage (TPV) characterization is used to investigate the carrier lifetime $( \tau )$ . As depicted in Figure S19 (Supporting Information), the PAS-treated devices demonstrate a much longer lifetime of $3 . 4 3 ~ \mu \mathrm { s }$ than that of the control device $\lbrace \tau = 2 . 1 7 \mu \mathrm { s } \rbrace$ , demonstrating the descend carrier recombination channels in the PAS based devices. Transient photocurrent (TPC) measurement is also conducted to evaluate the carrier extraction actions by testing the current density decay under the short-circuited condition.44] As shown in Figure $3 \mathrm { f } ,$ the carrier extraction lifetime for the control device is $3 . 9 4 \mu \mathrm { s }$ , whereas the calculated lifetime of the PAS-based devices is $3 . 3 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { s }$ suggesting a more efficient charge extraction process. Moreover, as shown in Figure S20 (Supporting Information), the PAS-treated device possesses a faster charge dynamic response by tracking the current density controlled through the light pulse turn-on and turn-off procedures. Overall, the FF improvement of PAStreated device can be ascribed to the boosted carrier transport, suppressed carrier trap-assisted recombination, and heightened carrier extraction kinetics.[45]

To explore the PAS working mechanism, flm-depthdependent light absorption spectroscopy (FLAS) is used to study the carrier dynamic actions in the vertical direction.[46] Consistent with the previous Y6 single component absorption spectra, as shown in Figure 3g,j, the absorption peaks contributed by Y6 in the PM6:Y6 blend film are redshifted from ${ \approx } 8 0 0$ to ${ \approx } 8 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ after the PAS treatment. More vitally, we observe that the absorption peak of Y6 varies with the film depth in the control device (highlighted in Figure 3g), indicating that the energy levels are not uniform along the film-depth direction, especially for Y6. This spatial fluctuation of energetic levels inevitably impedes charge transport, deteriorates charge collection and increases the charge recombination potentials, which would induce the FF loss, especially in the thick film devices. For the device with PAS treatment, the alignment of Y6 peaks is available for all film-depth active layers, implying a uniform distribution of energetic levels, which would enhance the thickness tolerance probability in the case of thick active layer film.[46]

On the other hand, the exciton distribution contours, at each film-depth at different wavelengths within the devices, are simulated from the FLAS distribution in combination with a modified optical transfer-matrix model. The light is incident from the bottom side (ITO/PEDOT:PSS side), due to the light interferences among different layers of the photovoltaic devices. As depicted in Figure 3h,k, the excitons generated by Y6 are mostly generated in the bottom part of the active layer (near substrate). After the dissociation of exciton into electrons and holes, the electrons need to cross a $1 0 { \cdot } \mathrm { n m }$ distance toward the cathodes. However, during the long-distance charge transport, the charge recombination (especially trap-assisted recombination) plays an important role in determining the photovoltaic performance. The higher crystalline Y6 phase (verified in Figure 2d) in PAStreated device provides a high-quality n-type pathway for electron transport toward the cathode (near air), which is constant with the considerably boosted exciton generation rates in the range of $7 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ from the PAS device.[47]

After quantifying the exciton distribution, as clarified in Figure S21 (Supporting Information), the electrons are hindered alone with the lower density of exciton in the control device, which is not conducive to the charge transport process. However, the PAS-treated device features a higher exciton generation rate, thus a higher $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF can be yielded in the corresponding device.[48] Furthermore, to discuss the component distribution in the vertical direction, we distinguish the PM6 and Y6 weight ratio with respect to thickness. As shown in

Figure 3i, a low variation of PM6/Y6 ratio as a function of thickness is clarified in the control film without PAS treatment, indicating relatively mixed domains in vertical dimension. In contrast, due to the higher miscibility between Y6 and DBCl, DBCl enshrouds and brings Y6 to the surface during the annealing process. Thus, as plotted in Figure 3l, the Y6/PM6 ratio gradually improves in the photoactive film and Y6 is concentrated at the top region, whereas PM6 is enriched in the bottom part (near substrate). To further confirm this phenomenon, the contact angle is measured by the water droplet. As shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), there is a decrease in the contact angle in the PAS-treated film, which implies more Y6 aggregates are enriched on top of the surface due to the more hydrophilic Y6 molecule. All these results demonstrate that such a top Y6 gathered distribution with obvious phase separation is more beneficial to the carrier extraction, and further improves FF.[49]

The influence of PAS treatment on molecular packing and crystal texture is investigated by GIWAXS measurement. As shown in Figure 4a,b, the neat PM6:Y6 blend film does not show any $\pi - \pi$ stacking signals in the OOP direction, which is due to the moderate crystallinity of Y6 (in agreement with the GIWAXS patterns in neat Y6 film). The diffraction of PAStreated blend film shows a sharp arc in the OOP direction, which is suitable for the efficient charge transport and extraction process. Moreover, the $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance of $3 . 7 1 \ \pm$ $0 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { \AA }$ and coherence length of 22.09 A can be extracted from Figures S23 and S24 (Supporting Information), indicating that the PAS-treated blend possesses a much extensively compact molecular packing in face-on orientation. Moreover, through comparing the (010) peak of PM6, Y6, and the PM6:Y6 blend polymer (Figure S25, Supporting Information, Figure 2a,b, and Figure 4a,b), we identify that the improved morphology of the PAS-treated Y6 leads to an enhanced device performance. Figure S26 (Supporting Information) illustrates the 2D and 3D topographic images of the control and PAS-treated blend films by atomic force microscopy (AFM) technique. Although a relatively smoother surface (RMS: $0 . 9 8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ ) appeared in the neat PM6:Y6 blend film, the lower degree of phase separation matrix is destructive to the charge generation and charge transport due to the fast liquid-liquid film-forming kinetics.49] On the contrary, the PAS-treated film represents a slightly higher roughness $\mathrm { | R M S { : } 1 . 3 5 \ n m }$ with the scrutable surface, which is attributed to the enhanced crystallinity of Y6. Confocal laser scanning confocal Raman microscope is recorded to elucidate the influence of PAS treatment on phase separation behavior in the lateral direction.50] According to our previous results,[51] PM6 possesses a characteristic Raman peak at $2 9 5 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , and Y6 has a characteristic position at $2 2 1 2 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ By monitoring these two signals, we can accurately distinguish the donor and acceptor domains in the blend film. As demonstrated in Figure 4c,d, the domain size of Y6 increases together with the formation of clear interpenetrating charge transport pathways after the PAS treatment, which is attributed to the control of nucleation and crystalline kinetics of Y6 with this DBCl incorporation.

These instinctive investigations from morphological (FLAS, GIWAxs, Raman mapping, AFM) characterizations enable us to understand the original mechanism of the PAS strategy. Combined with film-forming kinetics and vertical distribution analysis, the working mechanism of PAS treatment is proposed with a carton shown in Figure 4e. When the film is processed from the solution without DBCl, the crystal formation of PM6 and Y6 occurs simultaneously due to the fast evaporation of chloroform solvent. As a result, the whole system is quenched into a mixed PM6:Y6 state accompanied with interrupted aggre gation, especially for Y6.51] In contrast, due to the better miscibility of DBCl and Y6 molecule after the PAS treatment, in period ii, an intermediate phase (Y6:DBCl mixture) is formed in the photoactive film, which would construct upgraded noncovalent interaction and dipole moment interaction compared to neat Y6. In period iii, due to the sublimation ability of DBCl, PM6 and Y6 have enough time for reconstructing the aggregation conformation toward the thermal driving force in the lateral and vertical direction. This PAS engineering facilitates the blend system to approach its equilibrium state, i.e., more uniform distribution in vertical orientation and appropriate phase separation morphology (proved by Raman mapping).[51] Such superiorities of PAS engineering would accelerate the exciton generation, charge transport, and collection kinetics, which is beneficial for the $\mathrm { \ h i g h \ : } J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF in the corresponding devices.

# 3. Conclusion

A multifunctional morphological tailoring strategy (PAS engineering) is devised to simultaneously optimize the blend morphology and enhance the aggregation state owing to the asymmetric halogen distribution and high-volatile property of DBCl. The molecular organization of Y6 can be efficiently manipulated via coupling with DBCl and Y6, especially the significant affinity to the core BTP unit, thereby boosting the aggregation state of Y6. After annealing, proper phase separation in lateral direction accompanied with uniform vertical distribution would spontaneously promote the exciton generation, charge transport, and extraction and suppress the trap-assisted recombination. Furthermore, this PAS engineering is a versatile strategy to improve the PCE of Y-series small-molecule systems. Especially for the PM6:L8-BO system, a maximum PCE of $1 8 . 5 \%$ with a superior FF of $8 0 . 2 \%$ is yielded after the PAS treatment. More crucially, the PAS treatment grants a superior thickness-tolerance feature for the active layer (a record PCE of $1 7 . 0 \%$ with a $3 0 0 \mathrm { - n m }$ thickness active layer for devices). Our work demonstrates that PAS engineering could accurately control the aggregated structure and phase separation in organic solar cells, showing a promising future for applying in other organic electronic fields.
