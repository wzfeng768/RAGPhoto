# Medium Bandgap Conjugated Polymer for High Performance Polymer Solar Cells Exceeding 9% Power Conversion Efficiency

Jae Woong Jung, Feng Liu, Thomas P. Russell, and Won Ho Jo\*

Polymer solar cells (PSCs) with bulk heterojunction (BHJ) structure composed of conjugated polymer and fullerene derivative have emerged as the most promising renewable energy resource due to their advantages such as low production cost, light weight, flexibility, and solution processability.[l For the past decade, enormous effort has been made for development of new conjugated polymers to improve photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Especially, low bandgap polymers $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } < 1 . 6 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ have enhanced the short-circuit current density $( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } )$ of PSC[2] and thereby the PSCs fabricated from low bandgap polymers have exhibited high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) close to $10 \%$ in single-junction devices.3l However, most PSCs made of low bandgap polymers suffer from limited open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ because of their high-lying highest occupied molecular orbital (HOmO) energy levels and relatively low incident photon-to-current efficiency (IPCE) due to the energy loss derived from low bandgap.4] Moreover, the intrinsic narrow absorption range of low bandgap polymer is far from the broad photoresponse for high efficiency PSCs. These features of low bandgap polymers may limit the PCE of the derived PSCs.

Another class of conjugated polymers for photovoltaic applications is medium bandgap polymers $( 1 . 6 < E _ { \mathrm { g } } < 1 . 8 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ ).[5] Despite the limited light absorption of relatively short wavelength range $( < 7 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ of medium bandgap polymers, the strategy of molecular engineering and morphology control allows us to improve exciton generation and charge transport for achieving high $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ comparable to low bandgap polymers. Particularly, rational molecular design of medium bandgap polymer can tune its molecular energy levels to have deep HOMO energy level without increase of bandgap, which affords enhanced $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ without sacrifice of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ . Moreover, the medium bandgap polymer is suitable for a short-wavelength light absorber in multijunction tandem device.

Donora-cceptor (D-A) alternating structure has shown great promise to lower the bandgap of conjugated polymer.[6] Particularly, a combination of "weak donor" and "strong acceptor" could be an effective strategy to achieve medium bandgap polymer $( \mathrm { { \approx } 1 . 7 ~ e V ) }$ for high efficiency PSCs.7l Since "weak donor" affords deep HOMO energy level while "strong acceptor" lowers the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level of conjugated polymer,[8] the polymer has deep HOMO energy level without broadening of bandgap. As an ideal building block for medium bandgap polymer, benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b]dithiohpene (BDT) and 2,1,3-benzothiadiazole (BT) have gained much attention as "weak donor' and "strong acceptor," respectively, due to their high chemical stability, planar molecular structure, and benefit of simple preparation.9l Recently, several BDT/BT-based copolymers have shown promising PCEs over $8 \%$ , demonstrating that the combination BDT and BT is one of ideal pairs for high performance PsCs.[10] Furthermore, introduction of various functional groups on BDT and/or BT can modulate optical, electrical, and morphological properties of the polymers, which affect the final performance of photovolatic device.[11]

Wang et al.[12] have reported that $\pi$ bridge units between D and A units largely affect the molecular architecture and optoelectronic properties of D-A type conjugated polymers. It has also been reported that extended $\pi$ conjugation in polymer backbone effectively reduces conformational disorder and thus increases molecular rigidity in the solid state to facilitate electron delocalization, resulting in increase of the absorption coefficient, crystallinity, and charge transport of the conjugated polymers.[13] In this regard, optimizing the molecular structure of copolymer by proper choice of $\pi$ bridge unit could be an important strategy to enhance the PCE of medium bandgap polymer-based PSCs.

derivatives, we chose alkylthiothienyl substituted BDT (BDTTS) as D-unit to construct 2D $\pi$ extended conjugated polymer, which affords broader light absorption, strong interchain interaction, and efficient charge transport.[14] We also used BT or difluoro-substituted BT (ffBT) as A-unit, and utilized 6-octylthieno[3,2-b]thiophene (TT),[15] a $\pi$ extended fused heteroarene, as $\pi$ bridge unit for D-A type conjugated copolymers. Two copolymers synthesized in this work exhibit medium bandgap $( \mathrm { { \approx } 1 . 7 ~ \ e V ) }$ with broad light absorption from 350 to $7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ .Especially, the fuorinated one (PBDTTS-TTffBT) exhibits intense light absorption, deep HOMO level, decent hole mobility and favorable faceon orientation, which are key parameters to achieve high photovoltaic performance. Consequently, the solar cell device made of PBDT'TS-T'TffBT exhibits an impressive PCE of $9 . 4 4 \%$ , which is superior to the non-fluorinated counterpart (PBDT'TS-T'TBT) $( 6 . 7 8 \% )$

When the molecular energy levels of PBDTTs-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT were measured by cyclic voltammetry, the HOMO energy levels of PBDTTS-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT are $- 5 . 4 0$ and $- 5 . 4 5 \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively, as estimated from an empirical equation of $E _ { \mathrm { H O M O } } ( \mathrm { e V } ) = - ( E _ { \mathrm { o n s e t } } ( \mathrm { o x . } ) + 4 . 4 )$ (Figure 1c). The fluorine substitution in PBDTTS-TTffBT causes deeper HOMO energy level, which may enhance $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of the resulting

PSC device. We estimated the LUMO energy levels of PBDTTSTTBT $( - 3 . 6 7 ~ \mathrm { ~ e V } )$ and PBDTTS-TTffBT $( - 3 . 6 9 ~ \mathrm { { \ e V } ) }$ by subtracting the optical bandgap from the corresponding HOMO energy level. Given that the LUMO of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ is $- 4 . 1 0 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ the two polymers exhibit large LUMO offsets to $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ (0.44 and $0 . 4 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for PBDTTS-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT, respectively), affording enough driving force for exciton dissociation and electron transfer within the active layer (Figure 1d). When the molecular orbital distributions at HOMO and LUMO levels were calculated from the density functional theory (DFT), the HOMO electrons are distributed mainly on electron-rich BDT unit (extending slightly to the $\pi$ bridge TT unit), whereas the LUMO electrons are localized on electron-deficient BT unit for both PBDTTS-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT (Figure S2, Supporting Information). It should be noted here that the HOMO and LUMO electrons of PBDTTS-TTffBT are more delocalized over the polymer backbone, indicating that PBDTTS-TTffBT has more likely the quinoidal structure (strong donor-acceptor hybridization) than PBDT'TS-T'TBT.

One of the most important properties of conjugated polymers for optoelectronic applications is crystallinity, which favors charge carrier transport in the active layer. When the crystal properties of PBDTTS-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT are investigated by the grazing-incident X-ray diffraction (GIXD), both polymers exhibit distinct diffractions at $q _ { \mathrm { z } } = 0 . 3 9$ , 0.79, $1 . 1 8 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ corresponding to (100), (200), (300) reflection, respectively (Figure S3, Supporting Information), indicating that the two polymers form well-ordered lamellar crystallites with a lamellar packing distance of $1 . 6 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Particularly, the out-ofplane $( q _ { \mathrm { z } } )$ scan reveals that PBDTTs-TTffBT shows a strong peak of (010) diffraction at $1 . 6 1 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ , which corresponds to a $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distance of $0 . 3 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ while PBDTTS-TTBT does not show discernible (O10) diffraction. This indicates that PBDTTST'TffBT adopts a mixed orientation of face-on and edge-on while PBDTTS-TTBT takes predominantly the edge-on orientation.

To investigate photovoltaic performance of the polymers, we fabricated the PSC devices with an inverted configuration $( \mathrm { I T O } /$ ZnO/PEIE/polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } / \mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { A g } )$ .The $J { - } V$ curves of PSCs under AM 1.5G illumination are shown in Figure 2a and relevant photovoltaic properties are summarized in Table 2. The optimum blend ratio of polymer to $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ was 1:1.5 and 1:1.8 for PBDT'TS-TTBT and PBDTTS-TTffBT, respectively. In order to optimize the morphology of active layer for the best photovoltaic performance of the device, we used a mixed solvent (chlorobenzene (CB) with $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { v o l } \%$ of 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO)) (Figure S4 and Table S1, Supporting Information). Details of device fabrication procedures are described in the Supporting Information. The PSCs made of PBDTTS-TTffBT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend exhibits the best PCE of $9 . 4 4 \%$ (an average PCE of $9 . 2 2 \%$ with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 5 . 4 3 \ \mathrm { m A }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and a FF of 0.72), while the PSC made of PBDTTS-TTBT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend shows lower PCE (the best $\mathrm { P C E } = 6 . 7 8 \%$ with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 1 \mathrm { V } ,$ $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 2 . 5 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , and a fill factor (FF) of 0.67). To the best of our knowledge, $9 . 4 4 \%$ PCE is one of the highest values reported in the literature for the PSCs based on medium bandgap polymers. The higher $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of the device made of PBDTTS-T'TffBT than that of PBDTTS-TTBT may arise from deeper energy level of PBDTTS-TTffBT, because $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ is generally proportional to the difference between the HOmO energy level of donor polymer and the LUMO energy level of fullerene acceptor. While the optical bandgap of PBDTTS-TTffBT is nearly the same as that of PBDTTS-TTBT, the PBDTTS-TTffBT-based device shows higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ than the PBDTTS-TTBT-based one. As seen in Figure 2b, the PSC device made of PBDTTS-TTffBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ shows broad and intense photoresponse in $3 0 0 { - } 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ with the maximum IPCE of $89 \%$ , while PBDTTS-TTBT-based device shows lower IPCE, which is consistent with the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values.

To further understand the difference of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF between the two polymers, we investigated the charge carrier generation, transport, and charge recombination. To estimate the maximum rate of exciton generation, we measured photocurrent density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } \right)$ as a function of the effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ of the device, as shown in Figure 3a. Since $J _ { \mathrm { { p h } } }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ denote the photocurrent extraction and electric field applied in the device, respectively, it is reasonable to assume that photogenerated charge carriers rapidly move toward the corresponding electrodes with minimal recombination at high $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } .$ Hence, $J _ { \mathrm { p h } }$ is saturated as the $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ exceeds $0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ and therefore the maximum rate of exciton generation $( G _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ in the device can be assessed from an equation $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } = q { \cdot } L { \cdot } G _ { \mathrm { m a x } } ,$ where $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ is the saturation photocurrent density, $q$ is the electronic charge, and $L$ is the thickness of the active layer.6 The $G _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of PBDTTST'TffBT-based device is $1 5 \%$ higher than that of the PBDTTS-TTBT-based one due to larger $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } ,$ which arises from stronger light absorption of PBDTTS-TTffBT than PBDTTSTTBT (Table 2). We investigated the charge recombination in PBDTTS-TTBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and PBDTTS-TTffBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blends by measuring the dependence of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ on the light intensity. Since the exciton quenching in the BHJ active layer is closely related to the charge recombination, the light intensity dependence of $J { - } V$ characteristics can probe the dominant recombination mechanism which affects the device performance. The correlation between $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and light intensity $( P )$ can be described by $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \propto \ P ^ { \alpha } , { } ^ { [ 1 7 ] }$ where $\alpha$ becomes unity when bimolecular recombination is negligible. Logarithmic plots of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ versus $P$ (Figure 3b) show slopes of 0.96 and 0.99 for the devices made of PBDTTSTTBT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and PBDTTS-TTffBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ respectively, indicating that the bimolecular recombination in PBDTTS-TTffBT-based device is smaller than PBDTTS-TTBT. $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ -based one.

We also investigated charge carrier transport in device by measuring the dark current as a function of effective voltage, as shown in Figure 3c and 3d. Fitting by the space charge-limited current (SCLC) model reveals that the hole mobilities $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } )$ of PBDTTS-TTBT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and PBDTTS-TTffBT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend are $3 . 7 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $6 . 1 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, while the electron mobilities $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } \right)$ in the two devices are $2 . 2 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $5 . 7 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ respectively. This indicates that the PBDTTS-T'TffBT $\mathrm { \cdot P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ based device exhibits efficient and well-balanced charge transport compared with PBDTTSTTBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ -based one. The crystal orientation of conjugated polymers in BHJ active layer is one of important parameters to determine the charge transport property in photovoltaic device. In conclusion, we have designed and synthesized two medium bandgap conjugated polymers, PBDTTS-TTBT and PBDTTS-T'TffBT, composed of alkylthiothienyl-substitued BDT and BT with TT as $\pi$ bridge for photovoltaic application. In spite of their medium bandgaps $( { \approx } 1 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ , they exhibit affirmative optoelectronic properties such as intense absorptivity, lowlying molecular energy levels, and high crystallinity. Particularly, fluoro-substitutedPBDTTS-TTffBT $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend develops nanoscale phase separation with interconnected network structure and also exhibits mixed crystal orientation of edge-on and face-on orientation in the BHJ active layer, which afford efficient hole transport, balanced hole/electron transport and suppressed bimolecular recombination. In conjunction with deep HOMO level, PBDTTS-TTffBT achieved a high PCE of $9 . 4 4 \%$ with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } ,$ a $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 5 . 4 3 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , and FF of 0.72. To the best of our knowledge, the PCE value of $9 . 4 4 \%$ is among one of the best values reported for PSCs fabricated from medium bandgap polymers. This work not only demonstrates that medium bandgap polymer can be used as a donor material for high performance PSCs with PCE exceeding $9 \%$ , but also provides a possibility that the medium bandgap polymer can be used as a short-wavelength light absorber in multi-junction tandem device.
