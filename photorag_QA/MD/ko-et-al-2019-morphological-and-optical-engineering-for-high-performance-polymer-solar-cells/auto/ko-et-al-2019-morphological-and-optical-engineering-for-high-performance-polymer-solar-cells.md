ABSTRACT: We demonstrate morphological and optical engineering by using processing additives and optical spacers for polymer solar cell. Among various processing additives, introduction of diphenyl ether (DPE) into the active layer results in the smoothest surface roughness with uniform and welldistributed donor/acceptor domains, and the device with DPE shows the highest device efficiency of $1 0 . 2 2 \%$ due to enhanced charge collection efficiency and minimized recombination loss. Additional $\mathrm { Z n O }$ optical spacers on the active layer controls the distribution of the electric field in the whole device and enhances the light absorption within the active layer, thereby improving device efficiency up to $1 0 . 8 1 \%$

These improvements have mostly resulted from development of new donor polymers. Although polymers with small bandgap are favorable for more light absorption, it is difficult for these polymers to have a deep highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level. Specifically, the bandgap tuning process for designing small bandgap polymers simultaneously decreases the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level and increases the HOMO level, leading to an insufficient driving force for excition dissociation at the donor/acceptor $\left( \mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A } \right)$ interface and low $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ by small difference between $\mathrm { H O M O } _ { \mathrm { d o n o r } }$ and $\mathrm { L U M O } _ { \mathrm { a c c e p t o r } } .$ As a result of the needs for polymers that have both broad light absorption and a deep HOMO level, high-performance donor polymers have been developed and single-junction PSCs based on these polymers achieved PCEs of $9 - 1 1 \%$ 6-8

Morphology engineering is one of the critical factors for determining device performance. In spite of excellent properties of donor polymers, unless morphology optimization is realized in BHJ films consisting of polymers and fullerene derivatives, there are poor device efficiencies due to dominant charge-carrier recombination loss by incomplete formation of. $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ domains for exciton dissociation and charge transport. Among various methods including thermal annealing and solvent-vapor treatment, introduction of processing additives into BHJ flm has been commonly used for morphology optimization in BHJ PSCs because of its simple applicability and high reproducibility. A small amount of additive (less than $3 \ \mathrm { v o l } \ \%$ ) led to remarkable enhancement in device performance.9,10 When BHJ components of poly[4,8-bis(5-(2- ethylhexyl)thiophen-2-yl)benzo[1,2-b;4,5-b']dithiophene-2,6- diyl-alt-(4-(2-ethylhexyl)-3-fluorothieno[3,4-b]thiophene-)-2- carboxylate-2-6-diyl)] (PTB7-Th) and [6,6]-phenyl- $\mathrm { \cdot C } _ { 7 1 }$ butyric acid methyl ester $\mathbf { \Gamma } ^ { ' } \mathbf { P } \mathbf { C } _ { 7 1 } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } )$ dissolved in pure chlorobenzene (CB) were used for the active layer, this device exhibited PCE of $3 . 9 2 \%$ . In contrast, incorporation of 1,8- diiodooctane (DIO) additive into CB solution led to approximately 2-fold increase in PCE $( 7 . 4 0 \% )$ .11,12 1- Chloronaphthalene (CN) and 1,8-octanedithiol (ODT) were also incorporated in BHJ PSCs and yielded significant increase in device efficiency.13-16 These enhancements resulted from improvements in excition dissociation and charge transport by formation of $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ bicontinuous interpenetrating network. However, processing additives have not guarantee the improvement of device efficiency at all times. Qiao et al. reported that addition of CN and DIO additives in BHJ film of poly(diketopyrrolopyrrole-terthiophene) and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ improved domain purity and led to fast charge collection, while ODT resulted in well-connected domains with smaller size and increased charge collection time.17 The devices with CN and DIO exhibited 2-fold PCE enhancement, whereas there was PCE decrease for the device with ODT, compared to that of pristine device without additive. In the same group, they employed 2,3-dihydroxypyridine (DHP) additive in both BHJ flms of PDPP3T/ $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and poly(3-hexylthiophene)/ indene ${ \bf \cdot C } _ { 6 0 }$ bisadduct (ICBA).18,19 The device with DHP showed remarkable improvement in device efficiency due to improved polymer crystallinity, bicontinuous interpenetrated phase separation and balanced charge transport. However, few processing additives have been used for morphology engineering. This implies that there is still considerable room for new processing additives that can effectively optimize morphology of BHJ film and thereby maximize the device performance.

Here, we demonstrate that a morphology engineering technique using optimum processing additive successfully optimizes the active layer morphology and improves device efficiency over $1 0 \%$ . In addition, a zinc oxide $\mathrm { ( Z n O ) }$ layer as both electron transport20,21 and optical spacer layer is introduced between the active layer and top electrode to improve the short-circuit current density $( J _ { \mathrm { S C } } )$ by enhanced charge generation rate. We introduce various additives into BHJ system of poly $[ 2 , 6 ^ { \prime }$ -4,8-di(5-ethylhexylthienyl)benzo[1,2-b;3,3-b] dithiophene][3-fluoro-2[(2-ethylhexyl)- carbonyl]thieno-[3,4-b]thiophenediyl]] (PTB7-Th) and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ (Figure 1a). The device with DPE achieves the highest device efficiency of $1 0 . 2 2 \%$ . High PCE is attributed to maximized charge generation efficiency and minimized recombination loss by optimized morphology of the active layer and strong interaction between polymer backbones.

To compare additive effect on device performance, we employed various additives that are DIO, ODT, CN, and DPE (Figure 1a). We also used PTB7-Th as electron donor and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as electron acceptor. PTB7-Th with a band gap of 1.7 eV has a cascade energy level structure between $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT-PSS), enabling high-performance fullerene-based PSCs with PCE of over $1 0 \%$ (Figure 1b). We fabricated conventional PSCs with device configuration of indium tin oxide (ITO)/PEDOT-PSS/active layer/Al (Figure 1c).

As shown in Figure 3, the film prepared from CB had a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of $2 . 2 0 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . All additives resulted in even BHJ films with a smoother surface apart from ODT (ODT, $3 . 4 4 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ CN, $0 . 9 7 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ ; DIO, $1 . 2 8 1 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ ). Uniform and smooth surfaces also supported high performance of the device with DPE. Furthermore, the positive effect of DPE is in good agreement with previous literature, where DPE led to desirable morphological changes and agglomerated phase separation in the form of fbril-like nanostructures in other BHJ films.23

To further investigate molecular ordering and packing characteristics of pristine polymer and BHJ flms, we performed grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAxs) measurements.4 The GiwAxs patterns of pristine polymer and BHJ films with different additives are shown in Figure S2. The detailed GIwAXs plots and parameters are listed in Figure S3 and Table S1, respectively. All pristine polymer films exhibited reflection peaks in in-plane direction. Corresponding interlamellar spacing were 19.7, 20.1, 18.5, 19.0, and $\mathrm { i } { \bf \bar { 8 } } . 5 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for CB, CB/ODT, CB/CN, CB/DIO, and CB/DPE, respectively. Pronounced $\pi - \pi$ stacking peaks in out-of-plane direction were also observed regardless of additives, indicating dominant face-on orientation of polymer backbones. Resultant face-on stacking distances were around 3.90-3.92 A for all pristine polymer films with the exception of CN (3.95 A). The $\pi - \pi$ stacking face-on orientation is beneficial to efficient charge transport in PSCs. However, the BHJ films with any additives did not show any evidence of differences in lamellar spacing or $\pi - \pi$ stacking. Among those processing additives, the DPE may be the most favorable to facilitate aggregation and thorough phase separation of the polymer mixture relative to the other processing conditions, supporting efficient charge transfer and consistent with the high Jsc and FF observed with this solvent system.25

Transfer matrix formalism was carried out to estimate improved light manipulation by the insertion of $\mathrm { Z n O }$ optical spacer. The formalism is an effective technique to calculate the interaction between a thin film device and incident electric field. It calculates the transfer matrix of each thin film layer using optical constants of the corresponding layer, and the transmitted and reflected field at each interface between adjacent thin films are further calculated. This method is powerful especially for the PSCs, in which field interferences are dominant inside the device owing to the thin active layer; both incident light and reflected light by the top metal electrode have significant influences for the device operation. For the calculation, optical constants of each material, $n$ and $k _ { r }$ were deduced from the spectra collected by UV-vis absorption spectroscopy. The refractive index, $n$ was further corrected using the Kramers-Kronig relation. The optical constants are summarized in Figure S4a. Thickness-dependent $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ s were estimated for the devices with and without ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ optical spacer, and the results are shown in Figure S4b. Devices with the active layer thickness $\left( t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } \right)$ less than $1 4 0 ~ \mathrm { { \ n m } }$ exhibited gradual decreases in $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ with increasing thickness of $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ $\left( t _ { \mathrm { Z n O } } \right)$ . By contrast, devices with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } > 1 4 0 \ \mathrm { \ n m }$ outperformed the device without optical spacer as a result of constructive interference of incident and reflected light in the proper position (in this case, active layer) of the device. Electric field distribution profiles of the devices with all considered cases in Figure S4b are summarized in Figure S5.

The calculated field profiles reveal that $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ reduction for the devices with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } < 1 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ is attributed to the localization of electric field (EI, the localized fields right under the metal) at the interface between active layer and $Z _ { \mathrm { { n O } } }$ resulting in parasitic absorption by optical spacer. However, the ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ optical spacer effectively rearranged the electric field inside the active layer and led to improved light absorption of the device when $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } > 1 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . On the basis of the simulated results shown in Figure S4b and Figure S5, $t _ { \mathrm { Z n O } } = 3 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ and $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } =$ 190 and $2 8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ were chosen as optimal conditions.

Spectrally resolved spatial distribution of electric field inside the optimal devices are shown in Figure 4a. For the device with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } = 2 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ,$ it is shown that electric fields are strongly localized and form two peaks (EI and EII; EII is the localized fields below EI) inside the active layer at wavelengths from 400 to $8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which are the effective wavelengths for the PTB7- $\mathrm { T h / P C _ { 7 1 } B M }$ blend system, whereas, in the case of the device with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } = 1 9 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ EII was not mostly localized within the active layer at wavelengths longer than $6 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Considering the fact that the intensity and distribution of residual electric fields are directly proportional to light absorption of the device, it is expected that the device with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } = 1 9 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ would exhibit less light absorption in the range from 600 to $8 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ With the help of the $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ optical spacer, however, EII of the device with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } ~ = ~ 1 9 0 ~ \mathrm { { \ n m } }$ is included in the active layer even at wavelengths longer than $6 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ which promotes enhanced light absorption in the wavelengths. For the device with $t _ { \mathrm { a c t i v e } } =$ $2 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ,$ however, the profile was not significantly distorted by the $\mathrm { Z n O }$ optical spacer. Hence, it is expected that $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ s of the device would be nearly identical.

To investigate the role of $\mathrm { Z n O }$ is optical spacer and/or electron transport layer, we measured electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The EIS measurements were carried out at 1 sun condition in the frequency range $0 . 1 { - } 1 \mathrm { M H z }$ . The EIS spectra were plotted as real and imaginary components to differentiate between the resistive and capacitive components; a single semicircle was observed in Figure S6. The EIS spectra of the solar cells were fitted using a equivalent circuit (inset of Figure S6), where Rs and Rct are the series and charge transfer resistance, respectively. The introduction of ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ slightly reduced the Rs value from 34.5 to $3 0 . 5 \ \Omega$ In addition, Rct values were measured as 23.9 and $1 5 . 4 \Omega$ for the device without and with ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ , respectively (Table S2). These similar Rs and Rct values supported similar FF's of the two devices.

In conclusion, we demonstrate the morphology engineering using processing additive and optical engineering using a ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ optical spacer in PSCs based on BHJ films of PTB7-Th/ $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ Among four processing additives (ODT, CN, DIO, and DPE), the device with DPE showed the highest charge collection efficiency and minimized bimolecular recombination during exciton dissociation and charge transport process. In addition, the BHJ film with DPE had a uniform and smooth surface morphology with a well-distributed $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ network. As a result, the introduction of DPE into the BHJ film led to the highest efficiency of $1 0 . 2 2 \%$ which mostly resulted from enhancements of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF. Furthermore, the $\mathrm { Z n O }$ additional layer coated on top of the active layer played a role both as electron transport layer and as optical spacer, thereby leading to increased device efficiency up to $1 0 . 8 1 \%$ through enhanced light absorption within the active layer and reduced series and charge transfer resistance. Therefore, the morphological and optical engineering processes for PSCs with BHJ structure by using processing additives and introduction of an optical spacer are the effective methods for further improving the device efficiency of PSCs.
