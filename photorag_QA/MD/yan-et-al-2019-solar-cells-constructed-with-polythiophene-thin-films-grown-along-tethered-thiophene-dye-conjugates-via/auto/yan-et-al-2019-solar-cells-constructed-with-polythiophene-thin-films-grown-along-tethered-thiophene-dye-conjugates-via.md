ABSTRACT: A polythiophene-based solar cell (PTSC) is constructed by photoelectrochemically polymerizing thiophene onto an ultrathin compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer ( $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick) covered with a sub-monolayer of tethered 3-{5-[N,N-bis(4- diphenylamino)phenyl]thieno[3,2-b]thiophen-2-yl}-2-cyano-acrylic acid dye (ca. $1 0 \%$ coverage). The influence of morphology and thickness of the PT film on the photocurrent generated by the PTSC was investigated. With a $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film and $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } , 7 , 7 ^ { \prime } }$ -tetrakis( $( N , N -$ -di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino)- $^ { . 9 , 9 ^ { \prime } }$ spirobifluorene serving as the hole-transport material, the PTsC exhibited a short-circuit current density $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 2 . 9 0 \pm 0 . 6 3 \mathrm { { \dot { \ m A / c m } } ^ { 2 } }$ an open-circuit voltage $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 1 \pm 0 . 0 1 \mathrm { \ V } ,$ and a fill factor of $0 . 7 2 \pm 0 . 0 1$ . The high conversion efficiency $\left( 7 . 5 2 \pm 0 . 5 8 \% \right)$ of the PTSC is attributed to the controlled PT growth along the ordered and spatially accessible dye molecules at the compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer, which facilitates charge transfer, prevents the hole/electron recombination, and simplifies the polymer solar cell construction with a stable and easily processable material. # 1. INTRODUCTION

Solar cells based on semiconducting conjugated polymers have several distinctive features, such as low cost, light weight, and good processibility.1-8 Polythiophene (PT) is attractive among the conjugated polymers due to its high stability of (un)doped states, easy structural modification, tailorable electrochemical properties, and strong absorption in the visible spectrum.9-12

Recently, thin PT films have been used as conducting layers for charge transport, photoactive layers, hole-transfer mediators, and solid electrolytes.13-22 Polythiophene-based solar cells (PTSCs) are considered as a type of promising photovoltaic device.

PTSCs can be constructed by simple dip-coating or doctorblading methods.15,23-25 For example, Senadeera et al. dipcoated a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ /fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate in poly(thiophenemalonic acid), with the resultant polymer coating serving as a light absorbing material to yield a conversion efficiency of around $1 . 8 \%$ 23 Salatelli et al. obtained a conversion efficiency of $3 . 5 \%$ by doctor-blading a polythiophene/fullerene blend onto an indium tin oxide (ITO) glass slide precoated with a hole-transporting hybrid, poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)/polystyrenesulfonic acid (PEDOr/Pss).24 Another widely used method is spin coating, 16,26-28 with which Kim et al. deposited poly(3- hexylthiophene) (P3HT) nanowires and nanocrystals on a PEDOT/PSS/ITO substrate and obtained a conversion efficiency of $5 . 5 \%$ 16 Although these methods are simple, the connection between the photoactive and hole/electron transport layers is not tight and uniform. A thick, loose, and disordered polymer layer can impede charge transport and cause hole/electron recombination. To mitigate the abovementioned problems, electrochemical and photoelectrochemical polymerization methods have been used.17,29-33 By maintaining $1 . 2 \textrm { V }$ vs $\mathrm { \ A g / A g C l }$ for $_ { 6 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } }$ to deposit a PT layer onto $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanotube arrays, Lan et al. obtained a conversion efficiency of $1 . 4 6 \%$ 31 Under visible light and using $_ { 0 . 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ as the deposition potential, Lim et al. grew PT around cisbis(isothiocyanato)bis( $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -bipyridyl- $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -dicarboxylato)- ruthenium $\left( \mathrm { I I } \right)$ (N3)-covered $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles to fill the crevices in between. The use of such a thick N3/PT composite layer $\left( 1 2 \ \mu \mathrm { m } \right)$ as a light absorbing material produced a conversion efficiency of ${ \bar { 6 } } . 4 4 \%$ 33 However, the orientation of the PT backbone in these cells is disordered due to fast PT deposition and the uneven and thick layer of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles, which resulted in a relatively low fill factor (FF) and high charge-transfer resistance.33,34

The light-to-energy conversion of PTSCs consists of four steps:1o (1) electron transition from the ground state to the excited state of the polymer layer upon absorption of light; (2) electron diffusion from the excited state to the interface of polymer/electron- or hole-transport layer; (3) hole/electron separation at the interface; and (4) hole and electron movements to and collection by the opposite electrodes. In step (2), efficient charge transport is needed to reduce the electron/hole recombination. If the interior of the polymer layer is not ordered, the diffusion length and charge lifetimes will be reduced. Tremel et al. reported that the electrontransfer rate along the polymer backbone is 10-fold greater than that perpendicular to the backbone.3 Recently, Chen and co-workers demonstrated that the edge-on orientation of P3HT with respect to an underlying perovskite layer leads to a high hole-extracting property.18 This observation was further confrmed by Zhang et al.36 We have shown that onedimensional electron transfer could also be achieved with a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanowire array.37 Therefore, the PT backbone is an excellent one-dimensional nanostructure for electron transport and can delocalize charges while retarding the electron/hole recombination.

We wish to report a new design to form a layer of highly aligned PT molecules. A PTSC comprising a $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film produced a conversion efficiency of $7 . 5 2 \pm 0 . 5 8 \%$ which is better than the values from the studies cited above.16,23,24,31,33 The conductive PT film was grown at a sub-monolayer of 3-{5- [N,N-bis(4-diphenylamino)pheny]thieno[3,2-b]thiophen-2- yl}-2-cyano-acrylic acid (C207) dye molecules38 tethered onto an ultrathin compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer ( $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ n m }$ thick) via photoelectrochemical polymerization of thiophene (Figure 1A). The $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } , 7 , 7 ^ { \prime } }$ tetrakis( $_ { \mathrm { . } } N , N .$ di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino)- $^ { 9 , 9 ^ { \prime } }$ -spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) was coated onto the PT flm as the hole-transport layer, followed by spraying a layer of gold onto spiro-OMeTAD for electrical contact. As depicted in Figure 1B, each C207 dye molecule is tethered onto the compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer via ester formation between its carboxyl group and the hydroxyl group at $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface.39 From the absorbance value of the dye molecules stripped from the dye/ $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ /FTO layer (red curve in Figure S1A), the dye surface coverage was deduced to be $7 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 1 } \mathrm { m o l / c m } ^ { 2 }$ which corresponds to about $1 0 \%$ of the density of the hexagonal close-packed monolayer, $7 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } \mathrm { m o l } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ 40-42 Formation of such a sub-monolayer of tethered dye molecules is key to the subsequent PT flm grown, as space is provided for thiophene monomers to $\pi - \pi$ stack onto the dye aromatic moieties.

# 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 2A is an overlay of linear scan voltammograms collected at a dye/ $\mathrm { \ T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { F T O }$ substrate in the dark (black curve) and under irradiation (red curve). In the dark, the onset potential is about $_ { 0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ vs $\mathrm { { A g / A g C l } , }$ which initiates the electrochemical polymerization of thiophene. In contrast, the onset potential decreases to $- 0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ under irradiation. As for the $\mathrm { T i } \mathrm { \bar { O } } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { F T O }$ substrate, the onset potential is about $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { V }$ both in the dark (blue curve) and under irradiation (green curve) and the polythiophene molecules do not adhere to the compact layer. These results support our contention that the thiophene oxidation and polymerization were initiated by the tethered dye molecules. As shown in Figure 2B, when the substrate was biased at $0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ and under irradiation, thiophene polymerization proceeded continuously. The photocurrents increase with the dye surface coverage (cf. Figure S3). The PT flm can also be deposited via cyclic voltammetry (inset of Figure 2B). When the potential was cycled to $1 . 2 \mathrm { V } _ { \it \cdot }$ the PT film thickness increased. When the potential was cycled to $- 0 . 9 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ the dopant, $\mathrm { T M A } ^ { + }$ was incorporated into the PT film.

Figure 2C is an overlay of absorption spectra of a dye/ $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } /$ FTO substrate (black curve), along with the same substrate covered with a photoelectrochemically deposited $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film (red curves). After polymerization, the $\mathrm { P T } / \mathrm { d y e } / \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } /$ FTO substrates displayed a broad adsorption band between 370 and $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ which can be assigned to the $\pi - \pi ^ { * }$ (highest occupied molecular orbital-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) transition in the PT backbone.28,45 The PT flm and the dye molecules constitute 96.4 and $3 . 6 \%$ of the total absorbance, respectively. Thus, the PTSC relies almost exclusively on the PT photoactive film to generate photocurrent. Figure 2D compares a Raman spectrum of the dye molecules adsorbed onto the compact, $1 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer (black curve) to that after polymerization (red curve). The peaks at 144, 398, 517, and $6 \dot { 3 } 8 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ are ascribed to anat ase $\mathrm { T i O _ { 2 } } , ^ { 4 6 , 4 7 }$ and the peaks between 1300 and $1 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (inset) are assigned to the dye. The significant attenuation of these peaks in the red curve confirms the deposition of a PT film through the space between the tethered dye molecules to eventually cover the entire dye sub-monolayer (cf. Figure 1A). Upon PT film formation, the symmetric stretching of the thiophene ring within the polymer skeleton emerged at 1457 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - \hat { 1 } }$ 48,49

SEM images of two dye/ $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ /FTO substrates, covered with $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 3A) and $4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick (Figure 3B) PT films, respectively, revealed markedly different morphologies. The $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film has a smooth and uniform surface, and the boundary between the PT film and the $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ thick $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer is distinctive (inset of Figure 3A). The uniform PT film is in tight contact with the underlying compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer, suggesting that the thin and compact $\mathrm { \bar { T i O } } _ { 2 }$ layer with a small dye coverage is favorable to the orderly growth of the PT film.

However, the PT flm has a rougher morphology and contains larger and more irregular granules when its thickness exceeds $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ (e.g., $4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ shown in Figure 3B). The irregular and rough surface of the $4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film, shown in the crosssectional view, indicates that the PT film had grown disorderly. To gather further evidence of the ordered PT growth along the tethered dye molecules, we scraped small pieces of the PT/ dye/ $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ off an FTO surface and analyzed them with TEM and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX). The TEM image indicates that a smooth PT film is tightly connected to the $\mathrm { \bar { T i O } } _ { 2 }$ layer, and the EDX mapping clearly shows that the dye molecules (containing N atoms; Figure 3D) are evenly scattered across the entire $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface. These results are consistent with the sub-monolayer dye coverage deduced from the absorbance measurement. In contrast, the PT film (containing S atoms; Figure 3E) is much denser. Thus, the scattered and tethered dye molecules initiated the photochemical polymerization, and the PT flm growth is ordered and uniform. In addition, we investigated the effect of surface coverage of dye C207 at $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { F T O }$ substrates on the quality of PT flms. Compared with the PT film formed with $1 0 \%$ surface coverage of dye C207 (cf. Figure 3A), films with higher and lower coverages have rougher morphology and agglomerated particles (Figure S4). At coverages less than $1 0 \%$ $\pi - \pi$ stacking between the dye molecules and thiophene monomers is insufficient, which leads to a disordered PT film. At coverages higher than $1 0 \%$ , the growth of PT flm becomes accelerated, which leads to agglomeration of PT particles. These results indicate that the dye surface coverage plays an important role in growing orderly PT flms. Notice in Figure 3F that the PT absorbance increases with the film thickness. The X-ray diffraction patterns of dye/ $\mathrm { \Delta T i O _ { 2 } / F T O }$ substrates covered with PT films of different thicknesses are shown in Figure S5. The broad peak around $2 \theta$ of $2 0 { - } 2 4 ^ { \circ }$ is characteristic of amorphous PT, which decreases with the PT film thickness and is eventually overwhelmed by the diffraction peaks of anatase $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and FTO substrate.

Note that $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ increases with the PT film thickness from 150 to $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . This is expected as thicker photoactive layers absorb more light (cf. Figure 3F). However, all of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } ,$ $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ and FF began to decrease beyond $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure $4 \mathrm { A } { - } \mathrm { C }$ and Table 1). This behavior can be rationalized by the fact that the PT film grown orderly along the tethered dye molecules facilitates unidirectional charge transport and effectively prevents the hole/electron recombination. However, the rougher morphology and disordered structure associated with PT films thicker than $2 7 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ (cf. Figure 3B and inset) hinder the charge transport and cause more rapid hole/electron recombination, causing degradation of the photovoltaic performance (green and purple curves in Figure 4D). As shown in Figure S6A, it is evident that the PT flm significantly improved the $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ due to increased light absorption and decreased recombination. Moreover, the PT flm produced photoelectrochemically at a constant potential of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { V }$ exhibits a $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 0 . 3 4 \pm 0 . 2 9 \mathrm { \ m A } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 7 8 \pm 0 . 0 1 \mathrm { V } _ { ; }$ and a FF of $0 . 6 7 \pm 0 . 0 1$ . The corresponding $\eta$ is $5 . 4 \pm 0 . 3 1 \%$ a decrease of $2 8 . 2 8 \%$ when compared to that of the film prepared with cyclic voltammetry. The cyclic voltammogram of dye C207 adsorbed on a $_ { \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { F T O } }$ substrate shows an oxidation peak at $0 . 8 0 \mathrm { V }$ and a reduction peak at $0 . 7 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ (cf. Figure S6B). This decrease is attributed to doping of the PT films by cations from the electrolyte solution. The effect of dye surface coverage on the PTSC performance is shown in Figure S7. The $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } , \ V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ FF, and $\eta$ all increase with the surface coverage from 1.7 to $1 0 \%$ , then decrease beyond $10 \%$ . This result is consistent with the quality of PT films formed with different surface coverages in Figure S4. Hence, $1 0 \%$ is the optimal surface coverage of dye for PTSCs.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the second step of the light-to-energy conversion in PTsCs is the diffusion of electrons from the photoactive film to the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer. The electron-hole recombination within PT film is an undesired path, whereas the transfer through the external circuit to the electron-collecting electrode is desired.37 The recombination process is the only path under the open-circuit condition.37 $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ decays exponentially with time right after termination of irradiation, as shown in Figure 5A. The derivatives of the decay curves are an indirect way of measuring the electron lifetimes $\tau _ { n }$ (inset of Figure 5A).37,50,s1 The reverse logarithm linear dependence suggests that the recombination process is chargetransfer controlled, as it follows the Butler-Volmer equation.37,50 We found that $\tau _ { n }$ decreases in the order of $2 7 0 < 2 1 0$ $< 1 5 0 < 3 2 0 < 4 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m } .$ The result is in good agreement with the $J { - } V$ curves and FF values (Figure 4A-C), indicating that the hole/electron recombination increases as the PT flm becomes rougher and less ordered.

Photoluminescence (PL) emission is a useful tool for probing the electron-transfer and electron-hole recombination behaviors.2-54 The trend revealed by Figure 5B is also consistent with that shown in Figure 5A. Compared with the film directly coated onto FTO, the PL intensity of a $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film on dye/ $\mathrm { \Delta T i O _ { 2 } / F T O }$ is quenched by about $9 0 \%$ indicating that the hole/electron recombination has been largely suppressed (Figure S8). To further investigate the influence of the PT film thickness on the PTSCs performance, we performed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (cf. Figure S9 for the Nyquist plots of PT films with different thicknesses).55,56 The electron-transfer resistance decreased inversely with PT films between 150 and $2 7 0 \mathrm { n m }$ but increased when the film thickness exceeded $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ Thus, a thin and ordered PT flm improves the charge transfer, whereas a PT film thicker than $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ retards the charge transfer. On the basis of these data, we believe that it is possible to produce a thicker and more orderly PT film with the use of a dye containing a longer chain of multiple aromatic moieties.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

Controlled photoelectrochemical growth of ordered PT thin films was achieved at compact $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layers partially covered with tethered dye molecules. The resultant layered structure was used as the cathode of the PTSC, with PT acting as the predominant photoactive material. A PTSC constructed with a $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick PT film has a conversion efficiency of $7 . 5 2 \pm$ $0 . 5 8 \%$ which is the highest among solar cells based solely on PT. The ordered and uniform PT film ensures unidirectional charge transport and efficient hole/electron separation. Our PTSC is simple in design and can be constructed with stable and easily processable materials.
