ABstrAct: Tailor-made polymers containing specific chemical functionalities have ushered in a number of emerging fields in polymer science. In most of these next-generation applications the focus of the community has centered upon closed-shell macromolecules. Conversely, macromolecules containing stable radical sites have been less studied despite the promise of this evolving class of polymers. In particular, radical-containing macromolecules have shown great potential in magnetic, energy storage, and biomedical applications. Here, the progress regarding the syntheses of open-shell containing polymers are reviewed in two distinct subclasses. In the first, the syntheses of radical polymers (i.e., materials composed of nonconjugated macromolecular backbones and with open-shell units present on the polymer pendant sites) are described. In the second, polyradical (i.e., macromolecules containing stabilized radical sites either within the macromolecular backbone or those containing radical sites that are stabilized through a large degree of conjugation) synthetic schemes are presented. Thus, the state-of-the-art in open-shell macromolecular syntheses will be reported and future means by which to advance the current archetype will be discussed. INTroDuctIOn Macromolecules with tailored optoelectronic, biological, and stimuli-responsive chemical functionalities have emerged as some of the most promising materials in polymer science over the past 30 years.1-10 During this time, remarkable strides have been made in the synthetic methods used to produce these high-performance materials; however, the majority of the work has centered on the idea of using closed-shell polymers as the means by which to introduce functionality to the systems of interest (e.g., the introduction of large degrees of $\pi$ conjugation to the macromolecular backbone of semiconducting polymers in organic electronic applications).11-17 Therefore, this approach has been oftdevoid of macromolecules that bear stable radical entities either within the macromolecular backbone or along the pendant groups of functional polymers. This is understandable, as the stability of open-shell units is often of great concern, and common polymerization schemes (i.e., those containing a radical intermediate) are often not compatible with the presence of these open-shell functionalities. However, the existence of stable, lone-electron sites within functional macromolecules offers exciting handles by which to tailor specific magnetic,18,19 oxidation-reduction (redox),20,21 electronic,22 and biological responses23 into these nextgeneration soft materials. Consequently, elucidating the exact manner by which to theoretically design and practically implement radical sites into open-shell-containing polymers provides a clear means by which to advance a relatively unexplored synthetic opportunity in polymer science and engineering. Along these lines, we aim to review the current state-of-the-art in order to provide a methodology by which to extend the synthetic space available to radical-containing macromolecules, compare and contrast the current synthetic techniques available to those that are well-known for closedshell polymers, and highlight the potential for open-shell macromolecules and hybrid closed-shell-open-shell polymers in advanced soft materials enterprises.

While many categories could exist, we describe two general classes of macromolecules that contain stable open-shell sites within the materials: (1) radical polymers and (2) polyradicals. Here, we define radical polymers as macromolecules composed of non-conjugated, usually aliphatic polymer backbones and with pendant groups that contain stable radical sites. In contrast, polyradicals, in the context of this work, are defined to be macromolecules where the open-shell entity either resides directly on the macromolecular backbone or is heavily tied to the macromolecular structure through a large degree of conjugation. Note that in these scenarios the radical sites on the polymer backbone are stable and relatively long-lived in nature,4,25 which is opposed to the intermediate or relatively short-lived radical sites commonly associated with charge transfer sites in the organic electronics literature.26-28 Therefore, great care must be implemented in designing these materials and in generating synthetic schemes that allow for a stabilized open-shell site to exist through (or at the end of) the synthetic process. # COMMON STABLE OPEN-SHELL FUNCTIONALITIES USED IN RADICAL POLYMERS AND POLYRADICALS

While it is the focus of this article to highlight the synthesis of macromolecules that contain stable, unpaired electrons, small molecule organic radical species, in general, are wellstudied.37,38 Indeed, much of the work surrounding radical polymers and polyradicals builds from known, small molecule radicals. Thus, specific details of the chemistry that governs stable organic radicals is well cataloged elsewhere.39 Here, a brief overview will be provided of some of the most common radical species incorporated into radical polymer and polyradicals (Fig. 1). For instance, the nitroxide radical moiety is known for its robust stability. As such, it has been incorporated into many different chemical structures, including that of: (1) 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMP0)40, (2) 2,2,5,5-tetramethyl-1-pyrrolidinyloxy (PRoxyL)41; and (3) $N$ tert-butyl $. N$ -oxy-aminobenzene.42 Oxygen atoms bound to aromatic systems also maintain persistent unpaired electrons. Well known examples of this class of materials are the phenoxyl43 and galvinoxyl44 open-shell species. While less stable, purely hydrocarbon ensembles have been shown to maintain open shell electrons as well. Typically, these structures utilize combinations of carbon centered radicals covalently bound to multiple aromatic rings, the simplest example being the triarylmethyl radical.45 Radical species based on heteroatom-stabilized open-shell groups, including tetrahydro-s-tetrazin-1-(2H)-yl (verdazyl),46Blatter,47 and dithiadiazolyl48 radical compounds also have been evaluated in small molecule systems; currently, however, their incorporation into macromolecular systems has been limited. Ultimately, the synthesis and efficient application of radical bearing soft materials depends on the coupling of radical compounds with compatible polymeric backbones, and while the wealth of available information pertaining to stable radical compounds can serve as a guide, the incorporation and manipulation of radical groups within polymeric systems continues to prove non-trivial. Therefore, defining what tactics have been successful in previous efforts will aid in guiding the future design of these functional materials as well.

# RADICAL POLYMERS

Contrary to polyradicals, where unpaired electrons are most often delocalized across conjugated molecules, radical polymers contain stable, localized open-shell moieties that reside on the pendant groups of (typically) aliphatic and nonconjugated polymer backbones.2,2249 These pendant radical sites are capable of undergoing reversible, localized oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions, and the redox reactions allow for the transfer of an electron between adjacent radical sites (Fig. 2). This redox activity, along with the stability of the open-shell entity, allows for radical polymers to be implemented into myriad applications.2249 Careful tuning of the chemical structure of the open-shell entity dictates whether the site is more amenable to the oxidation or reduction reaction; this, in turn, impacts the final device application of the radical polymers. For instance, macromolecules that preferentially undergo oxidation (i.e., the analog to ptype materials in the conjugated polymer organic electronics realm), typically incorporate a nitroxide group within the pendant groups of the radical polymers. In fact, one of the most oft-studied systems is one that bares a 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl-like (TEMPO-like) chemical nature. Again, as with many conjugated polymer systems, the majority of stable radical groups described in the literature are those that are preferentially oxidized. While less common, materials that preferentially undergo reduction (e.g., the galvinoxyl radical) are present, and a similar analogy to common ntype organic electronic materials can be drawn.

Most commonly, these materials are utilized as the electrodes in electrolyte-supported rechargeable batteries.20,21 For example, a p- type radical polymer containing the TEMPO moiety can serve as the cathode in a battery. During the charging cycle, the radical will undergo oxidation and form the oxoammonium cation; during discharge, the cation will be reduced to regenerate the stable radical species $^ { 5 0 } \mathrm { { I n } }$ addition to battery applications, radical polymers have been shown to be electrically conductive in the solid state (i.e., in the absence of a supporting electrolyte). It is postulated that, in the presence of an electric field, adjacent radical sites will undergo electron transfer with one another if there is a sufficiently high density of radical sites present within the film to allow for electrochemical reaction to propagate from one electrode to the other51-53 This mechanism has allowed radical polymers to be implemented in non-volatile memory devices,54 diodes,51 and as an interfacial modifying layer in solar cells.55 It is critical to note that these electronic applications are only possible due to the open-shell moiety, and the chemistry of the radical unit plays a crucial role in the transport and stability properties of the materials.56 However, the chemical identities of the macromolecular backbones upon which the radical sites reside also play roles of great import with respect to the ultimate device performance. In the instance of electrolytesupported systems, controlling the solubility of the polymer is imperative for batteries, as dissolution of the radical polymer into the electrolyte results in self-discharge and drastically diminishes the battery capacitance and lifetime.22,9 Additionally, radical proximity within a polymer film has been shown to be crucial for solid state conductivity.57 Finally, stereochemistry has been shown to affect radical activity in radical polymers as well.58,59 Thus, altering the chemical structure of the backbone can influence both radical site density along the chain and the morphology of the bulk film. As such, careful tuning of both the radical moiety and polymer backbone is essential to optimizing the performance of radical polymer based devices. Along these lines, we describe the current state-of-the-art with respect to the design of radical polymer backbones and open-shell pendant functionalities. Notably, these two portions of the radical polymer chains usually can be synthesized in a decoupled manner due to the modular nature associated with the backbone and pendant groups in non-conjugated macromolecular materials. Therefore, a rather large degree of synthetic flexibility is afforded to this emerging class of open-shell polymers.

Ideally, a target radical species could be bound to a wellstudied monomer, and the polymerization would proceed in a controlled manner. However, commonly-implemented free and controlled radical polymerization schemes for radical-bearing monomers do not yield radical polymers, as the open-shell groups within the monomer readily terminate the polymerization. For this reason, polymerization mechanisms for radical polymers can be broken into two categories: (1) radical polymerization of closed-shell, protected monomers and subsequent chemical modification to an open-shell species and (2) ionic and transition metal-catalyzed polymerization of the radical monomers. Additionally, radical moieties have been attached to pre-synthesized polymers, affording radical functionality to exotic polymer architectures unobtainable to conventional polymerizations schemes. By describing the recent advances in these separate techniques, we will highlight the advantages and drawbacks of each synthetic pathway.

Closed-Shell Polymerization Followed by Functionalization Schemes to Generate Radical Polymers While a large number of combinations of open-shell pendant groups and polymer backbones have been reported, poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy-4-yl methacrylate) (PTMA) has served as a model radical polymer for a majority of these efforts. As such, there is a wealth of polymerization schemes available for the synthesis of PTMA. In the most straightforward synthetic scheme, free radical polymerization of the closed-shell monomer 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-4- yl methacrylate (TMPM) occurs through the introduction of free radicals from an azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) thermal initiation process; in turn, this yields the radical polymer precursor, poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-4-yl methacrylate) (PTMPM).50,60-65 The PTMPM is then chemicallyoxidized to form the open-shell PTMA macromolecule. The two most common oxidizing agents are meta-chloroperbenzoic acid (mCPBA) and hydrogen peroxide in the presence of catalytic ${ \mathrm { N a W O } } _ { 4 } ,$ but this oxidation reaction is not simple in nature. Specifically, the exact reaction conditions for this oxidative functionalization can greatly affect both the number of sites oxidized as well as the oxidation state of the functionalized sites. For example, our team has shown that functionalized radical sites in PTMA are readily oxidized beyond the stable nitroxide species to form the oxoammonium cation along the polymer chain (with a balancing chlorinated anion from the mCPBA residual in the polymer powder).66 This greatly impacts the final end-use properties of the PTMA polymer. Thus, careful control over the oxidation reaction is crucial to optimizing the radical content of the final polymer.

A nearly identical free radical polymerization scheme has been implemented to synthesize an acrylamide analog to PTMA.67 Here, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-4-yl acrylamide (TPA) was polymerized using AIBN as the initiating agent and the protected polymer was subsequently oxidized using the mCPBA reagent. The acrylamide backbone was specifically chosen for its hydrophilic properties, making it suitable for use in water-based batteries. Moreover, although outwardly simple, free radical polymerization has also been implemented to achieve even more exotic polymer architectures. In one such example, poly(4-allenyloxyl-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl) (PATMPO) was prepared via the free radical polymerization of a non-functional allenyl precursor (Scheme 1).68 In this case, the tetramethyl piperidine moiety was bound to a propargyl bromide to form propargyloxy2,2,6,6-tetramethyl piperidine. The propargyl functionality was then converted to an allenyl chemistry by reacting the species with potassium tert-butoxide. After this reaction, the allenyl group was polymerized using benzoyl peroxide as the initiator. However, the adjacent $\pi$ -bonds in the allenyl act as competing sites for polymerization. Radical attacks across the 1,2-unsaturated bond yielded a different repeat unit than radical attacks across the 1,3-unsaturated bond. Ultimately, this competitive reaction causes variations of the backbone architecture from repeat unit to repeat unit, but the piperidine that was present after the polymerization was oxidized by $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0 _ { 2 }$ on ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { W } } 0 _ { 4 }$ to form the final radical polymer species. While this synthesis represents a unique application of free radical polymerization, even the most complex free radical polymerization schemes are ultimately limited due to the lack of regulation inherent to the uncontrolled nature of free radical polymerization; while these schemes can access a wide range of molecular weights (e.g. $3 5 \textrm { - } 9 0 ~ \mathrm { k D a }$ J, typically the dispersity of the final polymer exceeds 3.0. In order to synthesize more complex polymer structures, different polymerization techniques have been employed, namely controlled radical polymerizations.

A number of controlled radical polymerization schemes have been shown for the synthesis of PTMA, specifically reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization69-71 and atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP).72,73 These synthetic approaches allow for better control over the molecular weight and dispersity of the polymers.74,75 Furthermore, these reactions schemes provide access to additional macromolecular structures (e.g, block polymers); however, some care must be taken in order to ensure that unanticipated reactions do not occur between the open-shell units and the chemical entities that are present (beyond the radical source) in order to ensure the controlled nature of the polymerization. When proper precautions are taken, both ATRP and RAFT polymerization schemes have been shown to achieve molecular weights from 10 to $1 8 ~ \mathrm { k D a }$ while maintaining dispersity values below 1.3.66,76,77 As both techniques are well established in the closed-shell polymer field, the exact polymerization mechanisms are excellently described elsewhere in literature,69-75,78,79 and this effort will focus on the points of these synthetic schemes that have unique aspects due to the open-shell nature of the final radical polymer structure. For instance, the RAFT-mediated polymerization of PTMA, requires a three step reaction scheme to be successful.51,66,80 In this reaction procedure, the TMPM monomer is polymerized in the presence of 2-phenyl-2-propyl benzodithioate, resulting in PTMPM with a chain transfer agent terminus (PTMPM-RAFT). This specific chain transfer agent, 2-phenyl2-propyl benzodithioate, was selected as it is specifically tailored for polymerization of methacrylates, and it also contained chemical functionalities that were not impacted during the entirety of the PTMA generation process. Second, the chain transfer agent terminus is cleaved from the polymer end by reacting the PTMPM-RAFT with excess AIBN.81

Finally, the polymer is oxidized to PTMA through the introduction of mCPBA (Scheme 2). The removal of the RAFT terminus is necessary prior to oxidation, as the direct oxidation of PTMPM-RAFT resulted in an insoluble product, presumably due to side reactions associated with the dithioate bonds of the chain transfer agent. RAFT polymerization also has been used to functionalize the surface of silica spheres with PTMA brushes.82 In these reactions, the chain transfer agent, S-methoxycarbonyl-phenyl-methyl $S ^ { \prime }$ -trimethoxysilylpropyltrithiocarbonate, was attached to the surface of the silica particles using typical silanization reaction conditions. Then the TMPM monomer was polymerized from the surface of the particles and subsequently oxidized with mCPBA. Interestingly, the report indicated that steric restrictions prevented the mCPBA from penetrating the PTMPM brush; thus, only the outer surface of the functional polymer brush was oxidized to the actual radical polymer moiety. In order to circumvent this issue, PTMPM was polymerized in solution (i.e., not from a chain transfer agent grafted to the surface of the nanoparticles) using a different chain transfer agent that had a coupling functionality included within its chemical structure, and then the PTMPM was oxidized to form PTMA. The active chain transfer terminus was then used to anchor the polymer chains to the surface of the particles in a grafting to-type of manner, and this strategy resulted in significantly higher radical densities present for the open-shell macromolecule grafted to the silica particles relative to the initial grafting from technique.

In a nearly analogous manner, surface-initiated ATRP (SIATRP) has allowed for the creation of functionalized surfaces with radical polymer brushes. For instance, the ATRP initiator 4-(trichlorosilyl)butyl 2-bromo-2-methylpropanate was attached to the surface of silica nanoparticles.83 TMPM was then polymerized from the surface in the presence of CuBr and $N , N , N ; N ^ { \prime \prime } , N ^ { \prime \prime }$ -pentamethyldiethyltriamine (PMDETA) to form PTMPM brushes on the nanoparticle surfaces. These brushes were then oxidized with $m { \mathrm { C P B A } }$ to form the final, surface-grafted PTMA chains. Interestingly, the aforementioned sterically limited oxidation was not reported here. This is possibly due to a lower brush density, but could have also occurred due to the differences in chemistry between the two polymerization reactions and the side reactions that are present due to chain transfer agents that are present in the RAFT-mediated mechanisms and are not present in the ATRP schemes. Furthermore, this same polymerization technique was used to grow PTMA brushes from tin-doped indium oxide (ITO) substrates.84 Additionally, patterning of the ITO and then selectively depositing the surface initiator on the portions of the substrate with exposed ITO ultimately allowed for patterned PTMA brushes.85

By growing patterned PTMA brushes directly from the surface of a conducting material, it was feasible to envision micron scale batteries with excellent charge storage and long lifetimes, as the covalently bound polymer will not dissolve into the electrolyte. However, surface initiated, controlled radical polymerization schemes are not limited to functionalizing inorganic materials. ATRP initiating groups can also be attached to tailored monomers. After these monomers are polymerized, the initiator groups can be used as handles to impart further functionality into the macromolecule. As such, ATRP also has been used to functionalize other polymers in order to generate radical polymer-based graft copolymers. In one study, ATRP-initiating species were added to the 3- position of thiophene rings.86 The thiophene monomers were oxidatively polymerized through the addition of $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 }$ to yield a polythiophene with an ATRP initiator bound on each repeat unit. PTMPM brushes were then polymerized from the initiators on the 3-position of the polythiophene repeat units, and these were subsequently oxidized to PTMA in order to form the polythiophene. $_ g$ -poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethyl piperidinyloxy-4-yl methacrylate) (PT $_ g$ -PTMA) macromolecules. Similarly, initiator-functionalized monomers have been developed such that PTMA brushes can be grafted from the walls of a 3-dimensionally ordered, porous polypyrrole inverse opal.87 In these impressive efforts, polypyrrole was electropolymerized throughout the void spaces of a polystyrene (PS) opal template. The PS was then selectively dissolved in tetrahydrofuran, resulting in a polypyrrole inverse opal. Upon removal of the PS, a surface initiatorfunctionalized pyrrole monomer, $N$ (3-(1 $H$ -pyrrol-1- yl)propyl)-2-bromo-2-methylpropanamide, was polymerized onto the structured polypyrrole template, resulting in a thin layer of initiator functionalized pyrrole lining the pore walls. PTMPM brushes were then polymerized from this functionalized monomer layer and oxidized to PTMA in the next reaction step (Fig. 3). In turn, the high surface area of the porous structure coupled with the PTMA brushes greatly improved the energy capacity, power density, and cycle-life performance of batteries relative to planar electrodes. This example highlights the synthetic flexibility associated with the radical polymer motifs, how these open-shell species can be combined with closed-shell conjugated polymers, and how these interactions can lead to synergistic effects in practical applications. In fact, these advantages of radical polymers can be extended to a number of other systems capable of self-assembling into ordered nanostructures as well.

One recent example of this type of behavior is the development of radical polymer-containing diblock copolymers. Specifically, ATRP has been effectively utilized to create PTMAbased block copolymers. For instance, poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy-4-yl methacrylate)- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -polystyrene (PTMA$b$ -PS) diblock copolymers have been synthesized. These systems were found to phase separate into several different configurations depending on the solvent environment and annealing conditions implemented.8-90 In these synthetic schemes, 4-toluenesulfonyl chloride was used as the initiator to polymerize the TMPM monomer. Then styrene was polymerized from the chlorine terminated PTMPM. The final PTMA $b$ -PS diblock copolymer was then formed by oxidizing the PTMPM- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -PS diblock copolymer with mCPBA. When dissolved in a battery electrolyte solution, the polymers formed micelles, with insoluble PS cores and soluble PTMA coronas.89 When cast onto a surface, these same polymer systems assumed cylindrical morphologies that were either perpendicular or parallel to the substrate, depending on the solvents used during the annealing process.9 Similar microphase separation behavior has been observed in other systems. For instance, poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy-4- yl  methacrylate) $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -poly(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate) (PTMA- $b$ -P3FEMA) diblock copolymers synthesized via sequential atom transfer radical polymerization of TMPM and 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate followed by the oxidation of the PTMPM moiety to PTMA produced nanostructured thin films when cast from solution (Fig. 4).91 In particular, the PTMA- $b$ -P3FEMA diblock copolymers demonstrated relatively well-ordered structures upon either thermal or solvent annealing. For the targeted compositions used in this initial effort, cylindrical nanostructures (where the long axis of the cylinders were running perpendicular to the substrate) were used. This allowed for easy visualization of the self-assembled thin films in the atomic force microscope (AFM). Although less frequently implemented, block polymers containing a radical species have been prepared via RAFT polymerization. Both a poly(ethylene oxide) $b$ -poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl-4-yl acrylamide) (PEO- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -PTAm) diblock copolymer as well as a poly(ethylene oxide)- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -polylactide-b-poly(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl-4-yl acrylamide) (PEO $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -PLA-b-PTAm) triblock polymer have been prepared by polymerizing 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-4-yl acrylamide (TPA) from a chain transfer agent terminated PEO and PEO- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ PLA, respectively.93 In both cases, the PTPA block was subsequently oxidized to form the open-shell-functionalized PTAm block. Similar to the PTMA $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -PS diblock copolymer system, both the PTAm-containing diblock polymer and triblock polymer formed self-assembled micelles when the block polymers were dissolved in water. However, in these two cases, the water soluble PEG and PEG- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \boldsymbol { b }$ -PLA domains formed the corona, and the radical PTAm formed the insoluble core. Therefore, the ability to tune the syntheses of radical polymer-containing block polymers offers exciting opportunities in both solid-state and solution-based applications.

The radical polymer syntheses discussed to this point have dealt exclusively with TEMPO-like radical groups; however, several other open-shell functionalities are accessible for radical polymer systems. For example, several studies have covalently-bound nitroxide radicals to phenyl rings (Scheme 3).94,95 In one of these studies, pentafluorostyrene was treated with excess tert-butylamine to form $N$ (tertbutyl)-2,3,5,6-tetrafluoro-4-vinylaniline (NTTV).

After radical polymerization of the NTTV monomer and oxidation of the closed-shell species to an open-shell species using ${ \mathrm { P b } } 0 _ { 2 } ,$ the resulting nitroxide radical-based polymer was formed; interestingly, it was found to be n-type in nature. Materials that preferentially undergo reduction are crucially important for the realization of entirely organic batteries% While the materials that preferentially undergo oxidation are well established as cathode materials, high performing n-type materials are necessary to serve as anodes. As such, expanding the number of n-type radical polymers is an important opportunity for the community.

Finally, all of the radical species discussed have been unipolar (i.e., containing a redox chemistry, i.e., either preferentiallyoxidized or preferentially-reduced). Conversely, a styrenic, nitonylnitroxide-bearing polymer was synthesized and used as an ambipolar redox active material.100 In this effort, terephthalaldehyde was reacted with methyltriphenylphosphonium bromide and potassium carbonate to form an aldehydefunctionalized styrene using Wittig reaction chemistry. The aldehyde group was leveraged to attach a 1,3-dihydroxy4,4,5,5-tetramethyl imidazolidine ring to the monomer. The hydroxyl groups were protected with tert-butyldimethylsilyl chloride, and the styrene base was then polymerized by AIBN.

Finally, the silyl protecting groups were removed with tetra-nbutylammonium fluoride, and the recovered hydroxyl groups are oxidized by manganese oxide. The resulting poly[4-(nitronylnitroxyl)styrene] was shown to have comparable oxidation and reduction potentials, lending to the ambipolar nature of the radical group. Further development of stable, ambipolar radical species stands as a critical development in the field of organic electronics, as similar attempts to synthesize ambipolar, closed shell polymer systems have been limited by instability associated with n-type behavior101

# Polymerization of Monomers Bearing Open-Shell Functionalities

In contrast to radical-mediated polymerization schemes, ionic polymerization schemes circumvent the issue of pendant group coupling of radical moieties. This allows for radical-bearing monomers to be polymerized directly (i.e., without having to undergo a closed-shell-to-open-shell postpolymerization conversion process). In addition to circumventing the need for post polymerization functionalization, ionic polymerization schemes display improved control, even when compared to RAFT and ATRP. Anionic polymerizations are particularly precise, capable of achieving molecular weights from 4 to $1 6 ~ \mathrm { \ k D a }$ while maintaining a dispersity below 1.1.102 As with the controlled radical polymerization techniques, the specific mechanisms for both anionic polymerizations and cationic polymerizations have been described in detail in previous reports.103-105 Therefore, we will focus on key concepts regarding the syntheses of the radical bearing monomers and the potential issues associated with polymerizing open-shell-containing monomers.

In an initial example of ionic polymerization techniques, the anionic polymerization of PTMA from 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl-4yl methacrylate (TEMPO-M) occurred using both phenylmagnesium  bromide54,106,107 and methyl methacrylate-capped 1,1-diphenylhexyllithium102 as initiating species. In the latter case, the methyl methacrylate was added to 1,1-diphenylhexyllithium before polymerizing TEMPO-M in order to control the reactivity of the propagating anion and prevent side reactions with the open-shell electron. Anionic polymerization also has been used to synthesize radical bearing polyethers by facilitating ring opening polymerizations of ethylene oxides.108-110 As an example, 4- hydroxy TEMPO undergoes an $S _ { \mathrm { N } } 2$ reaction with epichlorohydrin to form 4-glycidyloxy-TEMPO. The opening of the monomer ring is then initiated by potassium tert-butoxide. Interestingly, while ethylene oxide-based monomers readily polymerized, oxetane-based analogs could not be polymerized in a similar manner.109 This was attributed to insufficient ring strain within the four-membered oxetane ring relative to the three-membered epoxide ring. Cationic polymerizations have also been implemented to synthesize hydrophilic radical polymers, most notably, poly(TEMPO vinyl ether) (PTVE).11-115 In these syntheses, 4-hydroxyTEMPO was reacted with vinyl acetate and catalytic bis(1,5- cyclooctadiene)diiridium(l) dichloride $( [ \mathrm { I r C l } ( \mathrm { c o d } ) ] _ { 2 } )$ to for TEMPO vinyl ether. Cationic polymerization was then initiated using boron trifluoride diethyl etherate $\left( \mathrm { B F } _ { 3 } { \cdot } \mathrm { E t } _ { 2 } 0 \right)$ Notably, both ionic polymerization mechanisms allow ready access to hydrophilic polymer backbones, which in turn, allows for the development of aqueous and more environmentally benign batteries.116

Ring opening metathesis polymerization (ROmP) mediated by the ruthenium-based Grubbs catalyst117 provides another versatile route for direct polymerization of radical bearing monomers, as well as showing similar control to controlled radical polymerization schemes (i.e. molecular weights from 8 to $6 5 ~ \mathrm { k D a }$ with dispersity values below 1.3).99,118,119 Norbornene and norbornene derivatives are popular precursors for this technique, as each repeat unit has the ability to bear two open-shell species. For instance, the well-studied poly[2,3-bis] $( 2 ^ { \prime } , 2 ^ { \prime } , 6 ^ { \prime } , 6 ^ { \prime }$ -tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl- $\cdot 4 ^ { \prime }$ -oxycarbonyl)-5-norbornene] (PTNB) is synthesized by attaching two 4-hydroxy TEMPO molecules to 5-norbornene-2,3- dicarboxylic anhydride via esterification, and then utilizing these monomers in a Grubbs catalyst-mediated ROMP reaction (Scheme 4).107,120,121 Other researchers have successfully incorporated different radical functionalities onto the norbornene ring, including 4-amino TEMPo,122 2,2,5,5-tetramethyl-1-pyrrolidinyloxy (PRoxyL) radicals,123 nitonylnitroxide radicals,118 and phenoxyl radicals.99 Additionally, selectively incorporating cross-linking agents or solubilizing moieties onto the norbornene monomer instead of radical groups has been shown to allow for controllable solubility within the final polymer.12-126 One particularly interesting use of this highly tunable functionality was the development of a simultaneous MRI and fluorescence imaging contrast agent.126 Here, two uniquely-functionalized norbornene monomers were copolymerized. Both monomers contained PEO functionalities for improved polymer solubility, but one contained a spirocyclohexyl nitroxide as an MRI contrast agent and the other carried a cationic cyanine dye (Cy5.5) as a fluorescence contrast agent. The resulting bottle-brush polymer provided a dual-modality organic radical contrast agent.

Clearly, ROmP of functionalized norbornene units provides a versatile platform for designing radical polymers. However, a study examining the arrangement of the norbornene functional groups found that functional group configuration (i.e., exo-, endo-) could drastically affect the solubility of the polymer, placing a practical limit on the monomer design.127 As such, care must be taken when designing these materials for specific applications, especially if high-throughput solventbased processing is desired. In addition to allowing for customization of the monomer, ROMP can also be used to synthesize surface grafted polymers and diblock polymers. For instance, PTNB chains can be polymerized from single norbornene rings that have been covalently bound to a carbon nanotube.119 Additionally, ROMP has been used to synthesize diblock polymers of PTNB and poly(5-norbornene-2-yl carboxyethyl-3-ethylimidazolium  hexafluorophosphate)by successively polymerizing the respective monomers.128 While these examples demonstrate the remarkable synthetic flexibility of the norbornene architecture coupled with ringopening metathesis polymerization, these schemes are slightly limited by the necessity of cyclic monomers. In order to access additional backbone structures, other transition metal catalyzed polymerization schemes have also been implemented in the synthesis of radical polymers.

In a manner similar to ring-opening metathesis polymerizations mediated by Grubbs catalyst, radical polymers have also been synthesized via polymerization of functionalized acetylene monomers using rhodium-based catalysts. Polyacetylene backbones bearing PRoxyL radicals,129 TEMPO radicals,121,123 and even complex moieties containing multiple nitroxide radicals per monomer130,131 have been synthesized by this method. As an example, to synthesize the functionalized acetylene monomer $N$ -(4-carboxy-TEMPO) propargylamide, 4-carboxy TEMPo was activated with

$N$ methylmorpholine. Upon introduction of the propargylamine, the amine and activated carboxylic acid underwent a condensation reaction, forming the target monomer. The functionalized acetylene is then polymerized on a rhodium catalyst such as bis(norbornadiene)rhodium(I) tetraphenylborate $( \mathrm { { R h } ( \mathrm { { n b d } ) \mathrm { { B P h } _ { 4 } ) } } }$ . While some care must be taken in selecting an appropriate catalyst,132 acetylene based polymerizations are clearly a viable platform for the synthesis of novel radical polymers.

Aside from the reaction procedures discussed above, very few studies have reported the successful polymerizations of radical bearing monomers. The recent development of a group transfer polymerization (GTP) scheme has shown promise for directly polymerizing the radical-bearing TEMPO-M monomer to form PTMA.133 Here, TEMPO-M is polymerized in the presence of the group transfer agent 1- methoxy-2-methyl-1-trimethylsilyloxypropene and a catalytic amount of tetrabutylammonium fluoride. In a subsequent effort, the same polymerization scheme was also applied to monomer mixtures of TEMPO-M and methyl methacrylate.134 These reactions resulted in random copolymers, which were used to study radical interactions along the polymer backbone. Additionally, a radical bearing polymer network has been prepared via Michael addition of TEMPO acetoacetate (TEMPO-AcAc) and trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA).135 Here, TEMPO-AcAc was synthesized via a transesterification reaction between tert-butyl acetoacetate and 4- hydroxy TEMPO. The Michael addition was then promoted by the addition of 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene. The final macromolecule exhibited a unique backbone, consisting of ester and acetoacetate moieties which were artifacts from the Michael polyaddition.

# Attachment of Radical Groups to Pre-existing Polymers

Even with the diversity of synthetic techniques described above, there are still polymer architectures that are difficult or impossible to synthesize with radical moieties, functionalized or not, contained within them. However, many of these architectures exhibit beneficial properties (e.g., solubility, biocompatibility, and mechanical robustness). In order to exploit these systems as radical polymers, open-shell groups have been attached to pre-synthesized polymer structures. A simple example of this is the click reaction between acetylene functionalized TEMPO and azide functionalized poly(3- alkylthiopehene)s.136 Similar concepts have also been implemented to attach open-shell moieties to more exotic architectures including siloxane backbones,137 cellulose,138 and dendritic systems.3 Specifically, the attachment of TEMPO groups to siloxane backbones has been reported using platinum(0)-1,3-divinyl-1,1,3,3-tetramethyldisiloxane complex (Karstedt's catalyst) and poly(methyl, hydro siloxane) (PMHS).137 In an example synthesis, the TEMPO attaching group was synthesized by reacting 4-hydroxy TEMPO with NaH followed by allyl bromide. The resulting 4-allyl-2,2,6,6- tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl ether was then reacted with PMHS and Karstedt's catalyst to form poly[methyl(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxyl-4-oxypropyl)siloxane]. While  the final material displayed excellent mechanical properties, common to siloxane polymers, it was also insoluble. TEMPO and PROxyL radicals were added to ethyl cellulose via 4- dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) and (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide  (EDcl) based dehydration between carboxyl functionalized radical groups and hydroxyl groups on the cellulose. The functionalized cellulose was found to be soluble in common organic solvents and exhibited good charge-discharge capacity. This recent advance could pave the way towards easily recycled and environmentally-friendly batteries.

# POLYRADICALS

Typically, organic molecules display a singlet ground state resulting from conventional electron spin-pairing.139 Additionally, the energy gap between this singlet ground state and the nearest triplet state is several orders of magnitude greater than thermal energy at room temperature.140 However, significant amounts of research have been performed in order to synthesize organic molecules that reverse this paradigm. Such molecules display a triplet ground state and an energy gap separating the ground state and nearest singlet state $( \Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } } )$ that exceeds the available thermal energy at room temperature. These macromolecules (i.e., polyradicals) often exhibit magnetic properties, which makes them ideal candidates for studying magnetic phenomena, as their properties can be tuned with relative ease, especially when compared to the complex electronic structure associated with transition metals.141 Additionally, the high spin of these molecules can be used as a lens to examine how different chemical structures influence the electronic interactions and macroscopic properties of these materials.142 Finally, molecules that exhibit a triplet ground state, at a sufficiently high $\Delta \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ to remain in the triplet state at ambient conditions, and are stable at ambient conditions could find application in numerous applications including dynamic nuclear polarization techniques,143,144 organic magnets,18,19,25 and spintronics.145,146 The majority of the early work into polyradical materials focused on radicals centered on carbon atoms. Later, additional radical moieties were incorporated into polyradicals, namely nitroxide radicals and aminyl radicals. These new materials draw heavily upon the syntheses of carbon centered materials, but seek to address the aforementioned crucial parameters of ambient stability and increased $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ The following sections outline the various synthetic techniques used in the preparation of these molecules.

# Carbon Centered Radicals

Attaining ferromagnetic coupling between unpaired electrons is crucial to developing high-spin organic molecules.24 This is most commonly achieved by pairing two spin sites via a ferromagnetic coupling unit (FcU). Theoretical work proposes that this ferromagnetic coupling can be obtained through overlapping $\pi$ -orbitals between neighboring spin sites.147 The Schlenk hydrocarbon, first reported in 1915, is one of the earliest examples of this $\pi$ -orbital facilitated coupling.148 Here, two arylmethyl radicals are bound meta to each other across a phenyl ring, which serves as the FCU (Fig. 5). However, without sufficient steric hindrance, these phenyl rings are prone to twisting out of plane with each other, which can be problematic as molecular orientation is crucial to effective ferromagnetic coupling. In efforts to better control both the intermolecular conformations and intramolecular orientations in the solid state, Schlenk derivatives with strategically placed steric groups have been synthesized.149-151 As an example, 4,6-dibromo-1,3-disopropylbenzene was treated with butyllithium to facilitate a brominelithium exchange. The organolithium was then reacted with $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -di-tert-butylbenzophenone to form a tertiary alcohol.

![](images/a849133d2840399ec7a9168524b8c2cef77f425d90cfdde9a6ac01c5f3624a39.jpg)  
FIGURE 5 The Schlenk Hydrocarbon, first synthesized in 1915, represents one of the most famous organic diradicals. The 1,3 linkage across the center phenyl ring provides ferromagnetic coupling between the radical sites.

A second bromine-lithium exchange followed by another addition of $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -di-tert-butylbenzophenone resulted in a symmetric diol. The hydroxyl functionalities were then converted to methyl ethers via treatment with methanol and trifluoroacetic acid. Finally, the ethers were reduced to carbanions using lithium metal and subsequently oxidized by iodine to form the corresponding radical species. As predicted, the steric groups incorporated into the center phenyl ring influenced the magnetic coupling between radicals, with bulkier groups promoting ferromagnetic coupling.

The example synthesis presented above highlights a number of common themes in the synthesis of polyradicals based on phenyl linkages. First, substituted lithium is frequently used to facilitate bromine-lithium exchanges, and these reactions are followed by quenching the organolithium compound with a benzophenone. This is a popular and effective technique for linking phenyl systems. Additionally, the conversion of the resultant hydroxyl functionality to a methyl ether allows for the subsequent formation of stable open-shell species. Finally, the radical sites are commonly formed in two steps: (1) complete reduction to form carbanions followed by (2) oxidation to form the unpaired electrons.

Ideally, the ether moieties could be converted directly to the radical species; however, the reduction of the ether species proceeds sequentially (i.e., for a diradical precursor, one ether site will be reduced to the radical and then again to the anion before the second site is reduced to the radical).141 Additionally, anions sites within polyradicals have been found to act as antiferromagnetic coupling units.152 For this reason, the two step synthesis described above is most commonly employed. While seemingly rigid, this synthetic framework provides substantial flexibility for the design and evaluation of novel polyradicals. Most notably, the brominelithium exchange-reaction with benzophenone-conversion to ether process can be applied cyclically, allowing for the synthesis of higher order polyradicals.19,153-157

Typically, the synthesis of these larger order polyradicals follow a divergent path.157 Here, the initial steps for any synthesis are the same, but eventually, different linking agents are introduced (as opposed to $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -di-tert-butylbenzophenone) and the syntheses diverge to ultimately different molecules. As an example, the synthesis of a molecule containing 31 unpaired electrons began with a bromine-lithium exchange of 4,6-dibromo-1,3-dimethylbenzene by $n$ -butyllithium, followed by addition of a single $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -di-tert-butylbenzophenone. The hydroxyl functionality was then converted to a methyl ether by NaH and MeI. If three equivalents of this molecule then underwent bromine-lithium exchange by tertbutyllithium and were reacted with dimethyl carbonate, the resulting molecule ultimately became a tetraradical. However, if two equivalents of the same organolithium were instead reacted with methyl 5-bromo-2,4-dimethylbenzoate and then converted to the methyl ether by NaH and MeI, the resulting molecule was a triradical precursor that still contained a brominated phenyl ring. This bromine could then undergo bromine-lithium exchange, and two equivalents were reacted with methyl 5-bromo-2,4-dimethylbenzoate to form a heptaradical precursor with a brominated phenyl ring (Scheme 5).156 This process was repeated, subsequently forming a 15-radical precursor and a 31-radical precursor, or terminated at any step by reacting 1 equivalent of the radical precursor with methyl 4-tert-butylbenzoate.156 The formation of radicals from the methyl ether sites was then achieved as explained above. It should be noted that, although these molecules contain a high number of unpaired electrons, the bulk magnetism remains low, with the molecules bearing 31 unpaired spin sites achieving an average spin comparable to their heptaradical precursors $( S = 7 / 2 )$ This limited return was attributed to defects within the molecule that break the ferromagnetic coupling.156 For higher order polyradicals, there are more potential defects, and because each subsequent coupling links precursors of higher and higher spin, defects in later coupling reactions are increasingly detrimental. One proposed solution to this problem involves intermittently coupling large spin units with single spin sites via weak FCU, namely using calix[4]arene rings coupled via 3,4' biphenylene linkers.158,159

As opposed to the triaryl dendritic systems described above, which connect radical units in a linear fashion, calix rings form cyclic structures. In this way, a single defect within a calix ring will not catastrophically disrupt the ferromagnetic coupling, as there are multiple paths for coupling to occur. Additionally, as these rings utilize meta linkages between phenyl rings, they can be synthesized in a nearly identical method to the dendritic systems discussed above. In a representative synthesis (Scheme 6), 1,3 dibromobenzene was reacted $n$ -butyllithium to facilitate bromine-lithium exchange.160 The organolithium was then quenched with 3-bromo- $4 ^ { \prime }$ -tert-butylbenzophenone, and the alcohol was converted to a methyl ether by reacting with NaH followed by MeI. The resulting ether was then lithiated via tert-butyllithium and quenched with two equivalents of 1- (4-tert-butylbenzoyl)pyrrolidine to form a diketone. Ring closure was then facilitated by lithiating one equivalent of the methyl ether via tert-butyllithium and quenching with one equivalent of the diketone. Finally, the resulting ring structure was reduced to the corresponding tetraanion using a sodium/potassium alloy and oxidized by iodine to form the tetraradical. A nearly identical synthesis was also used to create the corresponding calix[3]arene triradical. However, severe out of plane twisting drastically reduced ferromagnetic coupling within the calix[3]arene system.160 while this effect was observed within the calix[4]arene system, it was to significantly lesser degree relative to the linear system such that sufficient coupling was preserved.

# Nitroxide Centered Radicals

Derivations of the nitroxide radical such as the 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMPO) radical are well known for their stability and persistence, even in ambient conditions.37

For this reason, the nitroxide group has become a popular spin center for incorporation into various polyradical designs.142 Many of the trends previously reported for carbon centered polyradicals have been re-investigated for systems that utilize nitroxide radicals. As with carbon centered polyradicals, the initial investigations focused on diradicals. For example, a diradical consisting of two $N$ -tert-butyl nitroxide radicals bound to the 1 and 3 positions of a benzene ring was found to be unstable, decomposing within a few hours.170 Additionally, interactions between water molecules and the polar nitroxide radicals were shown to induce changes in both molecular conformation and magnetic properties for unconstrained diradicals.171 To overcome these initial shortcomings, constrained or annelated structures were synthesized.172-176 As an example synthesis of a constrained nitroxide diradical, diethyl 4,6-diaminoisophthalate was reacted with two equivalents of methylmagnesium iodide to form 2,4-diisopropanol-1,5-diaminobenzene (Scheme 8).173 Ring closure was then facilitated by reacting this intermediate with two equivalents of acetone to form the annelated diamine. Finally, the nitroxide was formed by reacting the precursor material with the meta-chloroperbenzoic acid reagent (mCPBA). As was discussed previously with radical polymers, this final oxidation of the amine to the nitroxide radical is a crucial and often difficult step in the synthesis nitroxide polyradicals. Many studies have analyzed the effects of different oxidizing agents including mCPBA, dimethyldioxirane (DMDO) and benzoyl peroxide (BPO).174-176 One such study found that oxidation using BPO resulted in oxidative C-C and $_ { \mathrm { N - N } }$ coupling, ultimately yielding a non-functional conjoined helicene.175 As such, careful consideration and optimization of the oxidation step is crucial to an effective synthesis.

Again drawing inspiration from previous work on carbon centered polyradicals, the nitroxide functionality has also been incorporated into calix[4]arene architecture.77-181 However, the diatomic and polar nature of the nitroxide bond leads to additional coupling mechanisms not observed in carbon centered calix[4]arene systems. For example, both systems demonstrate through-bond ferromagnetic that results from coupling through the $\pi$ -bonds in the phenyl rings. However, nitroxide based macrocycles also exhibit through-space coupling, where nitroxide radicals extending into the annulus of the calix ring are in close enough proximity to influence each other.182 While this interaction is typically much weaker than through-bond coupling, it has been shown to affect the time scale of relaxation processes, crucial for the development of resonance contrast agents.

Additionally, both through-bond and through-space couplings, and consequently relaxation spectra, in nitroxide calix[4]arene systems have been shown to be dependent on the conformation of the calix ring.177,178,181 In one study, calix rings constrained to the alternating 1,3 geometry exhibited antiferromagnetic through-bond coupling, while the cone configuration exhibited ferromagnetic through-bond coupling.177 Both molecules also exhibited antiferromagnetic through-space coupling. The syntheses of these model molecules were relatively straightforward. That is, tetrabromocalix[4]arene rings sterically-constrained to the proper conformation were reacted with excess tert-butyllithium to facilitate bromine-lithium exchange at all four sites. The organolithium compound was then quenched with 2-methyl2-nitrosopropane dimer to form the corresponding tetrahydroxylamine. Finally, radical species were formed by oxidizing with the $\mathrm { A g } _ { 2 } 0$ reagent. A similar protocol was also employed to attach the nitronyl nitroxide to calix geometries.180 Here, the tetra-lithiated calix[4]arene was quenched with $N$ -methylformanilide to provide a tetra-aldehyde. The aldehyde moieties were then condensed with 2,3- bis(hydroxyamino)-2,3-dimethylbutane in the presence of para toluene sulfonic acid to provide the non-functional precursor, which was subsequently oxidized to form the radical using ${ \mathrm { N a I O } } _ { 4 }$ . Here, identical coupling trends to those seen with 1,3 alternating calix[4]arene nitroxide systems were observed for the nitronyl nitroxide radical, although the coupling was much weaker Additionally, $^ { 3 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ phenyl linkers have also been employed to attach nitroxide radicals to calix[4]arene rings.17 In an analogous reaction to those presented in the previous section, Negishi coupling was used to attach tert-butyldimethylsilyl ether protected hydroxyl amines to tetrabromocalix[4]arene rings. The protecting groups were cleaved with $n$ -butyl ammonium fluoride to produce hydroxylamines, which were oxidized to form radical by $\mathrm { A g C l } _ { 2 }$ . The introduction of the phenyl spacer ultimately allowed for finer tuning of the electronic relaxation time. The slower relaxation time, coupled with the aforementioned inherent stability of nitroxide radicals, marks these molecules as excellent candidates for magnetic contrast agents.

# Aminyl Centered Radicals

While the nitroxide spin center presents a radical moiety, that is, stable at ambient conditions, it inadequately addresses the need for materials with higher $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ values. This energy gap is crucial to the development of organic magnetic materials. As the thermal population of the singlet state at ambient conditions deteriorates, the magnetic properties and ultimate applicability of the organic materials are significantly weakened. One factor, that is, known to increase $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ is increased spin delocalization into the FCU of a polyradical.25,183 While nitroxide radicals display excellent stability, the associated spin is localized within the $_ { \mathrm { N - O } }$ bond. Contrarily, aminyl spin centers, where the unpaired electron lies directly on a nitrogen atom, delocalize their spin to a much greater extent. Unfortunately, high-spin molecules with large $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ also tend to be much less kinetically stable than those with lower $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } } { ^ { 1 4 2 } }$ Thus, while aminyl radicals display relatively high $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ continuing effort has been placed in order to increase the stability of these molecules such that they may be persistent at ambient conditions.184-189

The first example of an aminyl based diradical, the Platz diradical, was first reported in 1989.190 Here, the radicals were formed by photolysis of azide groups in 1,3-diazidobenzene and 2,4,6-trifluoro-1,3-diazidobenzene. These molecules were incredibly unstable, only persistent in solution at $- 1 9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ In order to improve the stability of such systems, a number of synthetic schemes were employed to stabilize and sterically shield the spin centers.184-186 In an example synthesis, diisopropenyl-1,3-dibromobenzene  and  4-tert-butylaniline were coupled by ${ \mathrm { P d } } ( 0 \operatorname { A c } ) _ { 2 }$ catalyzed CN coupling in the presence of sodium tert-butoxide (t-BuONa) and tri(tert-butyl) phosphate $\left( t { - } \mathrm { B u } _ { 3 } \mathrm { P } \right)$ 184 The resulting diamine was annelated by $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ via electrophilic aromatic substitution, ultimately forming a planarized diamine. From here, the diamine was brominated by $N$ -bromosuccinimide (NBs), and the bromine was then utilized in a Suzuki coupling191 reaction to attach additional steric groups.185,186 In one case, 4-tert-butylphenylboronic acid was bound by reacting with catalytic $\mathrm { P d } ( \mathrm { P P h } _ { 3 } ) _ { 4 }$ and sodium carbonate. Finally, the amine functionalities were converted to radicals by reducing with $n$ -butyllithium and then oxidizing to the radical by reacting with iodine. However, even with the annelated structure and increased steric hindrance, the resulting diradicals were still relatively unstable, decaying on the order of hours at room temperature. Recent theoretical work, however, has shed light onto possible sources of this instability.187 The computational study showed that fused aromatic rings can significantly decrease the stability of aminyl radicals. While the central phenyl ring within the aminyl diradical described above is essential to preserving ferromagnetic coupling, the most recent aminyl diradical syntheses have focused on creating annelated structures without the periphery phenyl rings.188189 In one such synthesis, 1,3-diaminobenzene was reacted with $\mathrm { N a N O } _ { 3 } , \mathrm { S n C l } _ { 2 }$ and methyl isopropyl ketone in a Fisher indole synthesis to provide an indolenine derivative (Scheme 9). A Grignard addition with allylmagnesium bromide added steric groups to the indolenine rings, and the unsaturated allyl groups were then reduced by $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ on $\mathrm { P d } / \mathrm { C }$ As seen previously, the phenyl ring was brominated by NBS, and Suzuki coupling was utilized to attach 4-tert-butylphenylboronic acid by reacting with catalytic $\mathrm { P d } ( \mathrm { P P h } _ { 3 } ) _ { 4 }$ . Finally, the amine moieties were reduced by $n$ -butyllithium and oxidized by $\mathrm { I } _ { 2 }$ to form the final diradical. The resulting aza-mxylylene aminyl diradical demonstrated large $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ but only marginally increased stability. Efforts to increase the stability of aminyl diradicals, or conversely the $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ of nitroxide diradicals, continue to push the cutting edge of organic magnetic materials.

# CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE OUTLOOK

Because of the excellent synthetic protocols that have been established for radical-bearing macromolecules, the future of open-shell polymers is very bright. As the field has matured, the synthetic creativity and flexibility associated with the organic species have grown, and, correspondingly, the number of applications in which radical-bearing macromolecules are used has increased. Therefore, the continued production and application of these materials will allow open-shell macromolecules to be used alongside of, and potentially in lieu of, their closed-shell polymer counterparts. However, it is clear that certain technological strides must be accomplished if radical polymers and polyradicals are to reach their true potential.

For instance, an increased level of computational and theoretical efforts into the realm of open-shell polymers would be of great utility. While current methods of synthesis that are based on chemical intuition and preliminary structureproperty relationships have helped build the current synthetic targets for open-shell macromolecules, having specific macromolecular architectures with predicted electronic and magnetic structures would provide a significant boost to the community. That is, computations that would predict the electronic band structure could aid radical polymer and polyradical efforts in much the same manner that has occurred for conjugated closed-shell conjugated polymer systems. Some very important efforts have determined how the proximity of radical sites to one another affect the charge transport relationships in radical polymers;21,2,25 however, very little work has been performed in order to elucidate how the rather sophisticated molecular architectures of radicalbearing species impact their dimensions in space. By predicting how various radical groups and backbone architectures impact chain configuration parameters (e.g, the Kuhn length), synthetic polymer chemists would have a better handle on how the end-use application properties of radicalbearing species would be impacted. Currently, very little work exists in this realm of computation and theory despite the clear launching points provided by the analogous work in closed-shell polymer systems. Therefore, having this feedback between experiment and computation could allow for the rapid development of structure-property-performance relationships in this developing class of materials.

Along these same lines of thinking, computational studies could predict currently-unexplored open-shell groups that are stable within polymeric architectures. To date, the number of radical chemistries that have been incorporated into macromolecular systems has been relatively limited compared to the functionality that has been seen in the closedshell polymer regime. Of course, much of this has to do with the relatively rare nature of stable radical sites. However, if significant experimental and computational efforts could uncover a larger suite of stable open-shell functionalities, significant progress could be had in a number of technologically-important application spaces. Additionally, the fact that the synthetic techniques described above are relatively robust with respect to the radical chemistry selected would make for a direct path for the inclusion of these new radical groups into known macromolecular systems a rather straightforward task. As such, generating these new stable radical chemistries is a very fertile ground for future synthetic polymer chemistry efforts.

These current molecular design challenges present great opportunities for significant advancements in the field of radical-bearing polymers, and, because of the synthetic flexibility associated with these organic molecules, the future of open-shell containing macromolecules is promising. Importantly, the ultimate performance of these next-generation materials has yet to be established either in theory or in practice. Therefore, the continued push to synthesize novel open-shell macromolecular architectures is of great import. Only in this way will radical-containing polymers be able to continue to make the strong impacts that they have to this point in their development. If these important strides are met, however, the future of radical polymers holds great potential. Specifically, if the combined talents of theoreticians, chemists, physicists, and engineers are placed at the fore of interdisciplinary open-shell macromolecular research, significant fundamental scientific breakthroughs will occur. In turn, these breakthroughs will lead to the transfer of technology that will allow radical-based polymers to appear in commercial applications in the relatively near term. Thus, the precise design and continued synthesis of open-shell macromolecules has the potential to revolutionize both fundamental science and applied engineering efforts for many years into the future.
