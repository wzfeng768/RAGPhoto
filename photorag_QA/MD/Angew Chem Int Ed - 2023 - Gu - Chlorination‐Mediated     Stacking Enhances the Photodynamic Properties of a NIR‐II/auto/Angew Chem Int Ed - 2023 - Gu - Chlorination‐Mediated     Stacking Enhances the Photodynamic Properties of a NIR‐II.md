# Chlorination-Mediated $\pi { - } \pi$ Stacking Enhances the Photodynamic Properties of a NIR-II Emitting Photosensitizer with Extended Conjugation

Abstract: NIR-II-emitting photosensitizers (PSs) have attracted great research interest due to their promising clinical applications in imaging-guided photodynamic therapy (PDT). However, it is still challenging to realize highly efficient PDT on NIR-II PSs. In this work, we develop a chlorination-mediated $\pi { - } \pi$ organizing strategy to improve the PDT of a PS with conjugation-extended A-D-A architecture. The significant dipole moment of the carbon-chlorine bond and the strong intermolecular interactions of chlorine atoms bring on compact $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the chlorine-substituted PS, which facilitates energy/charge transfer and promotes the photochemical reactions of PDT. Consequently, the resultant NIR-II emitting PS exhibits a leading PDT performance with a yield of reactive oxygen species higher than that of previously reported long-wavelength PSs. These findings will enlighten the future design of NIR-II emitting PSs with enhanced PDT efficiency.

# Introduction

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a photochemical-based cancer treatment method showing the advantages of noninvasiveness, spatiotemporal selectivity, less drug resistance, high efficiency, and low side effects. Photosensitizers (PSs) play a crucial role in driving the photochemical reaction of converting light energy to cytotoxic reactive oxygen species

Recently, more and more NIR-II emitting organic PSs have been reported due to their appealing applicating potentials in imaging-guided tumor phototherapy.l Compared with the D-A-D architecture, the A-D-A structured small molecular PSs show higher absorptivity and PDT efficiency making them more potent in developing NIR-II emitting PSs.1] In 2020, we reported a small-molecular AD-A type photosensitizer, COi6-4Cl, which exhibits superior NIR absorptivity and bright NIR-II emitting.11 Moreover, COi6-4Cl showed NIR-activated type-I/II combined PDT. However, the PDT efficiency of these reported NIR-I emitting PSs is still relatively low (Table S1) due to the weakened ICT nature of the PS's extended $\pi$ structures and the low excitation energy of long-wavelength absorption. Therefore, strategies are still desirable for enhancing the PDT efficiency of NIR-II PSs.

In this work, a new imidazole-involved seven-ring-fused electron-donating core was designed, and two A-D-A smallmolecular PSs, BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F with differenthalogen substitutions,  were synthesized (Scheme 1). The extended $\pi$ -conjugation structures endow these two PSs with deep-red absorption at ${ \approx } 8 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and NIR-II fluorescence with the main emission exceeding $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and the band tail over $1 3 7 5 \mathrm { n m }$ . Thus, by these new PSs, the spatiotemporal resolution of NIR-II fluorescence imaging was significantly enhanced. Moreover, despite the similar photophysical properties of BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F, we observed that the ROS yield of BMICBO-4Cl nanoparticles (NPs) was about 1.7 times higher than that of COi6-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F NPs, resulting from the concurrently enhanced type-I and type-II PDT processes. Single crystal X-ray studies showed the Cl-driving intermolecular interactions facilitated BMIC-BO-4Cl to form more compact $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in comparison with F-substituted BMIC-BO-4F, accordingly, which would construct more $\pi { - } \pi$ interaction of junctions for promoting energy/charge transfer. Due to the strong hydrophobicity of $\pi$ -conjugated BMIC-BO-4Cl/F molecules, the PDT-dependent photochemical reactions will mainly happen on the NP's surface with $\mathrm { { O } } _ { 2 }$ and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ Consequently, energy (type-II) and charge (type-I) transfer from the excited PSs in the center part to the surface became crucial for PDT efficiency, and both highly depend on $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the aggregated NPs. From these observations, we proved that the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking of organic PSs could facilitate photo-induced energy/charge transfer and catalyze the photoreactions at the surface of nanoparticles, efficiently improving the ROS production yield.

Further, the production of $\mathrm { O _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - } }$ , OH, and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ were detected by the fluorescence probes, dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123), aminophenyl fluorescein (APF), and singletoxygen-sensor-green (SOsG), respectively (Figures 2b, 2c, 2d and S26). We observed that the yields of $\mathrm { O _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - } }$ , .OH, and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ from photoirradiation of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs were 1.32, 1.47, and 1.49 folds higher than BMIC-BO-4F NPs. Accordingly, the enhanced $\Phi _ { \Delta }$ of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs came from concurrently improved type-I and type-II PDT processes. Further investigation was motivated by the significant difference between the two NPs in PDT performance. Especially the significant difference in type-I PDT provided an opportunity for us to explore the mechanism. Type-I PDT depends on the charge transfer between the PS and substrates to generate free radicals. The efficient charge separation of the PS upon photoexcitation and charge transfer from the interior of NPs to the surface will be crucial for the efficiency of the photochemical reactions. Accordingly, the charge separation and transport properties of the two PSs were investigated. First, transient photocurrent responses that were measured by the CV technique were applied to test the charge-separation capability of two materials under laser irradiation. Under six on/off cycles of laser irradiation, the photocurrent density of BMIC-BO-4Cl was two times higher than that of BMIC-BO-4F, as shown in Figure 2e. The result implies that BMIC-BO-4Cl exhibits higher charge separation efficiency of photogenerated electron-hole pairs.17] Next, the charge transfer properties of the two materials were characterized by electronic conductivity via the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) technique (Figure 2f). The charge transfer resistance can be read out from the semicircle's diameter of the Nyquist plot.[18] The BMIC-BO-4Cl film exhibited a smaller semicircle than BMIC-BO-4F, corresponding to a more efficient charge transfer. Moreover, the charge transport properties of the two materials were further investigated by the space charge limited current method.[9] The electron mobilities of BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F in films (aggregated states)  were calculated to be $1 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $0 . 8 3 \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , respectively (Figure $2 \mathrm { g }$ . These results demonstrated that BMIC-BO-4Cl was more conducive to charge separation and transfer compared with BMIC-BO-4F.

Charge separation and transfer properties highly rely upon the molecular packing structures; thus, we cultured the single crystals of the two molecules and analyzed their molecular arrangements in crystals. Figure 3 depicts the single-crystal structures of BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO4F, respectively.20] In crystals, both molecules adopted relatively planar backbones via the intramolecular noncovalent S (thiophenes in the core) -O (indanone in the terminal groups) interaction. Relatively short S..O interlock distances of ${ \overset { - } { \approx } } 2 . 6 8 { \overset { \circ } { \mathrm { A } } }$ were observed for both molecules. However, a small torsional angle of $1 . 8 5 ^ { \circ }$ between the core and terminal segments was observed for BMIC-BO-4Cl (Figure 3a), and the torsional angle increased to $9 . 8 1 ^ { \circ }$ for BMIC-BO-4F (Figure 3c). We had observed similar results from other A-D-A small molecules, i.e., chlorinated molecules showed relatively planar structures in comparison with their fluorinated analogs.l] The reason was that the strong Cl-derived intermolecular interactions would lead to more efficient $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking, which restricted the molecular structural torsions.22] In crystals, both BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMICBO-4F formed three-dimensional (3D) network $\pi - \pi$ packing structures. Such similar molecular packing modes were widely observed in the crystals of A-D-A small molecules, which greatly promote the charge transfer capability of this kind of materials. Generally, the compactness of $\pi - \pi$ stacking in such a 3D network was characterized by the framework size.23] As the size is smaller, the $\pi$ stacking is more compact. We observed that BMIC-BO-4Cl molecules were arranged into a very compact ellipse-like 3D network packing structure (Figure 3b) with a frame dimension of $\bar { L } x = 1 3 . 1 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ (major axis) and $L y = 1 1 . 0 \mathring { \mathbf { A } }$ (minor axis). On the contrary, BMIC-BO-4F formed a rectangular-like 3D network packing structure (Figure 3d) with a frame dimension of $\overset { \cdot } { L } x = 2 2 . 5 \overset { \circ } { \mathrm { A } }$ and $L y = 2 0 . 8 \mathring { \mathbf { A } }$ , exhibiting a looser molecular packing in comparison with BMIC-BO-4Cl. These molecular packing structures were well consistent with the results of electron mobility, i.e., BMIC-BO-4Cl in aggregated state exhibited charge mobility 2-fold higher than BMIC-BO-4F. In the crystal structures of BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F, typical halogen-driving noncovalent interactions were found, which played a crucial role in organizing $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking. To facilitate understanding, the seven-ring-fused donor part was denoted as core (C), and the two electron-accepting units were denoted as the terminal group (T). There exist various $\pi - \pi$ stacking configurations in the crystals, such as core-to-terminal (CT), core-to-core (CC), and terminal-to-terminal (TT). From the crystal structures, we noted that the Cl atoms showed strong Cl..N interactions with the imidazole units, leading to nearly parallel (with a dihedral angle of $0 . 2 7 ^ { \circ } .$ ) and close $\pi { - } \pi$ stackings ( $\pi - \pi$ distance of $3 . 4 3 { \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } }$ in the CT configuration of BMIC-BO-4Cl (Figure 3e). Differently, for BMIC-BO-4F, the F--N distance was $5 . 4 \dot { 8 } \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ , far exceeding the sum of the van der Waals radii of F and $\mathbf { N }$ atoms, which indicated the lack of F--N interactions in the CT configuration of BMICBO-4F. Accordingly, the dihedral angle and $\pi - \pi$ distance of the CT configuration increased to $9 . 0 8 ^ { \circ }$ and $3 . 4 9 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ (Figure 3f), corresponding to a relatively weak $\pi$ electron coupling effect. Similarly, some recent studies also revealed that chlorination is more efficient than fluorination in enhancing the intermolecular $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking and charge transport of organic semiconducting materials.?4 The main reasons include: (1) The $\mathrm { C - C l }$ bond has a larger dipole moment than the $\mathrm { C - F }$ bond, although the electronegativity of Cl is lower than that of F. (2) The surface electrostatic potential of the Cl atom in a $\mathrm { C - C l }$ bond contains both negative and positive regions, which could enhance its overlap (positive regions) with $\pi$ -electrons and electron pairs of other atoms.[25] As a result of the chlorination-mediated forces, BMIC-BO-4Cl formed a more compact 3D interpenetrating network than that of BMIC-BO-4F (Figures $3 \mathrm { g }$ and 3h), which can provide more intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ overlapping junctions for energy/charge transport. Considering the almost identical optical properties of the two NPs, their energy/charge transfer difference in the aggregated state might play a crucial role in determining their distinct ROS yields. Due to the strong hydrophobicity of $\pi$ -conjugated molecules, the PDT-dependent photochemical reactions will mainly happen on the NP's surface with $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ (Figure 2h). Consequently, energy (type-II) and charge (type-I) transfer from the excited PSs in the NP's center part to the surface became crucial for PDT efficiency.

Besides the improved PDT efficiency, the extended $\pi \cdot$ conjugation structure endows BMIC-BO-4Cl with redshifted main emission exceeding $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and the band tail over $1 3 7 5 \mathrm { n m }$ .According to the best of our knowledge, BMIC-BO-4Cl is the existing NIR-II emitting PS with the best PDT (showing both type I&II) performance (Table S1), showing a ROS yield 1.7-fold higher than COi6-4Cl.[11] Therefore, the phototheranostic properties of BMIC-BO4Cl were evaluated in the following part.

The high-resolution NIR-II fluorescence imaging will facilitate tumor diagnosis and guide PDT therapy better. Upon intravenous injection of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs, the whole body and brain fluorescence angiography of the representative mice were performed with different long-pass (LP) filters (1100, 1250, and $1 3 1 9 \mathrm { n m }$ . As shown in Figures4a and 4b, the vessels can be clearly observed $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ post-injection of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs. The NIR-II fluorescence images with the $1 3 1 9 \mathrm { n m }$ LP filter showed remarkably enhanced imaging contrast (Figure 4d). The signal-to-background ratio (SBR) values for hindlimb vessel imaging under the $1 3 1 9 \mathrm { n m }$ LP filter reached 2.8. On the other side, the imaging contrast (SBR values decreased to ${ \approx } 1 . 5 – 1 . 7 )$ was significantly compromised if 1100 and $1 2 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ LP filters were used. Owing to the enhanced contrast by the long-wavelength emission, tiny branching blood vessels could be distinguished more clearly (Figures $_ \mathrm { 4 c }$ and $4 \mathrm { e }$ ). These results proved that the longer emission wavelength of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs showed great significance in enhancing the spatiotemporal resolution of NIR-II fluorescence imaging.

Further, the intracellular ROS-production of BMIC-BO4Cl NPs were investigated under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions using four cell-permeable fluorescent probes (Figure 5a). The hypoxic environment was created by AnaeroPackTm-Anaero Anaerobic Gas Generator and confirmed by an anaerobic indicator (Figure S28). 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) is a standard fluorescent ROS probe indicating overall oxidative stress in live cells. SOsG, dihydroethidium (DHE), and APF and were employed as fluorescent probes for ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - }$ , and OH, respectively. Under $8 8 0 – \mathrm { n m }$ laser irradiation, 4T1 cells incubated with BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs and subsequently treated with DCFH-DA displayed bright green fluorescence no matter whether normoxic or hypoxic, indicating that ROS species could be effectively generated under both conditions. Also, the hypoxic condition showed a lower quantified fluorescence intensity (Figure S29), which consisted with fact that the production of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ could be suppressed in the hypoxic condition (Figure S30). To discriminate the ROS species, we observed that ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - }$ , and -OH were produced under normoxic conditions. On the contrary, under hypoxic conditions, $\mathrm { O _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - } }$ and OH were detectable, while no visible fluorescence was observed from SOsG. As $\mathrm { O _ { 2 } \cdot ^ { - } }$ and OH are produced from the type-I PDT process, the above phenomenon provided sufficient evidence that the type-I PDT was less dependent on $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ [26] Next, the cytotoxicity of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs to 4T1 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions were examined by cell counting kit-8 assay (CCK-8). As shown in Figure S31, BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs presented negligible dark cytotoxicity up to $5 0 \mu \mathrm { g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ in both conditions after $2 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of culture, indicating the good biocompatibility of the NPs. Upon $1 5 \mathrm { - m i n } ~ 8 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ laser irradiation, BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs showed concentration-dependent cytotoxicity (Figure 5b). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration $\left( \mathrm { I C } _ { 5 0 } \right)$ values of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs in normoxic and hypoxic conditions were calculated as 15.0 and $1 8 . 5 \mu \mathrm { g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, which indicated that BMICBO-4Cl NPs could effectively inhibit the proliferation of 4T1 cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In order to be more convincing, the cytotoxicity tests were conducted on HeLa cells as a model human cancer cell line. BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs showed good biocompatibility with HeLa cells in the dark condition and remarkable phototoxicity (Figure S32). The $\mathrm { I C } _ { 5 0 }$ values of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs in normoxic and hypoxic conditions for HeLa cells were calculated as 20.8 and $2 5 . 3 \mu \mathrm { g } \mathrm { m } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Next, a live/dead-cell staining experiment was performed (Figure 5c) to further confirm the anti-tumor capability of BMIC-BO4Cl NPs. Consistent with the CCK-8 results, 4T1 cells cultured with BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs under $8 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ laser showed that almost no cells survived.

Finally, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were employed to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacies of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs over a 16-day follow-up period. When the volumes of the subcutaneously implanted 4T1 tumors grew to $5 0 -$ $1 0 0 \mathrm { m m } ^ { 3 }$ , the mice were randomly divided into four groups $\scriptstyle n = 3$ for each group). The mice in these four groups were independently intravenously injected with PBS (Control group), $8 8 0 – \mathrm { n m }$ laser irradiation only (Laser group), BMICBO-4Cl NPs without laser irradiation (NPs group), BMICBO-4Cl NPs with $8 8 0 – \mathrm { n m }$ laser irradiation (NPs+laser group). Prior to the treatments, we examined the pharmacokinetic properties of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs. The semi-quantified blood circulation analysis showed that the half-life time of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs in blood was about $2 . 9 \mathrm { h }$ (Figure S33), which was sufficiently long for PDT.7] After intravenous injection of the BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs dispersion in PBS into tumor-bearing mice, NIR-II region fluorescence imaging was conducted to display the tumor accumulation. Figures 5d and 5e show that NIR-II fluorescence intensity at the tumor site continuously increased after NPs injection until the highest intensity was observed at $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . Moreover, the fluorescence remained strong at $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ post-injection, demonstrating the ideal accumulation time and long-term tumor retention of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs for guiding subsequent therapy. The 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were sacrificed after $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ injection (Figure S34) to quantitatively study the biodistribution in various major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors. The BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs had an apparent accumulation in primary reticuloendothelial system organs, including the liver and spleen. Notably, BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs presented obvious intensity in tumor tissues, which was consistent with the results mentioned above. Under laser irradiation, tumors' surface temperature of the Laser and NPs+laser group were monitored $2 4 \mathrm { { h } }$ post-administration (the optimized accumulation time) (Figure S35), and we observed that the temperature did not exceed $4 1 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ upon $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ irradiation, which ensured that no photothermal effect was involved in the therapeutic outcomes.28] Representative images of tumors extracted from the mice indicated the significant tumor-growth suppression effect after the mice received the treatment in the NPs+laser group (Figure S36). The tumor size and bodyweight of mice were recorded every two days. As shown in Figure 5f, during 16 days of treatment, mice in the NPs+laser group displayed pronounced suppression of tumor growth. In contrast, the tumor volumes of mice in the other groups (Control, Laser, and NPs) increased significantly and were approximately six times larger than the original. Further, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to verify the therapeutic efficiency by analyzing the morphology of tumor tissues (Figure 5h). In the NPs+laser group, tumor tissues showed severe cell necrosis, while a large number of undamaged 4T1 cells with intact nuclei and cytoplasm were observed in the other groups. Besides, the mice bodyweights in all four groups exhibited no noticeable weight loss (Figure 5g), indicating the biosafety of BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs and the applied laser power. To further prove the biosafety, the mice were sacrificed after 16 days to get the main organs (including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) for histopathologic analysis. The H&E staining images showed no significant physiological morphology abnormalities or histopathological lesions in all groups (Figure S37). In addition, blood panel analysis was conducted after the PDT treatment. All parameters in the NPs+laser group were in the reference range (Table S2). Notably, mice in the $\mathrm { N P s + }$ laser group showed declined white blood cells compared to the other three groups, which might be the outcome of the tumor suppression. Above all, the excellent tumor imaging capability, low toxicity, and good anti-tumor effect confirmed that BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs were suitable for NIR-II fluorescence imaging-guided PDT.

# Conclusion

In summary, two NIR-II-emitting PSs, BMIC-BO-4Cl and BMIC-BO-4F, were synthesized and investigated. The two PSs exhibited strong deep-red absorption at ${ \approx } 8 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and NIR-II fluorescence, with the main emission exceeding $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and the emission tail extending over $1 3 7 5 \mathrm { n m }$ greatly benefiting the spatiotemporal resolution of NIR-II fluorescence imaging. On the other side, the halogen substituents substantially affected the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the aggregated state of NPs. The PDT efficacy of BMIC-BO4Cl NPs was about 1.7 times higher than that of BMIC-BO4F NPs. Finally, by using BMIC-BO-4Cl NPs, a high NIR-II fluorescence imaging resolution and an efficient tumor suppression outcome were obtained. These observations will encourage the development of long-wavelength photosensitizers for NIR-II fluorescence-guided PDT applications.
