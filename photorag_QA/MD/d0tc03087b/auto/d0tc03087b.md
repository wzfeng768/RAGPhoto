# Charge transport and mobility relaxation in organic bulk heterojunction morphologies derived from electron tomography measurements†

Michael C. Heiber, $\textcircled{1}$ \*ab Andrew A. Herzing,c Lee J. Richter $\textcircled { 1 0 } ^ { \flat }$ and Dean M. DeLongchamp \*b

The charge carrier mobility is one of the most critical electronic materials properties that determines the ultimate performance of organic photovoltaic (OPV) cells. However, it is also a property with complex dependencies on the charge carrier density, electric field, lengthscale, and timescale, which can each vary depending on the chemical structure, molecular order and orientation, phase morphology, etc. These issues have made it extremely challenging to develop quantitative structure–property relationships that would allow rational molecular and materials design for next generation OPVs. Using a unique combination of advanced experimental morphology characterization (electron tomography) and recently developed open-source computational tools for morphology analysis and kinetic Monte Carlo charge transport simulations, we investigate how the microstructural features in real bulk heterojunction blends impact charge transport physics. This work demonstrates that simulated charge transport in real morphologies can differ significantly from that found with the commonly used Ising-based model. However, most significantly, there are fundamental differences in the mobility relaxation dynamics between homogeneous neat materials and bulk heterojunction blends. The tortuosity of the bulk heterojunction domain network causes electric-field-induced dispersion that can significantly prolong the mobility relaxation dynamics. These morphological effects must be considered when analyzing experimental mobility results and when choosing the appropriate measurement technique.

# I. Introduction

Organic photovoltaic devices continue to increase in performance via a combination of developments in new molecule discovery and morphology optimization, with single junction devices at $1 5 \%$ power conversion efficiency1 and tandem devices over $1 7 \%$ .2 High efficiency devices are created with an active layer composed of a blend of two or more organic semiconducting materials that phase separate to form a bulk heterojunction (BHJ) structure. Despite major progress over the years, there are still major gaps in fundamental understanding of how the multiscale morphological structure impacts the ultimate device performance. While a number of design guidelines have emerged,3–5 materials and device development remains driven by trial and error. Qualitatively, BHJ morphologies generally require small enough domain sizes to maximize exciton dissociation, while at the same time providing sufficient molecular order and domain connectivity as well as direct charge transport pathways to promote fast charge extraction. However, due to the complex BHJ morphology needed to produce efficient devices, detailed quantitative structure–property relationships have been challenging to develop. To enable rational synthetic design, formulation, and process design, further progress is needed.

Amongst a wide variety of different physical phenomena, charge transport in organic semiconductors and the associated charge carrier mobility metric that characterizes it has wide ranging impacts on eventual device performance.6 A detailed understanding of how the blend morphology impacts the longrange charge carrier mobility is particularly critical for designing high performance OPVs. Low mobilities cause slow charge extraction that leads to high charge recombination and low device fill factor.7–10 However, the challenge is more complicated than simply developing molecules with high mobility, because often the mobility is significantly reduced after blending with the second component.7,11–13 Although blending can disrupt molecular order and thereby reduce the mobility, recent kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations on model BHJ morphologies have shown that convoluted charge transport pathways can also have a major impact on the magnitude of the mobility and its electric-field dependence.14

When considering the mobility of photogenerated charge carriers, one must additionally consider transient effects. A series of studies by Melianas et al. have shown that the mobility can have a significant time dependence, which is argued to result from charge carrier relaxation into the tail of the density of states.15–17 The significantly higher mobility at short times is proposed to be an explanation for the high charge separation yield in some blends through the so-called disorder assisted charge separation mechanism.18–23 Another potentially major consequence of this phenomenon is that the often used space charge limited current (SCLC) or photo-CELIV measurement techniques may significantly underestimate the mobility of a particular material under device operating conditions.16,17 Conversely, further studies by Corre et al. have shown that SCLC mobilities are very consistent with the device charge extraction lifetime in a number of blends and argued that charge carrier relaxation occurs too fast to significantly affect the charge extraction process.24 These studies have prompted significant debate about the most appropriate measurement techniques and measurement conditions for determining the charge transport characteristics most relevant to device operation.25,26 However, none of these mobility relaxation studies have investigated the potential role of the BHJ structure in the relaxation process. While van der Kaap and Koster recently showed that mobility relaxation is fast in a neat P3HT diode,27 the behavior could be different in a BHJ blend due to tortuosity or blend induced disorder. Mobility relaxation has also frequently been argued as the origin of super-secondorder nongeminate charge recombination kinetics.23,28–30

Overall, a greater understanding is needed for how each of the morphological features of a BHJ impact charge carrier transport. However, blend morphologies are notoriously challenging to control and also difficult to quantitatively characterize. Ideally one would like to control domain size, domain composition, crystallinity, molecular orientation, film roughness, and vertical stratification, all while retaining the ability to rapidly cast films from solution. In reality, small changes to molecular structure, blend formulation, or processing conditions can cause significant changes to many of the morphological features at once, and a series of samples with well-controlled morphological features is extremely difficult to fabricate. Even if one could fabricate a reasonably well-controlled morphology set, fully characterizing the complex three-dimensional structure is difficult. While a handful of groups have demonstrated the ability of using advanced electron tomography measurements to create three-dimensional images,31–37 the measurements require careful sample preparation, expensive instrumentation, complex data analysis, and detailed expertise that is not available to most research groups.

To side-step these challenges, three-dimensional KMC simulations using well-controlled model morphologies have often been used to more systematically investigate the complex structure–property relationships due to features such as the domain size, domain shape, interfacial mixing, and domain network connectivity.38 Of the various morphology models available, the Ising-based model has become a widely used standard in KMC simulation studies.14,39–44 However, the Ising-based morphology model is highly simplified relative to most real systems, and there open questions about whether important structural features may be missed when using this simplified model.

While detailed structural analysis methods have been introduced for quantifying electron tomography images in the context of BHJ OPVs,45 quantitative connections between detailed structural features and charge transport physics have not been explored. Recently, Li et al. performed a device simulation study looking at a variety of factors using detailed morphologies derived from electron tomography measurements and found significant differences between experimentally derived BHJ morphologies and simpler simulated BHJ morphologies.46 Here, we expand on these previous studies by performing more detailed KMC charge transport simulations using BHJ morphologies derived from electron tomography measurements on a series of polymer:fullerene blend films cast from different solvents. These methods demonstrate an unconventional, state-of-the-art combination of physics-based charge transport modeling with 3-D real space materials microstructural imaging. To shed light on the structural features that affect charge transport, we compare the charge transport behavior between the experimentally-derived morphologies and simple Ising-based model morphologies. In particular, we perform a detailed analysis of how various structural features impact the electric-field dependence of the mobility and the mobility relaxation dynamics.

# A. Electron tomography thin film morphology measurements

OPV devices were fabricated using a conventional architecture, in which an aqueous PEDOT:PSS solution was spin cast onto clean, pre-patterned glass/ITO substrates in air. Blends of P3HT and $\mathrm { L u } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { P C } _ { 8 0 } \mathrm { B E H }$ (1 : 1 by weight) were dissolved in solution at $2 0 \mathrm { \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ , and the active layer was deposited by spin coating in a ${ \bf N } _ { 2 }$ glovebox. The $\mathrm { L u } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } { \cdot } \mathrm { P C } _ { 8 0 } \mathrm { B E H }$ acceptor molecules were provided by Luna Innovations.47 The general procedure was performed for three variants. One sample was prepared from a chlorobenzene (CB) solvent formulation, one from a 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB) formulation, and the last from a chlorobenzene:1-chloronaphthalene solvent mixture with $2 \ \mathrm { v o l \% }$ CN (CB:CN). These three variants will be hereafter referred to as the CB, DCB, and CB:CN samples. The CB sample was spin cast at 62.8 rad $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ $\left( 6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m } \right)$ for $2 0 \ : \mathrm { s }$ followed by open drying. The DCB sample was spin cast at 62.8 rad $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ $\left( 6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m } \right)$ for 75 s followed by slow drying under the cover of a glass Petri dish. The CB:CN sample was spin cast at 62.8 rad $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ $\left( 6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m } \right)$ for 20 s followed by open drying. Once dry, all three samples were annealed at $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 10 minutes followed by thermal evaporative deposition of a LiF/Al cathode. Following cathode evaporation, a post-fabrication annealing was performed at $1 6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $_ { 3 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } }$ . All three fabrication routes produced OPV devices with similar overall current–voltage behavior and power conversion efficiency of approximately $3 . 8 \%$ under standard AM1.5 illumination conditions, which is slightly lower than the optimized device performance47 due to reduced film thickness.

Following electrical measurements, samples for TEM analysis were prepared via floatation in de-ionized (DI) water. The films were scored with a scalpel and introduced to a Petri dish filled with DI water at approximately $4 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . After a short period of time, the underlying PEDOT:PSS layer dissolves in the DI water, and the BHJ film floats freely on the surface of the water. Pieces of the film were then picked up with a 200-mesh copper TEM grid. The films were then iteratively rinsed with clean DI water and dried with filter paper.

In electron tomography, images are collected over a range of specimen tilts with respect to the electron beam and a 3-D representation of the volumetric phase distribution is reconstructed from the stack of images.48 Electron tomography of BHJ blends can often be challenging due to the similar electron density of the constituent components and the resulting lack of strong amplitude contrast in the images.33 In the present case, the Lu-containing fullerene component provided strong amplitude contrast in bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images due to strong differential scattering of the incoming electrons. While this increased scattering from the Lu-containing fullerene aided phase discrimination when imaging the specimen at low tilt, the image quality in the tomography experiments eventually degraded to chromatic blur at high tilt where the effective specimen thickness and thus the amount of plural scattering was significant.

To overcome this limitation, energy-filtered (EF)-TEM was employed to eliminate the multiply scattered electrons and recover the image fidelity. A series of zero-loss filtered brightfield images was collected from $- 7 0 ^ { \circ }$ to $+ 7 0 ^ { \circ }$ using a Saxton-style tilt scheme, where the tilt step at $0 ^ { \circ }$ was set to $1 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ . Images were collected on a FEI Titan 80–300 TEM operating at $3 0 0  { \mathrm { ~ \ k e V } }$ primary beam energy and equipped with a Gatan Tridiem 865 imaging energy-filter for EF-TEM analysis. Each zero-loss filtered bright-field image was collected using an exposure of 1 s and the resulting image stack was then used as the input for tomographic reconstruction. This stack of images was aligned and reconstructed using the IMOD v3.10 software package.49–51 Alignment shifts were calculated via cross-correlation, after which reconstruction was carried out via weighted backprojection.

The 3-D images were then adjusted using Fiji software,52 a distribution of ImageJ,53 to fine tune the sample alignment by eye and manually cropped to remove the blurry edges at the top and bottom surfaces of the film. Since the data could not be collected over a full $1 8 0 ^ { \circ }$ tilt range, resolution loss occurs in the through thickness $( z )$ direction of the reconstruction. This, combined with a limited signal-to-noise ratio, makes location of the exact top and bottom surface boundaries in the reconstruction difficult. As a result, this study does not capture the effects of any thin skin layers that may or may not have formed at the film interfaces, and instead focuses on the structure and predicted charge transport behavior through the bulk of the BHJ film. The final images were approximately $1 \mu \mathrm { m }$ by $1 \mu \mathrm { m }$ in area, thereby providing structural data covering a large area of the film to generate a dataset that is representative of the overall film morphology.

# B. Morphology interpretation, generation, and analysis

In the series of 3D images measured here, contrast was obtained using the energy filter corresponding to the maximum contrast between the signals from the donor-rich domains (P3HT) and the acceptor-rich domains (endofullerene). While previous work on this blend system by Roehling et al. was able to spectroscopically resolve the mixed phase,36 here we do not observe a distinct feature that can be definitively assigned to the mixed phase. As a result, with the current EF-TEM tomography dataset, we cannot quantitatively resolve an interfacial mixed phase, and we perform a simple binarization of the datasets, resulting in a final morphology with two pure phases. Due to this limitation, this initial study focuses on the impact of the domain network without including the potential impact of an interfacial mixed phase. The specific impact of an interfacial mixed phase is left to future studies.

Each electron tomography dataset was imported into the open-source Ising_OPV v4.0.1 software tool, where it was interpreted and then analyzed to determine a variety of structural metrics.54,55 From the grayscale images, the pixel brightness cutoff between the donor and acceptor phases was automatically assigned based on the known overall blend composition by volume, assuming two pure phases with pure phase densities. The conversion from weight fraction composition to volume fraction composition was performed using the previously measured densities of the components $( 1 . 1 \textrm { g c m } ^ { - 3 }$ for P3HT and $2 . 0 7 ~ \mathrm { g } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ for $\mathsf { L u } _ { 3 } \mathsf { N } \mathsf { - P C } _ { 8 0 } \mathsf { B E H } ) ,$ 56 resulting in a composition of 0.65 P3HT by volume. Following site type assignment, the large area lattice was then split up into a 7 by 7 grid to extract 49 equally sized subvolumes that span the full height to form a morphology set. Morphology structural analysis was then performed on each set to determine characteristics like domain size, domain tortuosity, interfacial area to volume ratio, etc. following previously developed methods.14,41,57 The domain size was calculated from the radial compositional autocorrelation data such that $d = 2 \xi$ , and $\xi$ is the correlation length determined by the radial distance at which the autocorrelation data decays to $1 / e$ . Tortuosity probability histograms were also generated using the updated Ising_OPV v4.0.2.58 See the $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ for additional morphology characterization data and the detailed characteristics calculated for each morphology set.59

In addition to the morphology sets derived from the electron tomography measurements, a series of Ising-based model morphologies were also generated using the Ising_OPV software tool for comparison. Model morphologies were generated on a 140 by 140 by 148 lattice to match the lattice dimensions of the CB and DCB morphology sets without using periodic boundaries in the $z$ -direction. A 0.65 donor volume fraction was also used to match the volumetric composition of the experimental blends. Three morphology sets (Ising1, Ising2, Ising3) were generated with varying tortuosity and domain size to closely match the structural characteristics of the real morphologies. See the $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ to see the full list of parameters used to create the Ising-based morphologies and further characterization of each morphology set.59

# C. KMC charge transport simulations

To capture the impact of the various morphological features on charge transport, KMC simulations were performed using the open-source Excimontec v1.0 software package60 built using the KMC_Lattice library.82 More specifically, time-of-flight charge transport simulations were performed to determine the mean charge carrier mobility as a function of the internal electric field $( F )$ following previously developed methods.14 Briefly, charge hopping was simulated using the Miller–Abrahams model61 on a three-dimension lattice with a unit size (a) of $^ { 1 \ n m }$ and with energetic disorder following the uncorrelated Gaussian disorder model (GDM).62 The energetic disorder parameter $( \sigma )$ describing the standard deviation of the density of states is kept constant at $6 5 \mathrm { \ m e V }$ , which is a typical value for high-performance materials that can vary from around 50 to $7 5 { \mathrm { ~ m e V } } .$ .63 All simulations were performed at $3 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ , resulting in a normalized disorder $\mathbf { \Psi } \left( \hat { \sigma } \mathbf { \Psi } = \sigma / k _ { \mathrm { B } } T \right)$ value of approximately 2.5. Energetic disorder and charge carrier hopping rate parameters are set equal for the donor and acceptor phase to focus on the impact of the BHJ structure apart from potential differences in the intrinsic electronic properties of the donor and acceptor materials. Energetic disorder is also uniform and uncorrelated to tortuosity in our models, again to highlight the impact of structure. It could potentially be connected to local tortuosity in future studies to reflect commonly-expected correlations between local order and morphological structure. Periodic boundaries were used in the plane of the film $( x { - } y$ plane). Despite the morphologies themselves not being periodic, edge effects were minor due to the relatively large lateral dimensions of the lattice $\approx 1 4 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ such that most of the transport occurs through the interior of the lattice. Lattices of various lateral sizes were tested and the mobility was found not to be significantly affected by the lattice size until much smaller lateral dimensions were used. The events were efficiently chosen and executed using the BKL algorithm together with the selective recalculation method for event updating.64,65 Electrostatic interactions between charge carriers were included up to a large cutoff radius of $5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ .

For the time-of-flight charge transport simulations, a thin sheet of charge carriers were placed randomly at a surface plane of the lattice (on donor sites for holes and on acceptor sites for electrons) and then allowed to undergo charge transport through the film. As the charge carriers migrated through the lattice over time under the influence of the applied electric field, the transient current density, mobility, and average occupation energy were recorded. In addition, the transit time for all extracted charge carriers was recorded to produce a transit time probability histogram. Further simulation details and additional simulation parameters are provided in the ESI.† 59

# III. Results and discussion

# A. Morphology set analysis

Despite very similar overall device performance, even the standard plan view EF-TEM images for each sample shown in Fig. 1 reveal distinct morphological features between the samples. In these images, the bright domains correspond to P3HT aggregates and the dark domains correspond to endofullerene aggregates. Qualitatively, the CB sample appears to have small nodular P3HT domain features, while the DCB and CB:CN samples both have more of a fibrillar P3HT structure. It has often been found that P3HT crystallization is the primary driver of the phase separation and phase evolution in P3HT: fullerene blends as the films dry,66 so these varied morphologies are likely to arise from differences in nucleation density, crystallization kinetics, and solvent evaporation rates. However, a detailed investigation of the structural formation process in these blends is beyond the scope of the current study.

Fig. 1 EF-TEM plan view images and binarized cross-sectional images for each sample showing the P3HT donor domains in white/blue and the endofullerene acceptor domains in black/red.

From the standard plan view images, one might interpret these fibrillar structures as a disordered, overlapping mesh of one-dimension fibers where the P3HT domains are elongated in the plane of the film. However, this simple interpretation turns out to be incorrect once one takes a closer look at the full three-dimensional structure revealed by the electron tomography measurements. In all three samples, structural analysis reveals the opposite domain anisotropy, where instead there is a preference for the domains to be elongated perpendicular to the plane of the film. In the CB sample, this means that the nodular structure is not random, but forms short rod-like structures that primarily stand vertically within the film ( ${ \approx } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ lateral dimension and $\approx 3 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ vertical dimension). In the DCB and CB:CN samples, the fibrillar looking structures are not actually one-dimensional fibers, but have more of a twodimensional platelet structure, where the platelets stand edgeon to the film interfaces. Such oriented aggregates are not expected to form due to homogeneous nucleation and growth, and instead point to other directional driving forces. As a result of this anisotropic domain structure, all three samples show low donor phase tortuosity $\left( \tau _ { \mathrm { d } } \right)$ and mild acceptor phase tortuosity $\left( \tau _ { \mathrm { a } } \right)$ , as summarized in Table 1.

In the end, despite the appearance of major structural diversity, all three samples share the property of having mildly aligned domain structures with reasonable connectivity in the vertical charge transport direction. However, due to the blend volume mismatch, the minority phase (acceptor) has a significantly larger tortuosity than the majority (donor) phase. To probe the tortuosity differences between each sample further, Fig. 2 shows the tortuosity probability histograms for the donor and acceptor phases of each experimental sample and the Ising-based model morphologies. This comparison reveals that there can be other more subtle structural differences between the samples. In the Ising-based model morphologies, there is a broadening of the histogram as tortuosity increases, but the distributions remain very symmetric. Although the CB film tortuosity appears almost identical to the Ising1 set, the DCB film has a highly asymmetric acceptor tortuosity histogram, and the CB:CN film has a much broader histogram than in the Ising2 set. As a result, the morphology sets derived from the electron tomography datasets show distinct structural differences compared with the Ising-based model that may be expected to produce different charge transport behavior.

To obtain a broader overview of the structure, each tomography image was loaded into a single lattice and analyzed to generate tortuosity and composition maps for each film. One of the origins of the broader tortuosity probability histograms can be observed from the tortuosity maps shown in Fig. 3. In these maps, each acceptor pixel at the top surface is colorized based on the tortuosity of the transport pathway to the bottom of the film, and black pixels represent the donor material at the top surface. This allows one to see how the vertical transport pathways in the $z \cdot$ -direction vary across the $x { - } y$ plane of the film. Here, one can see that the tortuosity through the acceptor phase is not at all homogeneous across the films and that there can be major fluctuations in tortuosity across lengthscales of $_ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ } s }$ of nanometers. The DCB and CB:CN samples in particular show occasional regions with very high tortuosity. Overall, the acceptor phase tortuosity is clearly not uniform across the entire film and there are large variations that are likely to lead to current hot spots and dark spots. These fluctuations contribute to the broad tortuosity histograms and are also at least partly the cause of the broad charge transit time distributions. In the time-of-flight simulation, clearly some of the charge carriers will have a direct charge transport pathway and encounter almost no barriers while others will have to traverse a very convoluted pathway through the film.

These structural features are consistent with observations of fluctuations in current output in conductive atomic force microscopy imaging,67,68 where current fluctuations can be significantly larger than the domain size. This together with charge generation fluctuations due to domain size and other variations can lead to even larger photocurrent fluctuations.67,69 Donor tortuosity maps show less variation due to the lower overall tortuosity and narrower tortuosity probability histograms but are provided in the ESI.† 59 In the end, there is rich structural complexity that is expected to have a marked impact on charge transport that will be missed using the more regular Ising-based model or simple effective medium models.

# B. Electric-field dependent mobility

As demonstrated previously with Ising-based model morphologies by Heiber et al., the tortuosity of the domain network should reduce both the magnitude and electric field dependence of the mobility relative to a neat homogeneous film.14 Fig. 4 shows the electric-field-dependent hole and electron mobilities obtained from the six BHJ morphologies and a homogeneous neat material. As expected, the mobility in the BHJ morphology is generally lower and has a weaker electricfield dependence than the neat material. Hole transport through the ultra-low tortuosity donor phase produces mobility very similar to the neat material (Fig. 4, top), whereas a stronger effect is observed in electron transport through the higher tortuosity acceptor phase (Fig. 4, bottom). However, we note here that none of these BHJ morphologies produce a negative electric-field dependence that would be indicative of a highly tortuous morphology.14 This is mostly because all of the samples have preferential domain elongation perpendicular to the plane of the film and relatively direct charge transport pathways. Nevertheless, there are still quantifiable changes in the mobility behavior.

Fig. 4 Hole mobility (top) and electron mobility (bottom) as a function of $F ^ { 1 / 2 }$ comparing a homogeneous neat material with the BHJ morphology sets.

To focus on the impact of the domain network tortuosity apart from the details of the mobility behavior in the neat material, we use the analysis procedure developed by Heiber et al. and characterize the deviation from the neat mobility $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { n e a t } } \right) .$ ,14

$$
\frac { \mu _ { \mathrm { B H J } } } { \mu _ { \mathrm { n e a t } } } = f ( \hat { \sigma } , \tau ) \exp { \bigg [ g ( \hat { \sigma } , \tau ) \sqrt { \frac { F } { F _ { 0 } } } \bigg ] } ,
$$

where $f ( \hat { \sigma } , \tau )$ represents the reduction in the magnitude of the zero-field mobility, $g ( \hat { \sigma } , \tau )$ captures the change in the slope of the field dependence, and $F _ { 0 } = \sigma / e a$ , where $e$ is the elementary charge constant. Here, $\hat { \sigma }$ is kept constant at approximately 2.5, so this dependency can be set aside. The mobility data for each BHJ morphology was normalized by the neat mobility data, the normalized electric field $\left( F / F _ { 0 } \right)$ was calculated, and the mobility data from each morphology set was fit to eqn (1). Detailed fitting results are provided in the ESI. $\dagger ^ { 5 9 }$ Fig. 5 shows extracted $f$ and $g ^ { \star }$ values from hole and electron transport simulations through all six BHJ morphologies and the neat material. By definition the neat material has $\tau = 1 , f = 1$ , and $g = 0$ .

From this data, we can fit empirical expressions for both $f$ and $g$ that capture the observed behavior and shows the exponential scaling relationship with the tortuosity. First,

$$
f ( \hat { \sigma } \approx 2 . 5 , \tau ) = \exp { \left( - \frac { \tau - 1 } { A } \right) } ,
$$

where $A$ is 0.21. Second,

$$
g ( \hat { \sigma } \approx 2 . 5 , \tau ) = B \exp \left( - \frac { \tau - 1 } { C } \right) - B ,
$$

where $B$ is 2 and $C$ is 0.1. Based on previous results,14 it is expected that these fit coefficients will also depend on the energetic disorder and the temperature, but these relationships are not elucidated here.

While these fits reflect the general trends reasonably well and seem to capture the electric-field dependent mobility of both the real morphologies and Ising-based morphologies, there is a decent amount of scatter and one noteworthy outlier. Scatter in the $f$ and $g$ values does not stem from uncertainties in the fitting procedure used to extract the values but rather reflects the impact of some other structural features that affect the mobility, which are not captured by the tortuosity metric. As a more extreme example, the CB sample produces a much smaller $g$ value than expected given its mild average tortuosity. Despite very similar tortuosity characteristics between the CB and Ising1 morphology sets, there is a large difference in the mobility between the two. This shows that while tortuosity is indeed important, it may not be the only structural metric needed to fully explain the mobility behavior. It also demonstrates that real BHJ morphologies can contain more exotic structural features that are not represented in the Ising-based model and that these structural differences can have a major impact on charge transport behavior. Future work on a wider variety of real BHJ structures may be able to pinpoint other important features and create a more universal model.

# C. Mobility relaxation dynamics

Much of the debate around the mobility relaxation phenomena has centered around whether or not the charge carriers can maintain positions in high energy sites long enough to impact the charge carrier extraction dynamics in BHJ OPVs. To model their observed experimental behavior, Melianas et al. utilized the Gaussian disorder model (GDM) that has been widely used to describe charge transport in neat disordered organic semiconductors.15–17 In the GDM, there exists a critical time where there is a transition from dispersive (time-dependent) to non-dispersive (time-independent) transport, and this transition time depends on the energetic disorder, temperature, electric field, hopping rate, lattice constant, and polaron localization parameter.70 Due to these complex relationships, fitting mobility relaxation data from KMC simulation using the GDM to experimental data is fraught with challenges finding a unique fit unless a high dimensional experimental dataset is available.

Nevertheless, the general mobility relaxation phenomenon in homogeneous neat materials is well-understood. Mobility relaxation occurs during the dispersive time regime and only after sufficient time does the transport reach the quasiequilibrium, non-dispersive regime. The physical origin of this behavior is that charge carriers relax into the tail of the density of states over time as they migrate through the material. Charge carriers that occupy higher energy sites can more frequently undergo barrierless hopping to lower energy neighboring sites and therefore move faster through the film. At quasiequilibrium transport conditions at low charge carrier density and at zero electric field, the charge carriers reach the so-called equilibration energy at $- \sigma ^ { 2 } / k _ { \mathrm { B } } T$ and then have a fixed average activation barrier for charge transport.70

Here, all competing parameters that dictate the mobility relaxation dynamics are held constant to focus solely on the impact of the BHJ morphology. Focus is also placed on electron transport through the higher tortuosity acceptor phase to investigate the impact of mild tortuosity. As shown in Fig. 4 (top), transport through the ultra-low tortuosity donor phase does not differ very much from the neat material. Fig. 6 (top) shows the electron mobility relaxation dynamics and the corresponding carrier thermalization transients at low electric field, close to short-circuit device conditions. While the neat film simulation shows a slow transition to approximately nondispersive transport by $^ { 1 0 \ n s }$ , all of the BHJ blends show no clear signs of reaching a nondispersive steady-state mobility up to almost 100 ns. As a result, one can identify two distinct regimes for mobility relaxation in a BHJ morphology. At short times (sub 1 ns), the mobility relaxation is dictated by carrier thermalization closely following the behavior of a neat material. However, at longer times (beyond 1 ns), the charge carriers are mostly thermalized and the mobility of the neat material plateaus, yet the mobility relaxation in the BHJ morphologies continues due to the tortuosity of the domain network. The exact transition time between the two regimes should depend on the energetic disorder, temperature, electric field, and tortuosity, but this would have to be tested further in future studies.

The changes in dispersion observed here are not due to differences in how the charge carriers relax in the DOS but due to morphological traps that can be roughly characterized by the tortuosity metric. Despite similar carrier thermalization transients obtained with most of the BHJ morphology sets, the mobility relaxation differs greatly. Generally, the morphologies with the highest tortuosity exhibit the most dispersion. One interesting outlier here is the CB:CN sample, which has a persistently lower mobility and a higher occupation energy than the other BHJ morphology sets. This morphology appears to have a significant number of morphological traps near the top surface that significantly slows down some of the carrier from migrating through the film. Because their motion is initially very limited, these trapped charges significantly lower the average mobility, but they are also unable to hop to the lower energy sites and thereby raise the average occupation energy. These morphological traps could stem from a significant depletion of the acceptor phase at the film interfaces, see the ESI† for further details.59

![](images/81de8b17962a0e068f520f0aad71ca497e7eaca723a6043a50e98a5c3d4b0f0b.jpg)  
Fig. 6 Electron mobility relaxation (top) and electron thermalization (bottom) dynamics at low electric field, $F ^ { 1 / 2 } = \ 4 0 0 \ \mathrm { ( V ~ c m ^ { - 1 } ) ^ { 1 / 2 } }$ , for neat and BHJ morphologies.

To understand this behavior further, we take a closer look at the mobility relaxation behavior in the neat and DCB morphologies. To obtain more complete transient mobility behavior, we repeat simulations for a $1 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ thick neat film and extend the electric field range down to 100 $( \mathrm { V ~ c m } ^ { - 1 } ) ^ { 1 / 2 }$ for both morphologies. Fig. 7 shows the mobility relaxation curves for different values of the electric field. In the neat morphology, the transition from dispersive to nondispersive transport is much clearer, as the mobility plateaus at long time and reaches the steady state mobility value. With increasing electric field, the steady state mobility increases due to an increase in the equilibration energy. In the GDM for homogeneous neat materials, the electric field plays a similar role as temperature in increasing the equilibration energy.70 In addition, there is dramatic change in the transition time from dispersive to nondispersive transport depending on the applied electric field. For the highest electric field tested, the mobility is completely relaxed and nondispersive already by 10 fs, but at the lowest electric field tested, nondispersive transport is not reached until around $1 ~ \mu \mathrm { s }$ . While a number of studies have investigated how the transition time depends on the energetic disorder and temperature,70 there has been very little emphasis on the impact of the electric field. These results clearly show that even if the particular blend has very low tortuosity and a large disorder such that the mobility relaxation is still dominated by carrier thermalization, once must pay careful attention to the electric field at which the mobility is measured.

Regardless, in a typical BHJ blend with reasonably low energetic disorder at room temperature, the question of whether or not the charge carriers are thermalized is not the major consideration for determining if mobility relaxation occurs. Even after charge carrier thermalization in the DOS, charge transport continues to be dispersive due to morphological barriers and dead ends that the charge carriers encounter as they migrate through the film. To bypass a barrier or escape a dead end, charge carriers must travel perpendicular to or even against the electric field to find another transport pathway. A stronger electric field is of no help for perpendicular transport and makes it more difficult for the charge carriers to escape a dead end. This mechanism continues in a stochastic way such that some charges encounter very few morphological traps and move quickly through the film, while others encounter more morphological traps and are slowed down.

![](images/b6d1e11519c128aae810d61f5f83e47bf07194b77b3ec494b31eb483bde660f9.jpg)  
Fig. 7 (a) Mobility relaxation and (b) carrier thermalization transients for neat (top) and DCB (bottom) morphologies at different electric field values.

Fig. 7 shows that a primary driver of mobility relaxation on long timescales is the tortuosity of BHJ blend morphology together with the strength of the electric field. In the DCB morphology, as the electric field increases the mobility decreases and transport becomes more dispersive. This is in direct opposition to what is observed in the neat material. As a result, in BHJ blends, there is a de-coupling between the equilibration energy and the charge carrier mobility. In this study, charge transport within the donor and acceptor phases is isotropic in order to focus on the impact of the BHJ morphology. However, molecular scale features such as transport anisotropy due to molecular orientation within individual domains could also impart additional constraints that could further modulate the mobility and its dispersion.71

In a BHJ blend with low tortuosity and high energetic disorder, charge carrier thermalization may still be the dominant effect, but with realistic parameters representative of many high performance devices, tortuosity can have a significant impact and must be considered. In electric-fielddependent time-of-flight measurements on the disordered $\mathbf { T Q 1 : P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathbf { B M }$ BHJ blend reported by Melianas et al., 15 the transit time distributions do not become narrower as the electric field increases as expected in a homogeneous neat material, instead they broaden slightly. This electric-fieldinduced broadening of the transit time distribution is consistent with transport through a BHJ morphology that is affected by tortuosity.59 The standard GDM does not capture this behavior. Due to this electric-field-dependent dispersion, it is extremely important to be aware of the electric field at which the charge carrier mobility is being measured. A mobility measured near short-circuit conditions could exhibit significantly more dispersion than the mobility characterized at maximum power conditions.

Since the tortuosity of a given BHJ blend device is almost never known, it may be difficult to compare mobility measurements measured by different research groups even using the same measurement technique. Even when measuring the same neat material, SCLC measurements between different labs can vary significantly due to different measurement and data analysis protocols.72 In SCLC measurements and even other techniques, comparing measurement results between laboratories may be further challenged by the fact that differences in film fabrication methods could lead to morphologies with significantly different tortuosities. The complex electric-fielddependence characterized here for BHJ blends is also expected to vary depending on the charge carrier density.73 So even with identical morphologies, different measurement techniques that measure at different carrier densities may also produce very different mobility relaxation dynamics.

Ideally, more studies would characterize the detailed threedimensional morphology using electron tomography and then calculate the tortuosity to pair with the mobility measurement. However, due to the time and cost of the method, it is understandably not a routine measurement for characterizing and screening new materials. In lieu of the full characterization, a faster, qualitative method to gauge whether or not tortuosity is having a significant impact on the mobility is to characterize how the mobility behavior changes as the electric field increases. If tortuosity is not limiting charge transport, the mobility should increase and become less dispersive. And if tortuosity does play a significant role, the mobility should become more dispersive.

# IV. Conclusions

The details of the three-dimensional morphology can have a significant impact on the charge transport behavior in a BHJ OPV. While plan view TEM images provide more structural information than AFM surface images, projection images cannot capture structural features perpendicular to the plane of the film, which are some of the most critical features for efficient vertical charge transport in a high-performance OPV device. KMC simulations of charge transport behavior through realistic morphologies derived from electron tomography measurements reaffirm tortuosity as a dominant factor in the overall magnitude of the long-range charge carrier mobility. Despite differences in BHJ structure observed in plan view TEM images, low tortuosity morphologies are obtained in $\mathrm { P 3 H T : L u } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } \mathrm { - }$ $\mathrm { P C _ { 8 0 } B E H }$ blends via multiple solvent casting recipes, potentially indicative of a blend whose optimized self-assembled structure is relatively tolerant to processing variability. However, KMC simulations also reveal that transport behavior can be significantly different than expected from simple Isingbased model morphologies. As a result, three-dimensional imaging techniques such as electron tomography are critical for building the detailed processing-structure–property–performance relationships that will allow rational materials and device design in the future.

Furthermore, the large body of fundamental charge transport physics in homogeneous, neat disordered organic semiconductors must be carefully reassessed before being applied to BHJ blends given the clear impact that the morphological features have on transport. Even with the relatively low tortuosity of the BHJ morphologies investigated in this study, the impact on charge transport behavior is large. There are major differences in how the charge carrier mobility responds to an applied electric field, yielding a decreased electric-field dependence and an increase in dispersion. In the future, the standard Gaussian disorder model should not be applied to BHJ blends without careful consideration of the expected impact due to the tortuosity of the domain network.

While we have demonstrated these effects on both realistic and simple model BHJ blend morphologies, even in many neat materials, structural features introduced by semi-crystallinity are likely to have similar effects. Given significant changes in energetic disorder and shifting of energy levels between crystalline and amorphous phases, 74–77 semicrystalline films may often times exhibit similar charge transport phenomenology to BHJ blends. For example, semicrystalline regioregular P3HT films have been shown to produce a mobility with a negative electric-field dependence,78–80 consistent with transport affected by tortuosity. Mollinger et al. has shown that larger scale structural features and tie chains are needed to explain the negative electric-field dependence of the mobility seen in semicrystalline P3HT.81 In addition, molecular orientational heterogeneity at the nanoscale could have a similar effect as compositional heterogeneity in materials that exhibit charge transport anisotropy.

Future efforts to control the tortuosity of the domain network through chemical modifications and materials processing will be critical for realizing optimal power conversion efficiencies in OPVs. The open-source software tools to further quantify how specific morphological features affect each of the fundamental optoelectronic processes in OPVs are now readily available to make maximal use of laborious and expensive electron tomography measurements. Next-generation models could include more materials-specific details such as accurate charge carrier mobility and potentially mobility anisotropy within domains. Future work in this vein will help us reach more quantitative structure–property relationships and allow us to further understand the specific morphological features needed to engineer ever more efficient and cost competitive BHJ OPVs for a wide variety of applications.
