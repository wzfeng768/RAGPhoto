ABSTRAcT: We study the degree of $\pi - \pi$ interactions in two copolymers composed of 2-octylbenzotriazole and dialkyldithienosiloles with linear hexyl (P1) and branched 2-ethylhexyl groups (P2). Although the branched ethyl group can be positioned above the $\pi$ -plane of dithienosilole to bring disadvantage to stack among the polymers, the solid-state absorption spectra of the polymers reveal that P2 possesses stronger interchain interactions than P1. We also evaluate the photovoltaic properties of each polymer with [6,6]-phenyl- $\cdot \mathsf { C } _ { 7 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester. $\circledcirc$ 2012 Wiley Periodicals, Inc. KEYWORDS: $\pi - \pi$ stacking; branch effect; conjugated polymers; organic photovoltaics; side chain at sp3 element

INTRODUcTION Organic photovoltaics (OPVs) with a bulk heterojunction (BHJ) architecture comprising a $\pi$ -conjugated polymer as an electron donor and a fullerene derivative as an electron acceptor' have attracted considerable attention because of their light weight, flexibility, and potential for high-performance? as well as low-cost solution-based fabrication.3,4 To date, power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of over $8 \%$ have been achieved;5 however, further improvements are needed for commercial viability. The structure of a $\pi$ -conjugated polymer is closely linked to the properties and morphologies of its blend films with fullerenes, which affect device performance. The short-circuit current $\left( J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \right)$ and opencircuit voltage $\left( V _ { \mathrm { { O C } } } \right)$ are roughly predicted from the absorption spectra and the difference between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HomO) level of a donor and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMo) level of an acceptor, and the HOmO level is predominantly governed by the structure of the polymer backbones and finely tuned by the peripheral substituents.? Even if the electronic properties satisfy favorable conditions, the performance of BHJ solar cells still depends on the nanoscale molecular alignments of a donor and an acceptor. Control over the morphology of the blend films of a polymer and a fullerene derivative is a key step to achieve high efficiencies. The morphology is controlled not only by the backbone and side chains of the polymers but also by various aspects of device fabrication methods, such as the choice of solvents, solvent additives, and thermal and solvent annealing.

Thermal annealing is a reliable technique to enhance the crystallinity of the blend films of a regioregular poly(3- alkylthiophene) (P3AT) and a [6,6]-phenyl $\cdot \mathsf { C } _ { 6 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM).10 Because of thermal annealing, the charge mobilities of the blend films, which also affect $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } ,$ significantly increase. Padinger et al. reported that postproduction thermal treatment of the P3AT:PCBM blend film significantly improved the PCE owing to enhancement of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and the fill factor (FF) compared with the as-cast film.11 Branching of the alkyl chains is also an important factor. Although polymers that have branched alkyl chains at 3-position of thiophene ring exhibit lower crystallinity12 and reduced melting points,13 they can be produced in high molecular weight by virtue of increased solubility, giving rise to higher PCEs of over $6 \%$ 14 To the best of our knowledge, almost all OPVs with over $6 \%$ efficiency use polymers with branched alkyl chains. In fact, there have been many studies focused on the effect of long and/or branched alkyl chains on the morphologies and resulting photovoltaic performances. Thus far, the morphological control of polymers appears to be focused on polymers with alkyl chains at their sp2 elements,15 from which the alkyl chains protrude in the in-plane direction. Less attention seems to be paid to the alkyl chains attached at the sp3 element, from which the alkyl chains protrude in the out-of-plane direction.16

In this contribution, we used two donor-acceptor copolymers-PDTSi6-DTBTz and PDTSiEH-DTBTz-composed of dithienosiloles with hexyl (DTSi6) or 2-ethylhexyl (DTSiEH) groups at their sp3 silicons as donors and 4,7-dithienyl-2- octyl-[1,2,3]benzotriazole (DTBTz) as an acceptor. From detailed analyses of these two polymers with differential scanning calorimetry (Dsc), UV-vis spectroscopy, cyclic voltammetry (CV), organic field-effect transistor (OFET) hole mobility measurements, and atomic force microscopy (AFM), we conclude that the branched alkyl chains at the sp3 silicon improve the interactions of the polymers. The findings somewhat contradict the common knowledge of polymer chemistry that branched (bulky) alkyl chains usually disrupt interchain polymer interactions; we suggest that the branched alkyl chains attached at the sp2 or sp3 position will have an entirely different effect on the polymer interactions. The wellknown PSBTBT, which consists of dithienosilole (DTsi) and benzothiadiazole $( \mathrm { B T } ) ^ { 1 7 }$ and exhibits a PCE of over $5 \%$ has alkyl side chains at its sp3 silicon. Therefore, we selected DTSi as the donor part (Scheme 1). Benzotriazole (BTz) was chosen as the counterpart because it is an analog of BT with an alkyl side chain that assists the dissolution of the polymers in common solvents. Furthermore, to increase the planarity of the polymer, thiophene moieties were incorporated between DTSi and $\mathrm { B T z . ^ { 1 8 } }$ The photovoltaic properties of OPV devices based on the polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films annealed at different temperatures were also examined. PDTSiEH-DTBTz, which possesses branched alkyl chains at its sp3 silicon, was found to exhibit better photovoltaic properties.

# EXPERIMENTAL

# General

All chemicals were purchased from commercial suppliers (Wako Pure Chemical, TCI, Kanto Chemical, and Aldrich) and used as received without further purification unless otherwise noted. Air- and water-sensitive reactions were carried out under an argon flow atmosphere by using standard Schlenk techniques. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) (super dehydrated, Wako) was used for lithiation reactions. Column chromatography was carried out using Wakogel C-300 silica gel. Size exclusion chromatography was carried out using Bio-Beads s-X1 Beads (Bio-Rad) with THF as an eluent.

# Instruments

The $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra were measured on a DRx-600 spectrometer (Bruker Biospin). Chemical shifts were calibrated using tetramethylsilane at 0 ppm for $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR. UV-vis spectral measurements were performed on a UV-3600 spectrophotometer (SHIMADzU) for solutions and a U-3010 spectrophotometer (Hitachi) for thin films. CV was carried out on an ALS 624D electrochemical analyzer (BAs) under an argon flow atmosphere. Tetra-n-butylammonium hexafluorophosphate $( \mathrm { T B A P F } _ { 6 } , \ 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { M } )$ was used as a supporting electrolyte. $\mathrm { T B A P F } _ { 6 }$ was recrystallized from ethanol. The collected crystals were dried under vacuum at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ A conventional three-electrode cell was used with a platinum (Pt) working electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode. The potentials were measured with respect to the $\mathrm { A g / }$ $\mathrm { A g N O } _ { 3 }$ (0.01 M acetonitrile solution) reference electrode. Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) and DSC were performed with an EXSTAR TG/DTA 62OO and an EXSTAR DSC 6220 (SII NanoTechnology), respectively, under nitrogen flow. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was carried out using a Shodex GPC-101 (Showa-Denko) equipped with a guard column (Shodex GPC KF-G) and two $3 0 \mathrm { - c m }$ mixed columns (GPC KF-806L) with an internal differential refractive index detector eluted with THF at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at a flow rate of $0 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ $\operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and calibrated by polystyrene standards. Work functions of the films were obtained on an AC-3 photoemission yield spectrometer (RIKEN KEIKI). Theoretical calculations based on density functional methods were performed with Gaussian09 program.19 Becke's three-parameter2 gradientcorrected functional (B3LYP) with 6-31G(d,p) basis was used to optimize the geometry. The obtained structures were depicted with Mercury program obtained from the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center.

# Fabrication and Characterization of OFETs

OFETs measured to estimate hole mobilities of the polymers annealed at room temperature (RT) and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ were fabricated in a top-contact geometry. A substrate of glass/Au gate electrode/Parylene-C insulator was prepared using methods described in a previous article.21 The polymers were spin coated from an $o$ -dichlorobenzene (oDcB) solution onto the Parylene-C layer. The substrate was transferred to a $\Nu _ { 2 }$ glove box and then dried at RT or $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 \ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ on a hot plate. Source drain electrodes of Au $[ 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ]$ were thermally evaporated through shadow masks. The length and width of the channels were fixed at $7 5 \ \mu \mathrm { { m } }$ and $5 \ \mathrm { m m }$ .respectively. OFET measurement was conducted under vacuum conditions by using a Keithley 2636A System Source Meter.

# Fabrication and Characterization of OPVs

The OPVs were fabricated in the following configuration: ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer/LiF/Al. The patterned ITO (conductivity: $1 0 \Omega ^ { - 2 \cdot }$ glass was precleaned in an ultrasonic bath of acetone and ethanol and then treated in an ultraviolet-ozone chamber. A thin layer $[ 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ]$ of PEDOT:PSS was spin coated at $3 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ and subsequently dried at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ on a hot plate under air. The substrate was transferred to a $\Nu _ { 2 }$ glove box and then dried again at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ on a hot plate. An oDcB solution of the polymer:PcBM blend was subsequently spin coated onto the PEDOT:PsS surface, forming an active layer. The thicknesses of the blend films and PEDOT:Pss layers were measured using an automatic microfigure-measuring instrument (Surfcorder ET200, Kosaka Laboratory). To investigate the effects of the blend ratio and thickness of the active layers on photovoltaic performance, the active layers were formed by spin coating from solutions of polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blends, whose compositions varied from 1:1 to 1:2 (weight ratio). Substrates with the active layer were also dried at RT, $7 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ in the $\Nu _ { 2 }$ glove box. Finally, LiF (1 nm) and Al $\left[ 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } \right]$ were deposited onto the active layer by means of conventional thermal evaporation at a chamber pressure lower than $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ Pa, which provided the devices with an active area of $2 \times 2 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ . The current densityvoltage $\left( J - W \right)$ curves were measured using an OTENTO-sUN III Voltage Current Source/Monitor under $\mathrm { A M } \ 1 . 5$ solar simulated light irradiation of $1 0 0 \ : \mathrm { \ m W \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ (Bunkoukeiki). The photocurrent action spectra and incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPcE) were measured using a CEP2000 system (Bunkoukeiki).

# Synthesis and Thermal Stability

The two polymers, PDTSi6-DTBTz with two hexyl chains (P1) and PDTSiEH-DTBTz with two 2-ethylhexyl chains (P2), were synthesized by the Stille coupling reaction of two precursors of dibromodithienosilole and di(stannylthienyl)benzotriazole in toluene (Scheme 2). The reaction mixtures were poured into MeOH to obtain crude polymers as precipitates, before successive Soxhlet extractions were carried out with acetone and THF for P1 and acetone and DcM for P2, to remove polymers with short conjugation. Given that P1 has poor solubility in DCM, THF was used to extract P1. The THF fraction was further passed through a column of BioBeads with THF and then through silica gel with DCM and THF to remove oligomers with low molecular weight. After the solvents were evaporated, the residue was dissolved in a minimal amount of DCM, and the solution was again poured into MeOH to obtain the polymers as precipitates. The molecular weights $[ M _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and $M _ { \mathrm { w _ { \mathrm { \ell } } } }$ and polydispersity indices (PDIs) of the polymers were analyzed by GPC calibrated with polystyrene standards, as shown in Table 1. The values of $M _ { \mathrm { n } }$ of P1 and P2 were determined to be 8.4 and $1 5 . 3 ~ \mathrm { k g }$ $\mathrm { m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ , with corresponding PDIs of 1.85 and 2.30, respectively. Thermal analyses of the polymers were carried out with TGA and DSC under nitrogen flow, as shown in Figure 1. The TGA measurements revealed that the $5 \%$ weight loss temperatures $\left( T _ { \mathrm { d } } \right)$ of P1 and P2 were 439 and $4 2 7 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ respectively, showing good thermal stability of the polymers. Two thermal transitions were observed at 60 and $1 8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for P1 and 55 and $2 2 8 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for P2, respectively, in the DSC profiles of the heating process. Following the DSC analysis on other conjugated polymers,13,25 the transitions at low temperature were attributed to melting of side chains induced by motion of alkyl chains to give glass state, and high-temperature transitions were assigned to melting of polymer backbone.

# Optical Properties

The absorption spectra of P1 and P2 were measured in oDCB and DcM solutions as well as in solid films, as shown in Figure 2. The corresponding data are summarized in Table 2. The polymer solutions were prepared by diluting thick solutions of oDCB and DCM. The thick oDCB solutions were stirred at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 2 days to disperse the polymer chains in the solvent. The absorption spectra of P1 and P2 in oDCB showed an absorption band with maxima at 553 $( \varepsilon = 5 . 1 \times$ $1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ and $5 7 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $( \varepsilon = 5 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ respectively [Fig. 2(a)]. The absorption spectra in DCM showed absorption maxima at $5 5 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $[ \varepsilon = \bar { 5 } . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ for P1 and $5 7 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ $[ \varepsilon = 6 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ for P2 [Fig. 2(b)]. The red shift of the absorption maxima of P2 is also explained by the difference in the molecular weights. According to a study by Otsubo and coworkers, the 12-mer and 18-mer of polythiophenes in DCM showed absorption maxima at 506.5 and $5 1 7 . 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively, with a difference of $1 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $_ { ( = 0 . 0 5 }$ eVJ.26 The $M _ { \mathrm { n } }$ of P1 and P2 corresponded to the 11-mer and 19-mer, and the difference in the absorption maxima of P1 and P2 was $1 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $_ { ( = 0 . 0 7 }$ eV) in oDCB and $1 5 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ $_ { ( = 0 . 0 6 }$ eV) in DCM, yielding values similar to those in the literature. The $\pi - \pi$ stacked structure appeared prominently in the absorption spectra obtained in the solid films. Figure 2(c,d) shows the absorption spectra of the P1 and P2 films annealed preliminarily at RT, $7 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ The P1 film showed an absorption maximum at $5 7 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ with a weak shoulder around $6 1 5 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ The P2 film showed absorption maxima at 576 and $6 1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ .Similarly, Min et al. reported that P2 (which the authors referred to as PDTS-DTBTz) showed a strong shoulder peak regardless of the molecular weight difference.18 Figure 3 shows the optimized structures of donor moieties of the polymers, that is, DTsi6 and DTSiEH, calculated with Gaussian 09 at the B3LyP/6- 31G(d,p) level of theory. In DTSi6, hexyl chains protruded perpendicularly from the $\pi$ -plane of DTSi ring, so that the polymer based on DTSi6 seems to be able to readily stack each other [Fig. 3(a)]. In DTSiEH, there can be in two conformations for the ethyl groups when the hexyl groups protrude linearly; one is above and below the carbons next to the silicon with a distance between terminal carbon of ethyl group and $\pi$ -plane of $4 . 1 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ to settle unfavorable conformation to stack each polymer [Fig. 3(b)], and the other is apart from $\pi$ -system to facilitate interchain $\pi - \pi$ interactions [Fig. 3(c)]. The calculated heat of formation for DTsiEH in Figure 3(b) conformation was $7 . 0 ~ \mathrm { k J ~ m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ more stable than that in Figure 3(c) conformation, and the difference is greater than typical van der Waals interactions $( < 5 \mathrm { \ k J \ m o l ^ { - 1 } } )$ but smaller than typical $\pi - \pi$ interactions $( 0 { - } 5 0 ~ \mathrm { \ k J ~ \ m o l ^ { - } } ) , ^ { 2 7 }$ implying that $\pi - \pi$ stacking of each polymer occurs without interruption by the ethyl groups and DTSiEH moiety in P2 is in Figure 3(c) conformation. The obtained result indicates that P2 possesses intrinsically stronger interchain $\pi - \pi$ interactions than P1, presumably because it benefits from the stronger van der Waals interactions and closer packing due to crowded ethylhexyl groups. P1 showed no change in the thin-film absorption spectra, whereas heating of P2 increased the absorbance around $5 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ while decreasing the peak intensity at $6 1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . The intensity at $6 1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ relative to that of the absorption maximum at $5 7 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ is plotted in Figure 2(e). From the decline in the intensity, we roughly estimated the disordering ratio of the ordering structure in the P2 film by thermal annealing to be $1 \%$ at $7 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $2 \%$ at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ Given that the glass transition of P2 was observed at around $5 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ thermal treating possibly caused melting of the side chain to disturb the stronger $\pi - \pi$ stacking of P2. The onsets of the absorption spectra for the solid films of P1 and P2 were 681 and $6 8 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively, each corresponding to the band gap energy $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { \mathrm { o p t } } )$ of $1 . 8 2 ~ \mathrm { e V } .$ The absorption bands in longer wavelength regions are governed by the $\pi - \pi$ stacked polymers with enough long effective conjugation, which are $6 1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ for both P1 and P2. Therefore, the absence of any difference between the band gaps of P1 and P2 indicates that the band gap is not determined by the alkyl side chain but solely by the polymer backbone.

# Hole Mobilities

To further investigate the morphologies of nonbranched P1 and branched P2, we attempted to extract the electrical properties of polymers that arise from the morphology differences. To estimate the hole mobilities of the polymers, OFETs with a top-contact geometry were fabricated according to the reported methods.?2 The measured thin films were annealed at RT and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ . Figure 5(a) shows the typical drain current-voltage relationships in the gate voltage range from 0 to $- 8 0 ~ \mathrm { V }$ for OFETs with P2 as an organic semiconductor annealed at RT. From the plot shown in Figure 5(b), the field-effect hole mobility was calculated to be $9 . 7 \ \times \ 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The other hole mobilities obtained were $1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for P1 at RT, $2 . 0 ~ \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for P1 at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $1 . 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 }$ $s ^ { - 1 }$ for P2 at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Reflecting on the structural ordering of P2 observed by the absorption spectra in solid films, we found that OFETs with P2 yielded hole mobilities higher by an order of magnitude than those with P1. According to the DSC analysis and thin-film absorption spectra treated at different temperatures, we found that thermal treatment disrupts the ordering structure in solid films of P1 and P2, resulting in lower hole mobilities when the OFETs of the polymers are treated at higher temperature. Hole mobilities treated at $1 1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ however, exhibited higher values than those treated at RT. Given that impurities significantly affect the performance of OFET devices, OFETs annealed at RT, which can include more solvent as an impurity than those annealed at $1 1 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathsf C ,$ could not display the intrinsic performance obtainable from the thoroughly solvent-excluded OFETs. This may be why the OFETs annealed at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ exhibited slightly higher hole mobilities $( 2 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ for P1 and $1 . 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for P2) than those treated at RT ( $1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for P1 and $9 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ $\nabla ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ for P2).

# Photovoltaic Properties

Photovoltaic properties were investigated by fabricating OPV cells with a general structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M } ( 1 { : } x , y \ \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { L i F }$ $( 1 \ \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { A l }$ $[ 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ]$ by using P1 and P2 as donors and [6,6]-phenyl- ${ \bf \cdot } { \bf C } _ { 7 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester $( \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M } )$ as an acceptor under illumination with 100 mW $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ of $\mathrm { ~ A M ~ } \ 1 . 5$ conditions (Table 4). $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ was selected as an electron acceptor because it exhibits strong absorption in the range of $4 4 0 { - } 5 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ to complement the absorption of the polymers. The values of $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of 1:1 devices (entries 4-8) were greater than those of 1:2 devices (entries 1-3). # Morphology

AFM measurements were performed to investigate the surface morphologies of the 1:1 polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films treated at RT and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Fig. 8). The $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C _ { 7 0 } B M }$ blend films at RT and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ exhibited smoother surfaces with rootmean-square (RMs) roughnesses of 0.35 and $0 . 3 3 \mathrm { n m }$ respectively. The smooth surface may be attributed to the weaker $\pi - \pi$ interactions in P1 (see Optical Properties section) to render P1 miscible with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ . On the other hand, the $\mathrm { P } 2 { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films yielded slightly larger domains with RMS roughness of $1 . 2 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ at RT and $1 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ at 110 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathbb { C } ,$ respectively, potentially reflecting the stronger $\pi - \pi$ interactions of P2 to agglomerate each component. There was no change in the morphology of $\mathrm { P } 2 { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films at RT and $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ In contrast, thermal treatment changed the morphology of $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C _ { 7 0 } B M }$ blend films, which may cause a decrease in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of P1-based OPVs.

# CONCLUSIONS

The branch effect of alkyl group at sp3 element in the $\pi$ conjugated polymers on their physical and photovoltaic properties and their morphology was investigated using two polymers based on dialkyldithienosilole and 4,7-dithienyl-2- octylbenzotriazole with linear hexyl (P1) and branched 2-ethylhexyl (P2) groups at their sp3 silicon. Optical and electrochemical studies revealed that branched alkyl chain does not affect their optical and electrochemical band gaps and HOMO-LUMO energies but their interchain interactions to exhibit stronger $\pi - \pi$ interaction structure in absorption spectra of P2 than that of P1. The polymers were thermally stable over $4 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and exhibited a glass transition at around $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Unlike the case of poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT), in which a linear alkyl chain attached at the sp2 carbon and its structural order is improved by thermal treatments, the $\pi - \pi$ stacked ordering structures in P2 were partially disrupted by thermal treatment, as revealed from the UV-vis measurements. The 1:1 blend of P1 and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ annealed at RT gave the best PCE of $1 . 6 9 \%$ with $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } = 6 . 6 6 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } .$ $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } = 0 . 5 8$ $\mathrm { V } ,$ and $\mathrm { F F } = 0 . 4 4$ under AM 1.5 conditions. Under similar conditions, the best PCE of the P2-based OPV cell was $2 . 0 3 \%$ with $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } = 6 . 3 8 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } .$ $V _ { 0 \mathrm { C } } = 0 . 5 5 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ and $\mathrm { F F } = 0 . 5 4$ . The AFM images of $\mathrm { P 1 { : } P C _ { 7 0 } B M }$ blend films showed different images before and after thermal treatment, which may cause not only side-chain melting but also backbone disordering, resulting in a reduction in the PCE as the FF and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ decrease. In contrast, $\mathrm { P } 2 { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films showed similar AFM images before and after thermal treatment. Owing to the stronger $\pi - \pi$ interactions of P2, disordering in $\mathrm { P } 2 { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend films by thermal treatment may afford no interruption of interpenetrating channels between electrodes and might not reduce $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ but only FF.
