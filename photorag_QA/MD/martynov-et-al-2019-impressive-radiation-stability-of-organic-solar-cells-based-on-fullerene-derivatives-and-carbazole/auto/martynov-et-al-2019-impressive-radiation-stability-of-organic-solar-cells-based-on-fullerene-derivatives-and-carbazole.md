ABSTRACT: We explored the radiation stability of carbazole-based electron-donor conjugated polymers, acceptor fullerene derivative [60]PCBM, and their blends as active layer components of organic solar cells. An exposure to y rays induced evident degradation effects in bulk samples of the pristine fullerene acceptor ([60]PCBM) and two investigated electron-donor conjugated polymers: PCDTBT and PCDTTBTBTT. The most severe radiation damage occurred in [60]PCBM as can be concluded from the significant losses in open circuit voltage, fll factor, and efficiency of photovoltaic (PV) devices comprising the exposed fullerene acceptor. # INTRODUCTION

Historically, solar cells based on crystalline silicon were the first to achieve success in both terrestrial and space applications. Photovoltaic (PV) modules designed for space missions, for example, communication satellites, are generally characterized using an AM0 solar spectrum.1-3 Solar cells based on GaAs and Si usually have high AMo efficiencies of $2 5 - 3 0 \%$ However, they are not superior with respect to the power-toweight and power-to-volume ratios, which are currently among the most important device parameters when considering their space applications.1,4

Inherent advantages of organic solar cells such as light weight and excellent mechanical flexibility make them very attractive for space missions, particularly, to power up small satellites at low geocentric orbits.' The power conversion efficiencies (PCE) of organic solar cells increased within the last decade up to $1 0 { - } 1 3 \%$ (measured with an AM1.5G spectrum), and there is a considerable potential for further improvement.2,5-10

The application of organic solar cells on satellites can be possible only if they can sustain harsh space conditions maintaining their decent efficiencies for a long time. Depending on the particular orbital mission, materials and devices are exposed to high vacuum, thermal cycling, solar radiation including high-energy UV, X-ray, and $\gamma$ radiation, and highenergy particles (electrons, protons, and heavy ions).4 Radiation stability of organic solar cells and their active components has been scarcely explored so far. It was shown that organic solar cells based on poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) and phenyl- $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester ([60]PCBM) can tolerate $\mathrm { X }$ ray radiation in doses of up to

$3 0 0 \ \mathrm { k r a d }$ $( 3 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y } )$ 4,11 The radiation-induced drop in the open circuit voltage after $1 3 0 0 \mathrm { k r a d }$ $\left( 1 3 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y } \right)$ exposure was correlated with the formation of a positively charged layer near the anode contact.l2 However, this loss mechanism can be suppressed by a proper design of the interfaces.13

Solar cells based on the blends of the carbazole-containing polymer PCDTBT and the fullerene derivative [60]PCBM showed impressive operation stability with estimated lifetimes of up to 10-15 years under terrestrial conditions.14-17 Radiation stability of these devices was explored very recently. It has been shown that X-ray irradiation leads to the formation of deep traps acting as recombination centers. Interestingly, the induced states were quenched upon thermal annealing thus resulting in the healing effect.'s

Stability of organic materials and electronic devices with respect to y radiation received very little attention until now. However, y rays can cause significant aging effects in space due to their high energy and penetration ability. Here, we report a systematic study of the y radiation stability of individual electron-donor conjugated polymers and fullerene derivative [60]PCBM (Figure 1), their blends, and completed photovoltaic cells based on these materials.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

It has been shown previously that carbazole-containing conjugated polymers enable decent power conversion efficiencies and demonstrate excellent ambient and photochemical stabilit.17,19,20 Therefore, we selected polymers P1 (PCDTBT) and P2 (PCDTTBTBTT) as model systems for investigation of the radiation stability of organic-polymer-based electron donor materials (Figure 1). Synthesis and characterization of these polymers were reported previously.21-23 [60]PCBM was investigated as a representative fullerenebased electron acceptor material.

At the first stage of this work, we explored the radiationinduced effects in pristine organic semiconductors, which were exposed to 200 Gy of  irradiation $\left( E _ { \gamma } = 6 6 2 \mathrm { k e V } \right)$ provided by $^ { 1 3 7 } \mathrm { C s }$ source with a dose rate of $2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { G y / m i n }$ . Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was used to follow any evolution in the molecular weight characteristics of conjugated polymers and the fullerene derivative exposed to y rays. Figure S1 shows the GPC profiles of P1, P2, and [60]PCBM, which reveal just very minor if any changes after the irradiation with $\gamma$ rays. Probably, only P1 showed some increase in the average molecular weight due to a decrease in the content of the lowmolecular-weight fraction. However, since there is no shift of the GPC profile toward shorter retention times, the crosslinking of the polymer chains can hardly occur. Most probably, 200 Gy exposure was not sufficient to induce high enough concentrations of bond breaks, cross-linkages between the polymer chains, and dimerization/polymerization of the fullerene cages in [60]PCBM to be detected by GPC.

It is known that persistent radical species are formed in both conjugated polymers and fullerene derivatives under their longterm exposure to white (or solar) light. Accumulation of these radicals results in trapping of charge carriers and deterioration Of the performance of photovoltaic devices.425 Surprisingly, electron spin resonance spectroscopy (ESR) did not reveal the accumulation of stable radical species in all investigated materials after their exposure to y rays: the spectra recorded for pristine and exposed samples were virtually identical (Figure S2). This result is consistent with the GPC analysis data suggesting that both conjugated polymers and fullerene derivatives can tolerate well a radiation dose of 200 Gy without undergoing any massive radiochemical degradation leading to the accumulation of radical species.

Exposure of the samples to y rays might have a strong impact on the optoelectronic and electrophysical properties of organic semiconductor materials in thin flms. To verify this hypothesis, we deposited thin films of P1, P2, and [60]PCBM on the glass/ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates and exposed these samples to 500-6500 Gy of y rays. The absorbance of all films slightly increased after exposure (Figure 2). A similar effect was observed previously for conjugated polymers and attributed to radiation-induced enhancement of their crystallization.26 The photoluminescence (PL) of all materials was significantly quenched after $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y }$ exposure of the films, which might be either due to an enhanced molecular aggregation (as suggested by increased absorbance) or because of the accumulation of radiation-induced defects in the materials. The latter is the most probable explanation since defect accumulation and PL quenching were observed previously for organic semiconductors exposed to radiation.27,28

The most spectacular effects were observed while investigating the flms before and after radiation exposure using probe microscopy. Though the topography and phase images of the films did not reveal any significant changes upon radiation exposure, the observed strong evolution in local conductivity of the films was the most illustrative (Figures $S 3 -$ S5). Sets of representative current-voltage $\left( I { - } V \right)$ characteristics measured in several points for every sample are shown in Figure 2c,f,i. It is seen from Figure 2c that $I { - } V$ curves for thin films of P1 do not change significantly, which suggests that this polymer is quite tolerant to y rays. On the contrary, thin films of P2 showed a strong decrease in the current by more than an order of magnitude, which suggests that this polymer undergoes a severe radiation-induced degradation. Indeed, accumulation of defects behaving as deep traps for charge carriers increases the film resistivity and thus lowers the conductivity.

Interestingly, thin films of [60]PCBM demonstrated an opposite trend: current increased significantly after exposure of the films to $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y }$ and, especially, 6500 Gy of y rays. Such behavior is a very characteristic signature of a radiationinduced doping of the material leading to an increase in the density of charge carriers and improved conductivity. Similar effects were previously documented for other organic semiconductors.29 To summarize, [60]PCBM is also unstable under exposure to y rays, but its radiation-induced modification might even improve the electrical performance of this material.

Conclusions made considering the changes in the local conductivity of the polymer and [60]PCBM flms under radiation exposure were also supported by the Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) data. The surface potential of P1 films just slightly increased from $\scriptstyle - 6 3 0$ in average to $- 5 7 0 ~ \mathrm { m V }$ after 6500 Gy exposure (Figure S3). On the contrary, the average surface potential of P2 dropped dramatically from $- 5 2 0$ to $- 8 2 0 ~ \mathrm { m V }$ after 500 Gy exposure, thus evidencing a remarkable modification of electronic properties of this material induced by radiation (Figure S4). The surface potential of [60]PCBM films was rather stable and just slightly increased from $- 1 0 6 0$ to $- 1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { m V }$ (Figure S5). Thus, the obtained KPFM data strongly suggest that polymer P2 is much less tolerant to y rays than P1 and [60]PCBM.

In order to reveal the effect of $\gamma$ rays on the photovoltaic performance of organic semiconductor materials, we fabricated glass/ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer/ $\mathbf { \Delta } ^ { \prime } \mathbf { M g } / \mathbf { A l }$ solar cells using different combinations of the exposed and nonexposed polymers and [60]PCBM in the device active layer. Table 1 and Figure 3 show the evolution of the main device characteristics extracted from the current-voltage measurements due to exposure of polymers, [60]PCBM, or both of them to radiation $\left( 2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y } \right)$

Figure 3a shows that exposure of polymer P1 to y rays did not affect notably the performances of the solar cells. Interestingly, the solar cells based on both exposed materials $\mathbf { P 1 } \big ( + \big ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M } \big ( + \big )$ showed significantly reduced open circuit voltage as compared to the $\mathbf { P 1 } ( - ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M } ( + )$ series of devices, which illustrates a cumulative effect of the radiation exposure. Probably, the concentration of defects formed in P1 alone due to the radiation exposure is still below the threshold value affecting severely the characteristics of the $\mathrm { \bf P } \mathrm { \bf 1 } \big ( + \big ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { \bf P } \mathrm { \bf C B M } ( - )$ solar cell. However, these additional defects formed in the polymer phase increase the overall density of the trap states in the $\mathbf { P 1 } \big ( + \big ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M } \big ( + \big )$ blends, thus impacting their performance in comparison with the $\mathbf { P 1 } ( - ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P \tilde { C } B M } ( + )$ system.

It should be noted that the solar cells based on both exposed components $\mathbf { P 1 } \big ( + \big ) / [ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M } \big ( + \big )$ maintained ${ > } 7 5 \%$ of the performance delivered by pristine materials, which illustrates a decent stability of these materials with respect to $\gamma$ ray exposure. The situation is considerably different in the case of polymer P2: the application of the y ray exposed batch $\mathbf { P } 2 \left( + \right)$ in combination with both pristine $[ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M ( - ) }$ and exposed $[ 6 0 ] \mathrm { P C B M ( + ) }$ results in a ${ \sim } 3 0 \%$ decrease in the current density and ${ \sim } 1 0 \%$ reduction in the fill factor. Open circuit voltage is also dramatically affected in the case of using polymer and fullerene acceptor both exposed to $\gamma$ rays, thus leading to the drop of the solar cell efficiency by ${ > } 5 0 \%$ as compared to the value reached for pristine materials.

The obtained results suggested that y rays induce high concentration of ESR-silent defects (since they were not detected by ESR) in the materials ([60]PCBM and P2). On the one hand, the formation of such defects impairs the transport of free charge carriers leading to reduced FF in the photovoltaic cells. On the other hand, defect formation increases the density of states (DOs) within the band gap of the material, which are known to lead to significant $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ losses.30,31 Finall, the presence of defects functioning as deep traps results in trap-assisted recombination of charge carriers, thus decreasing $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of the photovoltaic cells.

While considering the data presented in Figure 3, one can notice that [60]PCBM is less tolerant to the action of $\gamma$ rays than P1. This seems to be opposite for the P2/[60]PCBM system where the impact of the fullerene exposure is negligible compared to the radiation-induced damage effects in pristine polymer P2. Therefore, the radiation stability of pristine materials decreases while going from P1 to [60]PCBM and then from [60]PCBM to P2.

Additional evidence for a strong impact of $\gamma$ radiation on the photovoltaic performance of P2 rather than P1 and [60]PCBM was provided by the external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements (Figure S6). The spectra of the P1/ [60]PCBM system showed just some random sample-tosample variations, while the maximal EQE value was within $56 \mathrm { - } 6 3 \%$ . On the contrary, EQE of the solar cells based on P2 dropped from ${ > } 6 0$ to ${ \sim } 4 0 \%$ when the polymer was exposed to y rays, which illustrates the effect of the trap-assisted recombination of charge carriers in the exposed samples leading to the decay of the current density. The effect of [60]PCBM exposure on the EQE of P2/[60]PCBM cells was very minor if any.

The obtained results reveal a significantly lower radiation stability of polymer P2 as compared to P1. This finding is surprising since both polymers are composed of the same building blocks (thiophene, benzothiadiazole, and carbazole units) and showed previously almost identical photostability when exposed to visible light.2' Probably, the radiation stability decreased because of the solubilizing alkyl groups attached to the thiophene rings in P2 or the presence of bithiophene fragments in the structure of this polymer, which are missing in P1. A more systematic study on a broader range of materials is required to reveal the chemical origin of the y-ray-induced degradation effects in conjugated copolymers.

At the next stage of our work, we used y rays to probe the stability of the multilayered thin film structures: the glass/ ITO/PEDOT:PSS/polymer-fullerene blend was prepared in the same way as organic photovoltaic cells. UV-vis spectra for the exposed fullerene/polymer blend films showed somewhat increased absorbance, thus demonstrating similar behavior to the films of pristine materials (Figure S7). The increase in the optical density was much more pronounced for P2/ [60]PCBM samples suggesting that they undergo stronger supramolecular ordering under radiation exposure than P1/ [60]PCBM films. The pristine blend films demonstrated very weak photoluminescence with the main contributions coming from [60]PCBM (in the case of P2/[60]PCBM samples), and also, some CT states formed in the P1/[60]PCBM system. However, these PL bands were completely quenched after exposure of the films to 500 Gy of y rays suggesting the formation of radiation-induced defects (Figure S7). The morphology of the fullerene-polymer blends did not evolve significantly upon radiation exposure (Figures S8 and S9). However, the average surface potential of P2/[60]PCBM films decreased by ${ \sim } 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m V }$ thus pointing to a strong modification of the electronic properties of the material by y rays. On the contrary, the P1/[60]PCBM system showed just a minor ( ${ \sim } 2 0$ $\mathrm { m V } ,$ ) decrease in the surface potential, which illustrates a superior radiation stability of this material combination. Notable also is the significant ( ${ \sim } 2 0$ fold) increase in the conductivity of the P1/[60]PCBM films, presumably due to the radiation-induced doping. On the contrary, the conductivity of the P2/[60]PCBM films remained unchanged upon radiation exposure, which might suggest that either doping does not occur (which is unlikely) or the doping effects are counterbalanced with the deep trap formation.

To explore the effect of y rays on the photovoltaic performance of the fullerene-polymer blends, we completed the solar cell architectures by depositing top $\mathbf { M g } / \mathbf { A l }$ electrodes by evaporation in a high-vacuum PVD chamber integrated inside a glove box with an inert atmosphere. It should be emphasized that we intentionally did not expose completed devices to y rays in order to investigate the intrinsic effects in the active materials rather than at the active layer/top metal electrode interface, which is known to be extremely sensitive to traces of oxygen and moisture. It should be noticed that y rays induce clearly different effects in P1/[60]PCBM and P2/[60]PCBM blends. In particular, a considerable decrease in the fill factor for P2/ [60]PCBM devices suggests that radiation exposure induces some problems with the transport of charge carriers, while in the case of the P1/[60]PCBM system, it works in an opposite direction. To gain a deeper understanding of these effects, we fabricated hole-only ITO/PEDOT:PSS/blend/ $\mathrm { { M o O } } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { { A g } }$ and electron-only ITO/Yb/blend/Sm devices, which were exposed to 500 Gy y rays before deposition of top electrodes. The charge carrier mobilities were estimated using the SCLC technique with the measurements performed in pulsed mode. In the case of the P1/[60]PCBM system, the hole mobility increased significantly from $2 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $8 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 } .$ which might be related to a radiation-induced doping effect. The electron mobility increased just slightly from $8 . 7 \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $9 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \dot { \mathrm { V } } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Importantly, the hole and electron mobilities become more balanced for the P1/ [60]PCBM system after radiation exposure, which might explain the experimentally observed increase in the device fill factor. The P2/[60]PCBM system demonstrated an opposite behavior since hole and electron mobilities became strongly unbalanced after radiation exposure, which is likely the cause for the experimentally observed decay of the fill factor in the corresponding solar cells. Indeed, the hole mobility for the P2/ [60]PCBM system strongly increased from $2 . 2 \times \mathrm { i } 0 ^ { - 3 }$ to $6 . 8 \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 3 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ , while the electron mobility slightly decreased from $1 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ to $1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } .$ Thus, the obtained results explain different trends in the evolution of the solar cell fill factors after radiation exposure of P1/[60]PCBM versus P2/[60]PCBM blends.

Decreases in the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ (observed for both types of devices) and $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ (occurred mainly in P1/[60]PCBM cells) might be related to the accumulation of defects in the active layers of the corresponding devices behaving as deep traps and facilitating recombination of charge carriers. To check this hypothesis, we investigated $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ versus light intensity $\left( I _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } \right)$ dependence for solar cells based on exposed and nonexposed P1/[60]PCBM and P2/[60]PCBM blends (Figure S10). The slopes of the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ versus $\ln \left( I _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } \right)$ dependences were equal to 1.77 and 1.57 $\mathtt { k T / q }$ for pristine P1/[60]PCBM and P2/[60]PCBM systems, respectively, thus suggesting that both bimolecular and monomolecular (e.g., trap-assisted) recombination mechanisms operate at open circuit voltage. Exposure of the blend films to y rays results in the increase of the slope up to ${ \sim } 2 \mathrm { k T } /$ q, thus implying that recombination of charge carriers at $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ occurs via the trap-assted monomolecular pathway.33 This result evidences the formation of deep traps in the fullerene-- polymer blends under radiation exposure, which governs the charge carrier recombination kinetics and reduces $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ and $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ of operating photovoltaic cells.

Additional evidence for the radiation-induced trap formation was obtained from the transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements. The exemplary transient profiles for the P1/[60]PCBM system are shown in Figure S11. It is clearly seen that the radiation exposure increases significantly decay time and leads to the appearance of the tailing effect. The characteristic time $\tau _ { 1 / 2 }$ (time when the TPV signal decays to $5 0 \%$ of the maximal amplitude) for the blends exposed to 6500 Gy $( \sim 1 3 ~ \mu s )$ was almost twice as long as that for the pristine films $( \sim 7 ~ \mu s )$ . A much stronger effect was observed for P2/[60]PCBM blends after their exposure to just $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y }$ $\tau _ { 1 / 2 }$ increased from ${ \sim } 5$ to $> 7 5 \ \mu \mathbf { s } ,$ thus confirming severe radiation-induced damage of this material combination leading to massive formation of defects. Thus, the results obtained using several complementary techniques proved that $\gamma$ rays indeed induce the formation of traps in the fullerene-polymer blends, which is the major factor affecting their photovoltaic performance.

Importantly, the solar cells based on the P1/[60]pCBM blends showed only minor degradation preserving $9 0 { - } 9 5 \%$ of their initial performance after absorbing ${ 5 0 0 } \ \mathrm { G y }$ of y rays. This result inspired us to apply higher radiation doses to these samples. The obtained results were quite impressive: the solar cells based on the P1/[60]PCBM blends showed an initial "burn-in' effect losing $7 - 1 0 \%$ of the initial PCE value after receiving ${ \sim } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { G y }$ and then their efficiency was maintained at a level of $8 5 { - } 9 0 \%$ even after exposure to as high as $6 5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { G y }$ doses of $\gamma$ rays (Figure 5). The experimental data were fitted well by an exponential function of the first-order kinetics commonly used to describe the aging behavior of organic solar cells (Figure 5). Similar results were also obtained for P1/ [70]PCBM blends, though the stability of this system was inferior compared to that of the P1/[60]pCBM devices (Figure S12).

The presented data suggest that reaching the $E _ { 8 0 }$ value for the P1/[60]PCBM, which is a dose of radiation required to decrease the solar cell efficiency down to $8 0 \%$ from the initial value, might require tens of thousands of Gy, which illustrates the remarkable stability of organic photovoltaics with respect to the radiation. The origin of such outstanding radiation stability is probably related to the following two major reasons. First, organic materials are not highly crystalline, which prevents the formation of radiation-induced defects in the crystal lattice, for example, ion vacancies or atom displacements. Second, organic materials intrinsically have a considerable number of defects of different types: structural defects, crosslinking or branching sites, partial doping due to oxidation, conformational or morphological disorder, and so on. Therefore, exposure of such materials to radiation leads to a relatively minor increase in the density of defects compared to inorganic semiconductors, where the defect concentration might increase by several orders of magnitude after their exposure to y rays.

According to the estimations provided by NASA, the satellites at Earth's orbit must withstand an unshielded radiation dose of up to $1 6 0 ~ \mathrm { G y }$ per year.34 If this estimation is correct, the solar cells based on P1/[60]PCBM blends can operate for 40 years while maintaining $9 0 \%$ of their initial efficiency value. Of course, this value might be overestimated since we do not consider here other stress factors such as visible light and heat, which can also strongly contribute to the degradation of organic solar cells. Nevertheless, there is real hope for achieving operation lifetimes of ${ \tt > } 1 0 $ years, which is a good benchmark for practical applications.

The revealed impressive radiation stability of P1/ [60]PCBM blends allows one to consider seriously organic solar cells as a promising PV technology for space applications. Indeed, these devices, when based on optimal material combinations, can deliver decent power conversion efficiencies of $1 0 { - } 1 2 \%$ while having ultralow weight, thus showing a record-high specific energy production of $1 0 \mathrm { ~ W ~ g ^ { - 1 } }$ or even beyond that.35

# CONCLUSIONS

To summarize, we have explored the effect of $\gamma$ radiation on the stability of organic solar cells and their components using two model electron-donor conjugated polymers and electronacceptor fullerene derivative [60]PCBM. It has been shown that exposure of pristine bulk samples of polymers and [60]PCBM to 200 Gy y rays did not lead to massive radiochemical degradation effects; for example, crosslinking of molecules and formation of stable radical species were not observed. However, the performance of the exposed materials in organic solar cells was significantly impaired due to the formation of traps. Exposure of thin films of fullerene-polymer composites did not lead to significant losses in their photovoltaic performance even when the absorbed radiation dose reached ${ 5 0 0 } \ \mathrm { G y } ,$ thus suggesting that blending donor and acceptor materials together improves dramatically their radiation stability. The most stable system based on PCDTBT/[60]PCBM blends maintained approximately $9 0 \%$ of the initial efficiency after being exposed to an impressive radiation dose of 6500 Gy. To the best of our knowledge, this represents an example of the record-high radiation stability ever reported for organic solar cells. Considering an average annual radiation dose of 160 Gy according to the NASA estimations for satellites at geocentric Earth orbits, the organic solar cells based on PCDTBT/[60]PCBM blends hold a promise to deliver lifetimes well above 10 years.

The revealed here impressive radiation stability of P1/ [60]PCBM blends in combination with the demonstrated previously record-high specific energy production of ${ \tt > } 1 0$ w $\bar { \mathsf { g } } ^ { - 1 }$ features organic solar cells as a promising PV technology for space applications.'5 Probably, organic solar cells can be considered as sources of power for small "nanosatellites" or can even be used as deployable solar sails in ambitious space missions.
