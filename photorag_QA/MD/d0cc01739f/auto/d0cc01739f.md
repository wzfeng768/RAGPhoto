# An asymmetric end-capping strategy enables a new non-fullerene acceptor for organic solar cells with efficiency over $10 \% \dagger$

Two different terminal groups, rhodanine-flanked benzo[c][1,2,5] thiadiazole (BR) and 2-(5,6-difluoro-3-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-inden1-ylidene)malononitrile (IM2F), were connected to an indaceno[1,2- b:5,6-b0 ]dithiophene (IDT) core to construct a new non-fullerene acceptor (IDTBF). Solar cells based on this acceptor exhibited promising photovoltaic performances with a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of up to $1 0 . 4 3 \%$ .

In the last decade, small molecule (SM) non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) that can replace fullerene derivatives (e.g. phenyl- $\mathrm { \cdot C _ { 6 1 } } /$ $\mathrm { C } _ { 7 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester $\left( \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } / \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \right)$ or indene- ${ \bf \cdot C } _ { 7 0 }$ bisadduct (ICBA)) in organic solar cells (OSCs) have garnered increasing attention in light of their outstanding photovoltaic performance.1–6 So far, power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) over $1 6 \%$ have already been achieved in the polymer donor/SMNFA systems.7,8 To further enhance the PCE values, various molecular engineering pathways have been paved, which led to the development of new NFAs. As an emerging kind of NFA, asymmetric NFAs, which were categorized according to their molecular symmetry, have made rapid progress in the past four years.9 Compared to their symmetric counterparts, asymmetric NFAs exhibit a higher intermolecular binding energy and larger dipole moment, both of which are beneficial to the reinforcement of donor/acceptor interaction, and hence increase the fill factor (FF) and PCEs of OSCs.9–11

Generally, a typical A–D–A structured calamitic NFA molecule is constructed with two main segments: a central electrondonating core (D) and an end-capping electron-accepting group (A). In most reports, great efforts have been devoted to designing certain new asymmetric donor-cores in order to construct the corresponding asymmetric NFAs. Only very limited literature about the asymmetrification of NFAs via terminal group (TG) modulation can be found.12–16 Besides the synthetic difficulties of attaching two different TGs to the central core, this situation might be attributed to the scarcity of excellent TGs. Thus far, only two kinds of TGs are capable of facilitating high performance binary OPV devices with PCEs of over $1 1 \%$ : 2-(3-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-inden-1-ylidene)malononitrile (IM) and its analogues, plus rhodanine-flanked benzo[c][1,2,5]thiadiazole (BR) and its analogues. At present, IM, especially its difluorinated derivative 2-(5,6-difluoro-3-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1Hinden-1-ylidene)malononitrile (IM2F), is recognized as one of the best TGs for NFAs.8 Hence, IM2F with its analogues began to be adopted in designing asymmetric NFAs as well.10,11 Regarding BR-based NFAs, although PCE records of their devices relatively lag behind, their unique advantages in reducing voltage losses have also attracted enormous attention.17–19 Yet surprisingly, BR-based asymmetric NFAs remain unreported.

In this contribution, we present a new asymmetric NFA, IDTBF (Scheme 1). Two distinguished TGs, BR and IM2F, were consequently connected to a classic indaceno[1,2-b:5,6- $b ^ { \prime }$ ]dithiophene (IDT) core to form the molecule. The device based on the polymer donor PM6 and IDTBF exhibits a promising PCE of $1 0 . 4 3 \%$ , which provides a new design strategy for asymmetric NFAs.

The synthetic procedure of IDTBF is depicted in Scheme S1 $\bigl ( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \bigr )$ . The brominated carbaldehyde of IDT (4) was prepared by the lithium–halogen exchange of the commercially available di-brominated IDT monomer 3, followed by quenching with anhydrous DMF in a yield around $6 5 \%$ . Then, the end-capping BR group was introduced via a 4-step continuous reaction: firstly, 1,3-propanediol acetal was synthesized to protect the aldehyde group of intermediate 4; secondly, the lithium–halogen exchange was performed again, followed by quenching with trimethyltin chloride; then, the freshly prepared tin reagent of IDT was subjected to a Pd-catalyzed Stille coupling reaction with brominated BR (compound 2); finally, diluted HCl was added for de-protection to obtain the aldehyde 5. It is worth noting that the aldehyde 5 exhibits poor stability, especially after being adsorbed onto the silica gel. So, we chose to run a flash column only once to obtain the crude aldehyde 5 without further purification. Finally, the IM2F group was introduced via a Knoevenagel condensation to yield IDTBF. The high-purity target compound was fully characterized by $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ , $^ { 1 3 } \mathrm { C }$ , and $^ { 1 9 } \mathrm { F }$ NMR and high-resolution mass spectrometry (see the $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ). In addition, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Fig. S1, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ) shows that IDTBF exhibits good thermal stability and decomposes $5 \%$ loss) only above $3 4 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .

The light harvesting ability of IDTBF was characterized by using UV-vis spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. S5 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , the diluted chloroform solution of the SM acceptor shows a narrow absorption band ranging from ca. $6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ to $7 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . While in the thin film (Fig. 1a), not only did the absorption peak notably red-shift from $6 7 5 ~ \mathrm { { \ n m } }$ to $7 2 3 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ , but also a significantly broadened absorption band ranging from ca. $6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { \ n m } }$ to $8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ was observed. And the formation of vibronic features present at ca. $6 5 5 ~ \mathrm { { \ n m } }$ in the thin film absorption profile suggested the ordered aggregation of acceptor molecules. Overall, the thin-film absorption of IDTBF makes it spectrally complementary with the wide band-gap polymer donor, which has a main absorption range of ca. 400–650 nm. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used to investigate the energy levels of the SM acceptor. The film of IDTBF on a glassy-carbon electrode was characterized in 0.1 mol $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { B u } _ { 4 } \mathrm { N P F } _ { 6 }$ acetonitrile solution at a scan rate of $5 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ m V ~ } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . As shown in Fig. 1c and Table 1, the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of IDTBF determined by CV were $- 5 . 6 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 3 . 8 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively (Fig. 2).

To inspect the photovoltaic performance of the SM acceptor, organic solar cells were fabricated and characterized. Based on the excellent performance of P3HT:o-IDTBR-based solar cells, polymer donor screening was also started from P3HT.22 However, the P3HT:IDTBF combination only gave a PCE record of $1 . 3 5 \%$ with an inferior $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ ( $\mathrm { 6 . 5 5 \ m A c m ^ { - 2 } } )$ ) and fill factor $\left( 3 6 . 0 7 \% \right)$ . The classic low bandgap polymer donor PCE10 was piloted as well, and it gave a slightly enhanced PCE of $4 . 8 1 \%$ (Fig. S7 and Table S3, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ).23 Finally, PM6 was chosen as the ultimate donor due to its suitable energy levels and complementary absorption with IDTBF (Table 1 and Fig. 1a), and, more importantly, its high compatibility with the low bandgap NFAs.20,24

The carrier mobility was fitted from the dark $J { - } V$ curves using the space charge limited current (SCLC) model. As Fig. S10 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ shows, the hole- $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } \right)$ and electron- $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } \right)$ mobilities of PM6:IDTBF blend are $3 . 0 2 \times { { 1 0 } ^ { - 4 } }$ and $2 . 5 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. The well-balanced $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ value (1.19) of the PM6:IDTBF-based device can guarantee efficacious charge transport and collection, consequently leading to an improved $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF.

For PM6:IDTBF combination, devices with the conventional architecture of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer/Phen-NaDPO/Ag were fabricated (device area of $0 . 1 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ). The device optimization conditions are detailed in the $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ (Section S6). The current density–voltage $\left( J - V \right)$ curve and the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum of the optimized device are depicted in Fig. 2. In the champion device, a high PCE of $1 0 . 4 3 \%$ was realized along with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ up to $0 . 9 4 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ , a $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 6 . 9 8 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and a FF of $6 5 . 1 4 \%$ . For a further in-depth morphological investigation on the active layer, atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) experiments were performed. Fig. 3 shows the AFM and TEM images of the optimized PM6:IDTBF blend film. As can be seen in Fig. S11 (ESI†), after being blended, the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the active layer drastically changed from 1.40 (PM6 pristine film) and 0.68 (IDTBF pristine film) to 6.23 (PM6:IDTBF). The increased roughness demonstrated a large domain size and phase separation in the D:A blend, indicating that the miscibility of PM6 and IDTBF is unsatisfactory and the presence of trap assisted recombination.28,29 These features were also revealed in the TEM images in Fig. S12 (ESI†).

In summary, a new non-fullerene acceptor, IDTBF, featuring two different kinds of terminal group, BR and IM2F, was designed and synthesized. After being employed in OSCs with a polymer donor, PM6, a PCE of $1 0 . 4 3 \%$ with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 9 4 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 6 . 9 8 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ was achieved. This result paves a new way in the molecular design of NFAs for OSCs. More work on device optimization and molecular modification of IDTBF is underway in our laboratory.
