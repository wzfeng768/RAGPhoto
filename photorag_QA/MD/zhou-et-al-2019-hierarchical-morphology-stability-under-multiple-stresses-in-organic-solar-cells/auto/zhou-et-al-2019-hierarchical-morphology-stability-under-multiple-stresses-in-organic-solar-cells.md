ABSTRACT: Improving device stability is an imperative area of realizing commercial utilization for organic solar cells (OsCs). Morphology evolutions of solutionprocessable active layers have proved to be responsible for ubiquitous device degradation due to their profound impacts on charge generation, transport, and extraction. This Perspective aims to understand and promote device stability in terms of morphology control and evolution from the subnanoscale to hundreds of nanometers. We start with analyzing the role of molecular packing and aggregation within domains on device stability and then focus on the structure of phase separation stemming from donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains. Potential mechanisms of morphology evolution within the individual length scale and some related reinforcing strategies when suffering from heating, irradiation, and mechanical stress are discussed. Finally, we summarize and propose major limitations and future directions for further promoting research on device stability in OSCs.

# Device stability in organic solar cells is closely related to the hierarchical morphology of active layers.

Multilayer OsCs usually suffer from many external stresses under operating conditions, such as heating, irradiation, and mechanical stress, which lead to rapid efficiency loss during the initial several hours in most of the fresh devices. This degradation process is typically called "burn in' loss, probably leading to an efficiency loss ranging from $2 0 { \mathrm { ~ u p ~ } }$ to $6 0 \%$ in the different active layers. The following period shows a relatively steady decrease of efficiency, which can be maintained for thousands of hours or a longer time.16 This enables the estimation of device lifetime by extrapolating the limited data measured during hundreds of hours. For example, an excellent lifetime of 10 years under illumination in a nonfullerene-based blend had been exhibited recently, which is the best device lifetime ever reported. Besides, good thermal stability for more than $1 6 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ at high temperature in fullerene-based blends was also reported.17 To realize their commercial utilization, highly efficient OSCs should generally improve their device stability, at least achieve a similar lifetime with the reported best values. This needs thorough understanding of efficiency loss pathways and mechanisms under different external stresses.

The crucial photoelectric conversion process takes place within active layers, and thus, the important role of their phaseseparated morphology should be emphasized so as to achieve long-term stability. Adjusting morphology parameters has been found to effectively improve device stability, such as molecular order-induced better photostability in fullerene blend systems.19 Indeed, most of the active layers feature hierarchical morphology: long-range phase separation is derived from the incompatible donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains, while the individual domain consists of ordered molecular packing and amorphous aggregation. However, comprehensive analysis concerning the relationship between morphology parameters with different length scales and corresponding device stability is still absent. On the other hand, morphology parameters may sometimes function inversely on the device thermal/photostability and mechanical stability. For example, increased molecular order has been proved to be a benefit for the thermal stability and photostability, while the amorphous phase in blend films improves their ductility and mechanical reliability. This suggests that discussion and analysis regarding the synergetic effect of illumination, heating, and mechanical stress on morphology stability are needed.20

In this Perspective, we will focus on the relationship between morphology parameters with length scales from the subnanoscale to hundreds of nanometers and device long-term stability in OSCs. Potential mechanisms of morphology evolution within individual length scales when suffering from heating, irradiation, and mechanical stress are discussed. We also highlight some related reinforcing strategies to inhibit device degradation in terms of morphology control. After a short summary, some major limitations and future directions are raised to further promote studies on device stability in OSCs.

Thermodynamic Factors Influence Morphology Stability. One favorable characteristic of OsCs is their ability to fabricate their active layers using solution-processing techniques, e.g., roll-toroll, blading coating, or spin coating, in which bulkheterojunction (BHJ) morphology is constructed with donor/ acceptor (D/A) crashing out of blend solutions during the solvent evaporation process. In this scenario, the preformed morphology is typically trapped in a nonequilibrium state, rendering a likely evolution pathway toward a more thermodynamically stable morphology. A classical thermodynamic parameter, the Flory-Huggins interaction parameter $\chi$ (representation of miscibility), was recently introduced to evaluate the stability of the original morphology and the following evolution trend of mixed phases. On the basis of the $\chi - \phi$ phase diagram,21 the relationship between miscibility and morphology stability can be graphically understood. Excellent device performance is probably achieved when the miscibility $( \chi )$ and component content $( \phi )$ are both optimum to reach the percolation threshold. Meanwhile, if the blend system with miscibility is close to this percolation threshold, the stable morphology under operating conditions is likely realized.

Molecular Packing and Aggregation within Domains. The phaseseparated morphology in OsCs usually features hierarchical scale, including molecular packing and aggregation in the donorrich and acceptor-rich domains, as well as interpenetrating networks with plenty of D/A interfaces. To achieve stable morphology against heating, irradiation, and mechanical stress, these morphology parameters should be optimized to ensure charge generation and transport.

The photophysics processes are closely related to the domain structure of both the donor and acceptor phases. Solutionprocessing conditions and post-treatment of the active layer can significantly change the molecular packing and aggregation within domains.223 This leads to a necessary trial-and-error procedure to identify the optimized domain structure based on different D/A combinations. Fortunately, some universal domain features to decelerate device degradation regardless of the material category have been found, which pave the way to improve morphology stability against external stresses.

Many previous works have proved that molecular order is closely related to thermal stability and photostability of organic blend flms.24-27 Improving molecular order probably freezes the optimal morphology by inhibiting the diffusion of adjacent molecules, which provides effective pathways to achieve better thermal stability. Yang et al.24 found that BDTTID (the full name can be found in the Supporting Information) showed better crystalline-like structure than BDT3TID (Figure 1a), and the device based on a BDTTID:PCBM blend yielded a more stable and higher PCE (Figure 1b). Besides, photon absorption by PCBM molecules would lead to a disorder acceptor phase (Figure 1c, top path) in the PTB7-Th $\mathrm { \cdot P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend film, which destructes exciton diffusion and charge mobility. After improving the molecular order of the $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ phase, this robust blend film was rather stable against illumination (Figure 1c, bottom path), and the corresponding device stability was largely improved (Figure 1d). Indeed, a light-induced disorder phase is one of the possible factors leading to device degradation, while the residual oxygen in the domains should act as the catalyst to accelerate this degradation process. Fortunately, the increased molecular order has been found to successfully exclude oxygen from the domains and thus decrease the photooxidation rate in small molecules/fullerene blend system. Usually, thermal annealing was employed to improve molecular order, in which the annealing time appeared to be important for controlling the content of the amorphous phase. Besides, some useful methods were also developed to improve molecular order and inhibit molecular diffusion, which can be universally applicable to various polymer donors, such as sequential deposition of donor and acceptor25 and an upside-down drying method.26

Amorphous and ordered molecular aggregations coexist in the domains of BHJ blend films due to the semicrystalline characters of donors and acceptors. Typically, amorphous parts in blend flms can dissipate loading stress by modulating their coil conformations; these elastic domains largely contribute to favorable stretchability of whole blend films. On the other hand, ordered packing molecules within crystalline domains usually exhibit stiffness, and grain boundaries distributed in BHJ blend films provide large amounts of crack points when suffering from tension, leading to fracture. To realize efficient charge collection, crystalline domains are needed to construct interconnected electron/hole transport paths, which pave the way to high photocurrent. Extra amorphous domains inevitably hinder charge transport, though beneficial ductility may be available. This suggests that the trade-off between mechanical and optoelectronic properties must be intensively considered in terms of molecular packing of organic semiconductors. Park et al.29 have changed chemical units along the backbone in a thiophene-based copolymer (RP33), which simultaneously decreased the crystallinity and facilitated formation of $\pi - \pi$ stacking between intermolecular chains. Compared to its highly crystalline counterpart P3HT, those RP33 films showed rather stable charge transport with increased mechanical properties (Figure $2 \mathsf { a } \mathrm { - } \dot { \mathsf { d } } ,$ . This is due to the formation of polymer networks induced by $\pi - \pi$ stacking, which can dissipate stress energy and render interchain charge transport (Figure 2e). In addition to changing the chemical structure of a single polymer, blending of polymers with different crystallinity also could sufficiently maintain excellent charge transport without damaging the mechanical stability.30 Those results indicate that careful control of molecular packing probably offers the opportunities to overcome stiffness and frangibility of highly crystalline conjugated polymer films and simultaneously sustains favorable charge transport properties to a large extent.

The molecular packing within domains, e.g., molecular order and orientation, should be optimized to meet long-term thermal and irradiation stability, while amorphous and crystalline aggregation should be balanced to achieve compatible mechanical stability and device efficiency.

The crucial role of molecular orientation on charge separation and transport has been widely investigated in various OSCs. However, studies regarding its effect on the device stability are still in progress. For many polymer thin flms, molecular orientation will transform from face-on to edge-on when extending their film drying time or upon thermal annealing, suggesting that edge-on is potentially a more thermodynamically stable molecular orientation than face-on for some conjugated polymers, such as N2200.34-36 However, rare effort has been made to carefully investigate whether edge-on orientation indeed shows better stability. Recently, Brabec et al.33 found that the PBDB-T/ITIC device showed inferior photostability, and the reason was attributed to the transformation of the ITIC molecular orientation from more face-on to edge-on after illumination (Figure $^ { 2 \mathrm { f } , \mathrm { g } } ,$ . This indicates that both heating and irradiation can induce a change of molecular orientation in polymer and nonfullerene OSCs. The importance of molecular orientation in device stability, therefore, should be emphasized, and more thorough understanding of the modulation methods and inherent mechanisms is still needed.

As discussed above, increased molecular order could inhibit molecular diffusion and exclude oxygen in the thin films and thus contribute to better thermal stability and photostability, while crystalline aggregations also provide possible crack points when suffering from external tension. Under this scenario, all-polymer blend systems should be the best candidates, which have been proved to exhibit excellent thermal stability and mechanical property.31,32 The ultimate issue is to restrain the efficiency loss under illumination and achieve better long-term photostability. This needs careful control of the molecular order, as evidenced by the conclusions based on fullerene and small-molecule-based blend systems. Other possible methods of improving morphology stability against irradiation are still unclear because the light-induced morphology degradations and the underlying mechanisms at present largely remain open questions.

Structure of Phase Separation. Light-induced excitons in organic semiconductors feature high binding energy, and their sufficient dissociations need a driving force provided at the D/A interface. The BHJ active layer comprises plenty of $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interfaces to ensure charge generation, while those D/A interfaces also provide possible pathways leading to device degradation. The components and distribution at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface dramatically influence thermal and mechanical stability of organic blend films. Additionally, long-range phase separation probably takes place under operating conditions in case the D/A interface is unstable, which will damage the charge generation and transport. Therefore, interfacial structure and phase separation should be carefully controlled so as to realize stable morphology and device performance.

The effect of D/A interfacial structure on the device stability not only arises from the component distribution at the interface but also relates to the demixing trend of donor and acceptor molecules at the interface. Figure 3a shows the effect of the free and trapped fullerene molecules at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface on the photostability in a pristine PTB7-Th $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ device.37 Those unbefitting distributions of fullerene molecules probably acted as the catalyst to promote oxidation reaction within PTB7-Th domains, leading to device degradation. However, the extraction of those free and trapped fullerenes from polymer phases facilitated by additive BT substantially improved device photostability. This indicates that the interface structure should be primarily optimized to avoid device efficiency loss. On the other hand, in order to inhibit phase separation between donor and acceptor phases, a method referred to as "molecular lock" was proposed by Zhan and co-workers.39 They found that the formation of a hydrogen bond between the additive and donors could effectively freeze the morphology of active layers, which dramatically improved device thermal stability. Surprisingly, this method showed favorable universality to various donors with fluorine atoms along their backbones or side chains. A similar method using interfacial stabilizer PCB-C8oc to construct a stable D/A interface was also reported, in which PCB-C8oc could distribute between donor and acceptor molecules and thus prevent severe phase separation upon thermal annealing (Figure 3b).38 Indeed, both molecular lock and an interfacial stabilizer demonstrate a term referred to as a compatilizer by rendering a strong molecular interaction with both the donor and acceptor to construct robust D/A interfaces against phase separation.

D/A interfaces also provide possible crack points within blend films when suffering from mechanical stress. The chemical structures and molecular properties of materials used for OSCs largely influence interface stability between donor and acceptor phases. Usually, polymer/polymer blend films exhibit better stretchability than their fullerene counterparts (Figure 4a,b).4 A large plastic conformation and flexible polymer bridging chains during crack growth contribute to a large plastic zone at the crack tip, which leads to a ductile all-polymer blend film. However, a weak interaction between polymer-rich and PCBMrich domains results in a small plastic zone at the crack tip and a brittle PTB7-Th:PCBM blend film (Figure 4c,d). Indeed, PCEs of all-polymer blends are still lower than their small molecular acceptor counterparts, and combining those two kinds of acceptors to control the D/A interfacial property should provide good mechanical and photoelectric properties simultaneously. A ternary blend of PTB7-Th:P(ND12HD-T2)): $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ is a good example to demonstrate this strategy.4' To our best knowledge, mechanical stability concerning a nonfullerene acceptor, however, has seldom been studied recently. Considering their excellent device performance, a ternary strategy combining polymer and nonfullerene acceptors will potentially further obtain compatible mechanical and optoelectrical properties in OSCs.

Future directions on facilitating morphology stability of organic solar cells should focus on further revealing the morphology-device stability relationship and relevant mechanisms and extending studies of morphology stability to their realistic operating conditions.

Long-range phase separation of blend films under external stress is another main reason leading to severe device degradation. For example, the PBDB-T/ITIC-DM device exhibited the worst photostability, and the possible reasons were attributed to their chemical instability as well as the lightinduced phase separation in the blend film.33 However, the driving force of light-induced phase separation is still unclear. We speculate that the interaction between PBDB-T and ITICDM molecules should be large so as to easily promote demixing at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface. Besides, vertical phase separation of the fullerene-based blend film after illumination was observed. As shown in Figure Sa,b, donor molecules tended to aggregate toward the $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ interface, and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ molecules diffused to the surface in the PBDTTT-EFT: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend film after $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ of irradiation. On the contrary, the PBDTTT-EFT:EHIDTBR blend film showed rather stable morphology, as indicated by the similar element distribution before and after illumination (Figure Sc,d). Incorporation of solvent additive DIO is believed to be responsible for this vertical phase separation in fullerenebased blends. Indeed, solvent additives are usually employed to optimize BHJ morphology and achieve high device efficiency. However, residual additives within the BHJ morphology either exhibit unstable chemical structure or induce phase demixing upon illumination, which inevitably lead to device degradation. Using additives with stable chemical structure37 or exploring other methods to partly replace the role of additive, e.g., blade coating+2 should be beneficial to achieve long-term device stability.

Conclusions and Future Directions. The morphology of the active layer largely influences the original device efficiency and long-term stability when suffering from external heating, irradiation, and mechanical stresses. The increased molecular order of the BHJ blend film not only promotes thermal stability but also benefits the robust morphology against irradiation for fullerene-based blend films. However, the content of ordered domains should be reasonably controlled to meet favorable mechanical reliability as amorphous domains within blend films can dissipate external stress by modulation of molecular packing, which usually arises in polymer-based blends. The structure of the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface also influences morphology stability. In fullerene-based blends, the elimination of interfacial fullerene traps substantially improves photostability. The domain interface in an all-polymer blend exhibits a large plastic zone and thus contributes to favorable ductile films, while the fragile domain interface in small-molecule-based blends leads to poor flexibility and stretchability. In addition, vertical and lateral phase separation should be inhibited upon irradiation, which calls for careful selection of solvent additives and material structures. Overall, investigations regarding morphology stability at different length scales in OsCs have continuously developed for nearly the past decade, and many useful viewpoints have been obtained that promote device stability relying on materials design and processing methods optimization. However, some issues of morphology stability in OsCs still remain unclear, which inhibits rapid developments in this research field.

(i) More studies are needed concerning the pathways and mechanisms of promoting device stability in terms of morphology control. Many efficient OSCs with good device stability have been reported recently in all-polymer blends and nonfullerene blends. However, the underlying reasons, either chemical stability of active materials or robust morphology under external stresses, must still be investigated. A phase diagram based on Flory-Huggins interaction and component content predicts the possible metastable morphology for a certain D/A combination.21 However, it is still challenging to directly obtain an optimized and stable morphology simultaneously in efficient nonfullerene and all-polymer blends, considering their complicated demixing and crystallization process. Therefore, guidelines should be constructed by clarifying relationships among molecular structure, miscibility, crystallization, and phase separation.

(ii) Studies of morphology stability in OSCs should be extended to their realistic operating conditions. Most present studies are mainly carried out in devices with a small active area, while commercial utilization of an organic photovoltaic module needs a large device area. Whether those conclusions are suitable for large-area devices still remains unclear. Nolasco et al.44 recently investigated the device stability with a scaled active area and found that stability was inclined to improve in those scaled devices. This suggests that the active area really affects device stability. The intrinsic changes in morphology when scaling the active layer and their relationship with thermal, mechanical, and irradiating stability should be further identified. Besides, studies on outdoor device stability are also needed because wind speed, light intensity, and alternation of day and night all influence the morphology and device stability in OsCs.45-48

In general, the recently reported high PCE of ${ \sim } 1 7 \%$ predicts a bright future in commercial utilization of OsCs,4 and we emphasize that improved device stability will accelerate this process. Morphology control provides effective pathways to eliminate efficiency loss and obtain long-term stability. Continuous efforts are needed to reveal the morphologydevice stability relationship in both efficient nonfullerene and all-polymer blends, which aim to further advance the development of organic photovoltaics.
