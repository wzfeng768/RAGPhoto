# Over 18% Efficiency of All-Polymer Solar Cells with Long-Term Stability Enabled by Y6 as a Solid Additive

Zhongwei Ge, Jiawei Qiao, Yun Li, Jiali Song, Chen Zhang, Zhen Fu, Min Hun Jee, Xiaotao Hao, Han Young Woo, and Yanming Sun\*

Morphology control greatly influences the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and long-term stability of all-polymer solar cells (all-PSCs); however, it remains challenging owing to their complex crystallization behavior. Herein, a small amount of Y6 $( 2 w t \% )$ is introduced as a solid additive into a PM6:PY-DT blend. Y6 remained inside the active layer and interacted with PY-DT to form a well-mixed phase. Increased molecular packing, enlarged phase separation size, and decreased trap density are observed for the Y6-processed PM6:PY-DT blend. The corresponding devices showed simultaneously improved short-circuit current and fll factor, achieving a high PCE of over $18 \%$ and excellent long-term stability, with a $\tau _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime of $\yen 1206$ and an extrapolated $\tau _ { 7 0 }$ lifetime of $9 1 8 5 ~ \mathrm { h }$ at maximum power point tracking (MPP) conditions under continuous one-sun illumination. This Y6-assisted strategy is successfully applied to other all-polymer blends, demonstrating its universality for all-PsCs. This work paves a new way for the fabrication of all-PsCs with high efficiency and superior long-term stability.

# 1. Introduction

Solution-processed organic solar cells (OSCs) have undergone extensive research and improvement over the past decade owing to their low weight, high flexibility, and semi-transparency.[1-5] OSCs based on a combination of polymer donors and smallmolecule acceptors (SMA) have demonstrated high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of over $1 9 \%$ [6-8] approaching the commercialization threshold. In addition to the high efficiency, their long-term stability must also be considered for OsC commercialization. All-polymer solar cells (all-psCs), which contain a polymer donor and an acceptor as the active layer materials, have greater morphological stability than their SMA-based counterparts, which is advantageous for practical applications.[9-12] Significant efforts have been made to enhance the photovoltaic performance of all-PSCs. The current PCEs of all-PSCs are lower than those of SMA-based OSCs because of their low short-circuit current densities $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ and fill factors (FF).[13-15] The complex molecular interactions and crystallization behaviors of all-polymer blends make morphology control more difficult than in polymer donor/SMA systems.[16,17] It is well established that the post-treatment of blend films, additive addition, and layer-by-layer device engineering are effective approaches for adjusting the nanoscale morphology of OSCs and achieving optimal device performance.1821] Among them, the introduction of additives into donor and acceptor blend solution is a facile and effective method. 1,8-Diiodooctane (DIO) and 1-chloronaphthalene (1-CN) are commonly used as solvent additives,22-24] delivering remarkable success in improving device performance. However, the frequent incomplete volatilization of these high-boiling-point additives during annealing leaves residual solvent that can severely deteriorate the device stability.[25] Recently, solid additives presenting a simple posttreatment process and good morphology optimization have been developed.[11,26-30] Moreover, small-molecule solid additives can interact with donor and acceptor hosts to enhance molecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking and ordering.31-331 Although it is generally considered to involve charge transfer, the underlying mechanism remains unclear and requires further research, particularly for all-polymer blends.

In this study, we employed Y6 as a solid additive to PM6:PY-DT blend.34] Y6 can interact with PY-DT to form a mixed phase. Y6- processed blends had a reduced molecular binding energy; thus, the excitons that diffuse to the donor-acceptor (D-A) interface can be dissociated effectively with a small driving force. Regarding morphological control, Y6 can improve the molecular ordering, leading to enhanced $\pi - \pi$ stacking and crystallinity that improve charge mobility. # 2. Results and Discussion

The chemical structures of PM6, PY-DT, and Y6 are shown in Figure 1a. The UV-vis absorption spectra of the corresponding thin films are shown in Figure 1b. PY-DT absorbed in the $3 5 0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ range with a maximum absorption peak $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ at $8 0 9 \mathrm { n m }$ , showing complementary absorption to PM6. The $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ of the neat Y6 film was redshifted by ${ \approx } 2 0 \mathrm { n m }$ compared with that of PY-DT. The absorption profile did not change significantly with the addition of Y6 $( 2 \mathrm { \ w t \% } )$ to the PY-DT film (Figure S1a, Supporting Information), indicating good miscibility between PYDT and Y6. The $I _ { 0 - 1 }$ absorption peak of PM6 containing Y6 decreased, and its $I _ { 0 - 0 } / I _ { 0 - 1 }$ ratio increased from 1.04 to 1.09, which suggests a stronger molecular packing and aggregation.[35,36] An additional peak at $7 6 9 \mathrm { n m }$ was observed in the PM6 film containing Y6 (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). This phenomenon demonstrates the inferior miscibility between PM6 and Y6. However, the utilization of Y6 slightly increased the PY-DT absorption in the blend film while the absorption of PM6 was nearly unchanged, as shown in Figure 1c and Figure S1c (Supporting Information).

A series of organic photovoltaic devices (Opvs) with a common ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PM6:PY-DT/PNDIT-F3N/Ag architecture were fabricated to investigate the effect of adding Y6 on the photovoltaic performance of all-PSCs. The PM6:PY-DT device achieved a PCE of $1 6 . 9 8 \%$ , with an open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ of 0.951 V, a $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of $2 3 . 2 6 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and an FF of $7 6 . 8 \%$ . When Y6 $( 2 \mathrm { \ w t \% } )$ was added to the PM6:PY-DT blend, the FF and $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ improved significantly, yielding a respectable PCE of $1 8 . 0 2 \%$ , with a $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ of $0 . 9 4 5 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ a $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of $2 4 . 5 1 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , and an FF of $7 7 . 8 \%$ To the best of our knowledge, $1 8 . 0 2 \%$ is one of the highest PCEs reported for all-PSCs (Figure 1g; Table S1, Supporting Information). To determine the function of Y6, we fabricated devices based on PM6:Y6 $( 2 \mathrm { \ w t \% } )$ that achieved a PCE of $0 . 0 3 \%$ , with a $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ of $0 . 0 9 8 \mathrm { V } ,$ $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of $1 . 4 3 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and an FF of $2 5 . 3 \%$ . As a result, Y6 functions as an additive rather than a third acceptor component. All photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1, and the corresponding representative current density-voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ curves are shown in Figure 1d. The integrated $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ values, calculated from the external quantum efficiency (EQE), corresponded well with those obtained from the $J { - } V$ measurements. As shown in Figure 1e, the EQE values of devices containing Y6 increased in the $3 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ range. We also investigated the effect of different amounts of Y6 on the performance of all-PSCs, and the statistical efficiencies of the corresponding samples are shown in Figure 1f. The performance of the all-PSCs first increased and then decreased with increasing Y6 content, with an optimal Y6 concentration of $2 \mathrm { w t } \%$ (Figure S2 and Table S2, Supporting Information). To assess the scope of this strategy, we employed Y6 as a solid additive in other all-PSC systems, including PM6:PY-DT-F, PM6:PYF-T-o, and PM6:PY-C11. These all-PSCs devices containing Y6 showed obvious PCE improvements compared to that without Y6 (Figure S3 and Table S3, Supporting Information), demonstrating that the addition of Y6 is a universal and effective strategy for improving the performance of all-PSCs.

We performed contact angle measurements of Y6 and the donor and acceptor hosts to investigate the miscibility (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The contact angles of water and glyc erol on the neat films are summarized in Table S4 (Supporting Information). The surface tensions $( \gamma )$ of PM6, PY-DT, and Y6 were calculated according to Wu's model as 21.9, 27.3, and 27.0 $\mathrm { m N m ^ { - 1 } }$ , respectively. The Flory-Huggins interaction parameter $\chi$ was employed to estimate the degree of molecule miscibility using the equation $\chi = \left( \sqrt { \gamma _ { \mathrm { A } } } - \sqrt { \gamma _ { \mathrm { B } } } \right) ^ { 2 }$ [37] The calculated $\chi$ values of PM6/PY-DT, PM6/Y6, and PY-DT/Y6 are 0.297, 0.266, and $0 . 0 0 0 8 ~ \mathrm { m N ~ m ^ { - 1 } }$ , respectively. The small value obtained for PYDT with Y6 $( 0 . 0 0 0 8 \mathrm { ~ m N ~ m ^ { - 1 } } ,$ indicates perfect miscibility and the formation of a well-mixed phase.[38] The contact angles of films containing Y6 were also measured to assess different additive concentrations (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The values of the $\mathrm { P Y - D T + Y } 6$ films increased with increasing Y6 content (Table S4, Supporting Information). The calculated $\chi$ values for PM6/PY-DT $+ \Upsilon 6$ $( 2 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ , PM6/PY-DT ${ \mathrm { + Y } } 6$ $( 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ , and $\mathrm { P M } 6 / \mathrm { P Y } { \cdot } \mathrm { D T } { + } \mathrm { Y } 6$ $( 1 0 \mathrm { \ w t \% } )$ are 0.340, 0.680, and $1 . 1 9 0 \mathrm { m N } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively. These results suggest that excessive Y6 concentrations cause poor miscibility between the donor and acceptor, resulting in large phase separation that hinders charge transfer. Therefore, the efficiency of OsCs can deteriorate with excessive Y6 concentration (Table S2, Supporting Information). Differential scanning calorimetry (DsC) was performed to confirm the hypothesis of a well-mixed phase. As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the $\mathrm { P Y - D T + Y } 6$ blend exhibited only one melting peak $( 3 6 0 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , which is slightly different from that of pristine PY-DT. Our combined results clearly indicate the presence of a well-mixed phase between the PY-DT acceptor and Y6.

Solid additives greatly influence the morphology of the active layer, which is associated with device performance. Therefore, we employed atomic force microscopy (AFM) and grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) to determine the effect of Y6 addition on the morphology of thin films. AFM images of PM6:PY-DT blends with and without Y6 are shown in Figure 2a. The Y6-assisted PM6:PY-DT blend exhibited a rough surface with a larger root mean square (RMS) value $( 1 . 2 9 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ than that of the blend without Y6 $( 1 . 1 9 \mathrm { n m }$ . The fibril size of the blend increased after the addition of Y6, as shown in the AFM phase images (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The blend film with a high Y6 concentration exhibited a higher RMS value of $1 . 5 3 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S8, Supporting Information), which agrees with our contact angle measurements. GIwAXS measurements were performed to evaluate the molecular packing and ordering. The 2D GIWAXS patterns and corresponding line curves of pristine and blended films are shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information) and Figure 2b,c. PM6 films displayed a weak (010) $\pi - \pi$ stacking peak $( \bar { q } _ { z } = 1 . 6 6 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 } )$ and a (100) lamellar peak $( q _ { z } = 0 . 2 8 \mathrm { ~ \AA ^ { - 1 } } )$ in the out-of-plane (OOP) direction, and a strong lamellar (100) stacking peak $( q _ { x \gamma } = 0 . 2 8 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 } )$ in the in-plane (IP) direction, indicating that the films have a mixed face-on/edge-on orientation. Both PY-DT and Y6 presented strong (010) peaks $( q _ { z } = 1 . 6 6$ and $1 . 5 8 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively) in the OOP direction and (100) peaks $( q _ { x \gamma }$ $= 0 . 2 9$ and $0 . 3 9 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively) in the IP direction, demonstrating their face-on orientation with respect to the substrate. These results are summarized in Table S5 (Supporting Information). PM6:PY-DT showed a predominant lamellar (100) peak at $q _ { x \gamma } =$ $0 . 2 9 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the IP direction, which is associated with the PY-DT acceptor. The crystalline coherence length (CCL) of the blend increased from 98.99 to $1 0 3 . 7 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ , suggesting a slightly enhanced lamellar ordering of the acceptor in the blend films (Table S6, Supporting Information). In contrast, a CCL of $9 3 . 9 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ was obtained from the (100) peak of the Y6-assisted blend, which was slightly smaller than that of the acceptor, possibly because of the formation of a new well-mixed phase. Sharp $\pi - \pi$ stacking peaks were found at $q _ { z } = 1 . 6 6 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the OOP direction for blends with and without Y6. However, the CCL value increased from 19.36 to $2 0 . 7 1 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ after adding Y6, demonstrating the increased molecular packing in the blend with Y6.

Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) is considered a reliable method for characterizing the charge generation and transfer processes in nanoscale films. In this study, we used TAS to determine the mechanism of action of Y6 in the blend. PM6:PY-DT films with and without Y6 were pumped with an $8 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ laser to selectively excite the acceptor. As shown in Figure 3a-d, groundstate bleach (GsB) signals were observed in the $6 0 0 { - } 6 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ range, corresponding to the characteristic signals of PM6 GSB (Figure S10, Supporting Information) and a sign of hole transfer from the acceptor to the donor. When pumped with $8 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ light, the excitons in PY-DT transition from the ground state to the singlet excited state within 1 ps (Figure 3e). Subsequently, the GSB signals of the blends probed at $6 2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ appeared with the decay of signals at $8 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , confirming the hole-transfer process between PY-DT and PM6. The decay dynamic curves of blends probed at $8 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ were obtained from Figure 3f and fitted by a biexponential function to obtain the lifetime of exciton dissociation at the D-A interface $( \tau _ { 1 } )$ and exciton diffusion $( \tau _ { 2 } )$ [39] PM6:PYDT with Y6 showed a faster decay with smaller $\tau _ { 1 }$ $( 0 . 2 6 ~ \mathrm { p s } )$ and $\tau _ { 2 }$ (17.25 ps) compared to the blend without Y6 ${ { \tau } _ { 1 } } = 0 . 4 5$ ps and $\tau _ { 2 } = 2 5 . 6 7$ ps), indicating that a small amount of Y6 plays an important role in facilitating charge generation and transfer in the blend. The excited-state absorption (ESA) signals of the acceptor appeared in the near-infrared range at $8 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 3c,d). The corresponding dynamic decay curves, shown in Figure S11a (Supporting Information), revealed that PY-DT with Y6 decays slowly with a long exciton lifetime. Notably, new ESA features, absent in the neat films, appeared at $9 4 0 \mathrm { n m }$ after a few picoseconds of delay and then decayed together with the donor GSB features. Thus, we consider this to be the charge-transfer (CT) state.40] The blend with Y6 presented slightly longer CT state decay profiles (Figure S11b, Supporting Information), indicating more efficient charge generation at the interface. A long exciton lifetime commonly leads to a long exciton-diffusion distance $( L _ { \mathrm { D } } )$ . In the case of large phase separation, a long $L _ { \mathrm { D } }$ is a prerequisite for the generated excitons to reach the D-A interface and contribute to the photocurrent. We used pump fluence-dependent TAS measurements to evaluate the change in $L _ { \mathrm { D } }$ in acceptors with and without Y6. The equation ${ \cal L } _ { \mathrm { D } } = \sqrt { D \tau }$ , where $\tau$ represents the exciton lifetime and $D$ is the diffusion constant, is used to calculate the diffusion length. Figure 4a,b show accelerated decay with increasing fluence density, which is attributable to exciton-exciton annihilation (EEA). Considering bimolecular recombination, the dynamic decay of the excitons can be described as[41,42]

$$
\frac { d n ( t ) } { \ d t } = \triangledown - k n \left( t \right) - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \gamma \triangledown n ^ { 2 } \left( t \right)
$$

with the solution

$$
n ( t ) = \frac { n ( 0 ) \mathrm { e } ^ { - k t } } { 1 + \frac { \gamma } { 2 k } n ( 0 ) [ 1 - \mathrm { e } ^ { - k t } ] }
$$

where $n ( t )$ is the exciton density as a function of delay time, $k = 1 / \tau$ is the monomolecular decay rate constant, and $\gamma$ is the bimolecular recombination rate constant arising from the EEA. The fluence-dependent TAS data (solid lines) were fitted using Equation 2, and the corresponding parameters are summarized in Table S7 (Supporting Information). The calculated $L _ { \mathrm { D } }$ of PY-DT $\left( 3 4 . 2 4 ~ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ was smaller than that of PY-DT with Y6 $( 3 8 . 2 4 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ , matching our results for the exciton lifetime. A long exciton diffusion length reduces non-geminate recombination, improving the movement of excitons to the D-A interface. Therefore, a large number of excitons at the interface are separated into free charges, which manifest macroscopically as a sig. nificant improvement in $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of devices with Y6. The photocurrent density $( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } )$ versus effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ characteristics were measured to quantify exciton dissociation (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The exciton dissociation possibility $( P ( E , T ) )$ is determined by $J _ { \mathrm { { p h } } } / J _ { \mathrm { { s a t } } } , ^ { [ 4 3 ] }$ where $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ is the saturated photocurrent density. As expected, $P ( E , T )$ increased from $9 6 . 8 \%$ to $9 7 . 6 \%$ when Y6 was added to PM6:PY-DT. Previous reports have shown that the binding energy $( E _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ of a material is another parameter to determine exciton dissociation. The singlet excitons at the interface of the blend must overcome the strong Coulomb attraction known as $E _ { \mathrm { b } }$ under the driving force to dissociate into free-charge carriers. $E _ { \mathrm { b } }$ can be experimentally determined by the equation $E _ { \mathrm { b } } ~ = ~ E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { t } { } ~ - ~ E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { 0 } { } ^ { [ 4 4 ] }$ where $E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { t }$ is the fundamental gap between the ionization potential (IP) and electron affinity (EA) and $E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { 0 }$ is the optical gap obtained from the absorption band edge. Here, cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were used to calculate the fundamental gap of PY-DT and mixed $\mathrm { P Y - D T + Y } 6$ materials, as shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information). The calculated $E _ { b }$ values of PY-DT and $\mathrm { P Y - D T + Y } 6$ are 0.45 and $0 . 3 6 \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively (Table S8, Supporting Information), suggesting that the well-mixed phase between PY-DT and Y6 can reduce the exciton binding energies. Therefore, a small driving force is required to separate the excitons into delocalized charges in the blend with Y6, leading to a high charge-dissociation rate.

Furthermore, light-dependent measurements were performed to investigate the benefits of Y6 additives. Figure 4c,d show the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ vs light intensity $( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ and $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ vs $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ curves, respectively. From Figure $_ { 4 \mathrm { c } }$ , a relation of $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } \propto ( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } ) ^ { \circ }$ $( \alpha$ is the exponential factor) is used to show the charge recombination behavior of the devices. The $\alpha$ value of the all-PSC with Y6 (0.996) was higher than that without Y6 (0.985), indicating suppressed bimolecular recombination. In the case of the dependence of $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ on $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ the slope of the linear fitting was closer to $k T / q$ $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the temperature in Kelvin, and $q$ is the elementary charge), suggesting negligible trap-assisted recombination. The slope of the all-PSC decreased from 1.27 to 1.09 by incorporating Y6, indicating that Y6 can reduce blend trap densities. To carefully investigate the density of states (DoS) of the trap distribution, we carried out frequency-dependent capacitance spectra measurements in a dark environment. The DoS data of different devices were determined using Mott-Schottky analysis, as shown in Figure 5a, and can be described by the following Gaussian shape:[45,46]

$$
N _ { \mathrm { t } } \left( E \right) = \frac { N _ { \mathrm { t } } } { \sqrt { 2 \pi } \sigma } \ : \exp \left[ - \frac { \left( E _ { \mathrm { t } } - E \right) ^ { 2 } } { 2 \sigma ^ { 2 } } \right]
$$

where $N _ { \mathrm { t } }$ is the total density, $E _ { \mathrm { t } }$ is the energy center of the DoS, and $\sigma$ is the disorder parameter. The detailed fitting parameters are summarized in Table S9 (Supporting Information). The device with Y6 has low trap DoS and disorder values of $2 . 2 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 3 } \ e V ^ { - 1 } }$ and $1 6 . 2 \ \mathrm { m e V } ,$ respectively, suggesting that the well-mixed phase efficiently suppresses trap densities and improves the molecular energy distribution order. Capacitance spectra were also used to determine the relative dielectric constants $( \epsilon _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ , an important factor associated with the intrinsic molecular polarizability and dipole moment, using the equation $\varepsilon _ { r } = C d / \varepsilon _ { 0 } A , ^ { [ 4 7 ] }$ where $C$ is the device capacitance measured at a frequency of $1 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { H z }$ $d$ is the active layer film thickness, $\epsilon _ { 0 }$ is the permittivity of vacuum, and $A$ is the device area. As shown in Figure S14 (Supporting Information), the $\epsilon _ { r }$ values for PM6:PYDT devices with and without Y6 are 2.98 and 3.10, respectively, which is the evidence that the new well-mixed phase has improved molecular polarizability and charge delocalization. The binding energy $( E _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ is also related to $\epsilon _ { \mathrm { r } }$ via $E _ { \mathrm { b } } ~ \approx ~ e ^ { 2 } / 4 \pi \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { 0 } } \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { r } } \mathrm { ; } ^ { [ 4 4 ] }$ high $\epsilon _ { \mathrm { r } }$ values represent a small $E _ { \mathrm { b } }$ , which is consistent with our calculated $E _ { \mathrm { b } }$ values.

To evaluate the photostability of all-PSC devices with and without Y6, we measured the performance of the encapsulated devices over time in an ambient environment at MPP tracking conditions under continuous one-sun illumination. All-PSCs with Y6 had better long-term photostability than those without Y6 (Figure 4e). By incorporating Y6 into the blend, the $T _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime (time required to reach $80 \%$ of the initial efficiency) of the allPSCs increased from 250 to $1 1 8 0 \mathrm { h }$ . Furthermore, we obtained $T _ { 7 0 }$ lifetime of $9 1 8 5 \ \mathrm { h }$ for the Y6-assisted devices by extrapolating the measured photostability data (Figure S15, Supporting Information). To investigate the effect of Y6 on the stability of the devices, we compared their absorption spectra before and after illumination (Figure S16, Supporting Information). The PM6:PYDT blend film without Y6 showed significantly reduced absorption intensity across the entire absorption spectrum after illumination, whereas the Y6-assisted blend showed only a small variation. This reduction in the absorption intensity may be caused by the degradation of the molecules in the blend. AFM and GIWAXS measurements were used to determine the morphological evolution of the aged blend films. The film surface of PM6:PY-DT became rough after illumination, with an increase in RMS from 1.19 to $1 . 2 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S17, Supporting Information). In contrast, the roughness of the film with Y6 decreased from 1.29 to $1 . 2 4 \mathrm { n m }$ , suggesting a slightly decreased phase separation. To better understand the morphological changes, we compared the fibril widths (full-width half-maximum (FWHM) of the peaks in the line profiles of the AFM phase images) of fresh and aged blend films (Figures S18 and S19, Supporting Information). The calculated average values of fibril width decreased with aging, from 15.45 to $8 . 9 4 \mathrm { n m }$ in PM6:PY-DT and from 18.70 to $1 5 . 6 6 \mathrm { n m }$ in Y6-assisted PM6:PY-DT, indicating a slight deterioration of the morphology in the Y6-assisted film and the charge-transfer behavior is still splendid. In terms of the evolution of molecular packing and ordering, the PM6:PY-DT film showed a weak crystallization and $\pi - \pi$ stacking after aging (Figure S20, Supporting Information), with lower CCL values $( 1 9 . 3 6 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ to $1 8 . 0 7 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } \}$ and increased $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance ( $d$ spacing) $( 3 . 8 3 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ to $3 . 8 5 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } )$ in the OOP direction (Table S10, Supporting Information). The blend film with Y6 exhibited nearly unchanged $d$ spacing and CCL, indicating a stable microstructure and molecular ordering. These results confirm that the well-mixed phase formed between PYDT and Y6 maintained its molecular structure under illumination. The changes in morphology after aging affect the physical characteristics and performances of OsC devices. In this case, the relative dielectric constant and DoS of the trap distribution of different devices before and after aging were compared to evaluate the effects of long-time illumination on device physics (Figure S14, Supporting Information and Figure 5a). The degradation of the blend film led to a low dipole moment, resulting in a reduction in the relative dielectric constant. However, the difference of $\epsilon _ { r }$ between fresh and aged Y6-assisted devices is smaller in comparison with the devices without Y6. The corresponding $\epsilon _ { r }$ values of the aged devices with and without Y6 are 2.97 and 2.66, respectively. Furthermore, the trap density of the PM6:PY-DT device increased from $4 . 5 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 }$ to $3 . 6 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 7 } \mathrm { c m ^ { - 3 } } \mathrm { e V ^ { - 1 } }$ after illumination. Similarly, the trap density of the Y6-assisted device slightly increased from $2 . 2 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 }$ to $4 . 4 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 3 } \ e V ^ { - 1 } }$ . These results suggest that the well-mixed phase in the blend containing Y6 is favorable for holding the intrinsic properties of devices under prolonged illumination.

The charge-transport properties of ten devices were also characterized using space-charge-limited current (SCLC) methods (Figure S21, Supporting Information). The corresponding statistical data are shown in Figure 5b. The average electron mobility $( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } )$ and hole mobility $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } )$ values of devices with Y6 $( 5 . 4 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $4 . 6 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively) were both higher than those of devices without Y6 $( 5 . 2 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $3 . 7 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathbf { S } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively). Notably, the $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ ratio declined from 1.41 to 1.16 for the all-PsCs after adding Y6. It is believed the balanced $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ value is responsible for achieving a high FF in OSCs, so that the improved FF value in Y6-assisted devices can be understood. The statistical values of $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ for the aged devices are shown in Figure 5c. As expected, the charge mobility decreased with aging. However, the introduction of Y6 has the ability to suppress the decrement rate of charge mobility. Further details are presented in Table S11 (Supporting Information). As we all know, thermal stability is also a vital property of all-PSCs. Therefore, we measured the thermal stability of devices with and without Y6. As shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), the Y6-assisted device exhibited more stable performance under continuous heating than the device without Y6, which is consistent with our photostability results. From the discussion, the improvements in efficiency and stability are attributed to the formation of a new well-mixed phase between the PY-DT acceptor and Y6 additive, according to the schemes shown in Figure 5d. The introduction of the Y6 additive induced PM6 aggregation, and PYDT combined with Y6 to form a new well-mixed structure owing to its good miscibility. The blend film exhibited more ordered molecular packing and increased phase separation, resulting in improved charge mobility and suppressed charge recombination. Moreover, the well-mixed phase "locked" the morphology of the active layer, improving its stability under continuous illumination.

# 3. Conclusion

We have introduced Y6, as a solid additive, into a PM6:PY-DT blend. The similar surface tension between PY-DT and Y6, as well as the single melting peak in $\mathrm { P Y - D T + Y } 6$ , suggest the formation of a new well-mixed phase between PY-DT and Y6. Benefiting from the well-mixed phase, the blend film exhibited suitable phase separation, strong $\pi - \pi$ stacking, and high molecular ordering. This promoted exciton separation and charge transport, as evidenced by the suppressed trap densities, improved exciton diffusion length, and reduced charge recombination. The all-PSCs with Y6 exhibited improved $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF values, achieving a high PCE of $1 8 . 0 2 \%$ The long-term stability of the Y6-assisted device is also noteworthy. The Y6-assisted all-polymer blend was not sensitive to light illumination, preventing its degradation. As a result, these devices exhibited a $T _ { 8 0 }$ lifetime of $1 1 8 0 \mathrm { h }$ (a $T _ { 7 0 }$ lifetime of $9 1 8 5 \mathrm { h }$ ) at MPP conditions under continuous light illumination, greatly improving the properties of organic photovoltaics for future commercialization.
