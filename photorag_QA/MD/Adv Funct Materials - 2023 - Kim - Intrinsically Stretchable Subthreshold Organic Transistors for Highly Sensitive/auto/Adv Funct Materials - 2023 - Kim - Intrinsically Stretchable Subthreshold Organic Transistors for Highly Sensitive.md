# Intrinsically Stretchable Subthreshold Organic Transistors for Highly Sensitive Low-Power Skin-Like Active-Matrix Temperature Sensors

Jun Su Kim, Min Woo Jeong, Tae Uk Nam, Ngoc Thanh Phuong Vo, Kyu Ho Jung, Tae II Lee,\* and Jin Young Oh\*

Stretchable wearable sensors ultimately require low power consumption and high response to physiological signals with skin conformability. However, power-response tradeoff and strain-dependent sensing instability remain key challenges for electronic skin (e-skin) sensors. Herein,an intrinsically stretchable organic subthreshold transistor operating at low voltage $( - 1 \mathsf { V } )$ is presented, leading to ultralow power consumption $( < 1 \mathsf { n } \mathsf { w } )$ for highly sensitive skin-like temperature sensory devices. The highly temperature-dependent hopping transport of the fully stretchable subthreshold transistor exhibits high-temperature sensitivity $( 9 . 4 \% ^ { \circ } { \mathsf C } ^ { - 1 } )$ and excellent sensing stability up to $100 \%$ strain. A skin-like subthreshold organic transistor active-matrix array is successfully fabricated that sensed the surface temperature distribution under a 3D deformation.

# 1. Introduction

Many diseases are identified by changes in body temperature. Monitoring body temperature daily is an easy and useful way to obtain information about current health conditions and could provide an early warning for infectious diseases. Recently, on-site examination of body temperature has been widely used as an essential prerequisite to prevent the spread of infectious diseases such as influenza and COvID-19. Keeping pace with the rapidly growing importance of health monitoring and epidemic control, the demand for rapid and accurate $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ monitoring of individual body temperature is growing exponentially.[1,2] To meet this need, skin-like temperature sensors directly integrated or attached to human skin have been considered because of their mechanical compliance and imperceptibility to strains from daily body movements.[3,4]

Based on the inherent mechanical softness and high temperature-dependency in hopping transport of organic semiconductors, organic thin-film field-effect transistors (OTFTs) are a promising candidate for skin-like temperature sensors, with easier processability for active-matrix arrays and lower cross-sensitivity compared to conventional two-terminal temperature sensors such as thermistor and capacitor. Recently, the rapid development of intrinsically stretchable organic semiconductor materials, especially organic polymers, has encouraged the potential of OTFTs as skin-like sensors.[5-8]

However, conventional OTFTs must overcome their low-temperature sensitivity and electrical instability under strain to be adopted as skin-like temperature sensors.9-11] In addition, their high operating voltage should be lowered to reduce power consumption, given the limited capacity of rechargeable batteries. Although many efforts have been made to meet these requirements, OTFT technology still needs further development.[12-24]

The subthreshold regime with the highest transconductance efficiency $( \mathrm { g } _ { \mathrm { m } } / I _ { \mathrm { D } } )$ at near zero voltage has been suggested for high gain and low power consumption in field-effect transistor-based sensor applications.[25-271 However, the subthreshold instability of OTFTs, due to the strain- and thermal-effects on the charge trap density, remains a long-standing challenge for highly sensitive and low-power stretchable sensors.[23,28,29]

In this study, we introduce an intrinsically stretchable subthreshold OTFT that operates stably for a highly sensitive and low-power skin-like active-matrix temperature sensor array. Our OTFT showed a steep subthreshold slope at low voltage, which enabled high transconductance efficiency, leading to high-temperature sensitivity $( 9 . 4 \% ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ^ { - 1 } )$ and ultralow power consumption $[ < 1 \mathrm { n W } _ { : }$ maximum $6 7 0 \mathrm { p W }$ without any operating instability up to $100 \%$ strain, sufficient to meet the requirements for bio-integrated devices. Moreover, our skin-like sensor exhibited a fast response time (0.3 s) with a low threshold voltage (Vth) deviation $( \pm 0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ and a high sensing resolution $( 0 . 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , outperforming stretchable temperature sensors previously reported. Finally, we integrated subthreshold OTFTs into an active-matrix array for 2D temperature mapping under arbitrary deformation.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Figure 1a shows a schematic diagram of an intrinsically stretchable subthreshold OTFT based on nanoconfined [poly-[2,5] bis(2-octyldodecyl)-3,6-di(thiophen-2-yl) pyrrolo [3,4-c]pyrrole1,4(2H,5H)-dionel-alt-thieno [3,2-b]thiophene](DPPT-TT) nanofibers in styrene-ethylene-butylene-styrene (SEBs) elastomer. The DPPT-TT:SEBS blend film with the nanoconfinement effect showed the highest field-effect mobility among the intrinsically stretchable semiconducting polymer films previously reported, being highly stretchable up to $100 \%$ strain and reliable over multiple stretching cycles without degrading electrical performance.30,31] The morphology of nanoconfined DPPT-TT networks in a SEBS matrix is shown in the atomic

force microscopy (AFM) height and phase images (Figure 1b;   
Figure S1, Supporting Information).

A steeper subthreshold slope in the transistor transfer curves resulted in higher transconductance efficiency at a low drain voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { D } } )$ , leading to a high response to a thermal stimulus with low power consumption (Figure 1c). To evaluate the drain current $( I _ { \mathrm { D } } )$ dependency on temperature, the OTFTs were fabricated on the SEBS dielectric-coated ITO-glass substrate for better interface compatibility between semiconductor and dielectric. The homojunction between the semiconductor and the dielectric films could efficiently minimize the interface charge trap and thermal expansion coefficient mismatch for better interface compatibility than heterojunction.[32] As a gate dielectric, the insensitive capacitance value of the SEBS dielectric layer was verified at near body temperature $( 0 - 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ (Figure S2, Supporting Information). After the semiconductor transfer printing on the dielectric, the source and drain $\mathrm { A g }$ electrodes were defined using thermal evaporation.[33] Figure 1d,e shows the OTFTs transfer and output curves, as a function of $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ without a dual slope (or kink effect) related to charge traps and as a function of the gate voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { G } } )$ effect, respectively. The transfer curves showed an identical subthreshold slope (SS) with a negligible Vth shift (Vth) between 0 and $- 2 ~ V _ { \mathrm { G } }$ regardless of $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ , indicating the trap-free hole transport in the subthreshold regime.[34] The charge trap density $( N _ { \mathrm { t } } , 1 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 1 } ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 2 } ~ e V } )$ , calculated as a function of SS $( 0 . 8 \mathrm { V } \mathrm { d e c ^ { - 1 } } )$ , remained almost unchanged between $- 3 0$ and $^ { - 1 }$ $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ (Figure 1f).

Figure $1 \mathrm { g }$ shows the changes in subthreshold current at $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ $V _ { \mathrm { G } } = - 1 \mathrm { V } ,$ and Vth according to temperature modulation from 0 to $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (transfer and output curves are shown in Figures S3 and S4, Supporting Information) and their average reliability indicating ideality (1: ideal, 0: non-ideal) was 0.99.[35] The current exponentially increased from 29 to $3 1 7 \mathrm { p A }$ , whereas Vth remained at -- $3 . 3 \pm 0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ indicating that the subthreshold current resulted from trap-less hopping transport, which required a higher thermal activation energy than that for the carrier transport in the saturation regime (Figure S5, Supporting Information).

The main reason we applied the transistor to a temperature sensor in this study was to enable low-power and highly sensitive temperature sensing through its stable operation in the subthreshold regime. As shown in Figure 1c, the subthreshold regime allows the device to operate at near-zero gate voltage compared to the saturation region, resulting in lower power consumption. Moreover, in the subthreshold regime with low carrier concentration, charge transport primarily occurs through diffusion, making it extremely sensitive to changes in temperature, where charge transport is dominated by drift due to high carrier concentrations.[25,26]

However, in the subthreshold regime, where charge transport occurs primarily through diffusion at low carrier concentrations, interface, and bulk charge traps significantly influence the $I { - } V$ characteristics of the transistor. The interface charge traps induce the temperature dependence of Vth of OTFT, making it challenging to reliably detect temperature variations at low gate voltages. On the other hand, bulk traps such as semiconductor defects make the SS value significantly increase, leading to a decrease in the response of drain current to temperature. The $I _ { \mathrm { D } }$ dependency and electrical stability in the subthreshold regime of different semiconductor and dielectric systems were compared by testing OTFTs with neat DPPT-TT/SEBS and DPPT-TT:SEBS/ $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ (semiconductor/dielectric), considering the nanoconfinement effect related to: i) bulk and ii) interface charge traps of the semiconductor and semiconductor-dielectric interface, respectively. In the subthreshold regime, the OTFT with neat DPPT-TT film on the SEBS dielectric layer showed unstable SS and large Vth according to $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ and temperature, respectively, even though the device had a current dependency on temperature (Figure S6, Supporting Information).[36-38]

Moreover, the neat DPPT-TT film, which is not stretchable but flexible, showed strain-dependent electrical degradation and temperature-dependent electrical instability (Figure S7, Supporting Information).[39,40] These results indicate that the semicrystalline, neat DPPT-TT thin film has numerous grain boundaries that provide hole-trapping sites, inducing unstable transport behavior in the subthreshold regime for the external electric field and temperature. In addition, this thin film is easily cracked and disconnected by external stretching, resulting in electrical deterioration. In the case of DPPT-TT:SEBS/ $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ , the device had a large Vth shift in the transfer curves as a function of $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ , associated with the interface charge trap of the oxide surface hydroxyl group, leading to an inconsistent current dependency on the temperature in the subthreshold regime (Figure S8, Supporting Information).[41,42]

Based on the above experiments, we found that the nanofiberbased semiconducting film is more sensitive to temperature changes in the subthreshold region than the neat polymer semiconductor film and conventional oxide dielectric systems, which cause bulk and interface change traps, respectively. The intrinsically stretchable semiconductor film has a mainly intramolecular charge transport network, which is less sensitive to bulk traps than the intermolecular charge transport mode. In addition, the homojunction interface at the semiconductor and dielectric interfaces efficiently reduces the interface charge trap compared with the heterojunction semiconductor-dielectric interfaces.

Normalized subthreshold currents (NS-currents, $I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ at $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ $V _ { \mathrm { G } } = - 1 \mathrm { V } )$ were plotted as a function of $V _ { \mathrm { G } }$ (Figure 1h) to determine the subthreshold current response to temperature. Interestingly, the response increased up to $9 . 4 \% ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is the highest temperature sensitivity among the polymer semiconductors previously reported (Table S1, Supporting Information). Moreover, the response in the subthreshold regime is 7.5 times higher than that in the saturation regime $( 1 . 2 \% ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ^ { - 1 }$ , Figures S5 and S9, Supporting Information), reflecting higher activation energy for carrier diffusion in the subthreshold regime compared to that for carrier drift in the saturation regime, which results in a strong temperature dependency, as shown in Figure 1i. Measurements of the changes in NS-current and Vth over time at a temperature were performed to confirm the possibility of using NS-current as an indicator for continuous temperature sensing; these measurements were stable for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ (Figure 1j; Figure S10, Supporting Information). In addition, the typical temperature-sensing resolution of our OTFTs was estimated to be $0 . 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , as shown in Figure 1k. The contact resistance of the OTFT was stable without significant change in cooling and heating (Figure S11, Supporting Information). These results imply that subthreshold OTFTs have reliable charge injection and transport over the temperature retention time without facing electrical instabilities related to charge traps. Furthermore, unchanged OM and AFM images and UV-vis absorption spectra, collected during heating up to $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ confirm the reliable thermal stability of our subthreshold OTFTs (Figures S12-S14, Supporting Information). In terms of transport mode, OTFTs showed a 2D charge carrier transport, which can reduce the length of the current path for more rapid charge transport compared to 3D transport (Figure S15, Supporting Information).[43,44]

To investigate the combined effects of strain and temperature on the electrical property of DPPT-TT:SEBS blend film, the OTFTs were fabricated with films that were first stretched and then transferred on rigid substrates to exclude the influences of other components on the strain (Figure S16, Supporting Information). Figure 2a,b shows the NS-current changes in the temperature range from 0 to $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for each strain direction (parallel and perpendicular to the channel length). The initial NS-current value and temperature response remained stable with a small Vth $( < 1 \mathrm { \ V } )$ up to $100 \%$ strain in both directions (Figure 2c,d; Figures S17 and S18, Supporting Information).

These results indicate that nanoconfined DPPT-TT nanofibers preserved their interconnected network in the elastomer matrix even at $100 \%$ strain, which enabled efficient hopping transport with minimal interface trapping and accumulated charge scattering generated at the interface between semiconductor and dielectric by the strain.[16,45] The Vth coupled with interface trap and charge scattering was $2 . 5 \pm 0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { V }$ up to $100 \%$ strain throughout the temperature range $( 0 - 5 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , a value much smaller than that of previously reported works.116,2,26,28,30] Considering these results, the semiconducting film reliability at different temperatures was further tested by repeating cooling and heating cycles. Figure 2e,f presents the NS-current changes during multiple cooling $( 2 5 { - } 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ and heating $( 2 5 - 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ cycles on various strains. All transfer curves for the reliability test are shown in Figures S19 and s20 (Supporting Information). The semiconducting film exhibited identical temperature dependency of the $I _ { \mathrm { D } }$ changes regardless of strain and stretching directions during multiple cooling and heating cycles. These results clearly indicate that the NS-current has a highly reliable temperature response without strain effect.

Using the temperature-responsive blend film, we fabricated a fully stretchable subthreshold OTFT in a $5 \times 5$ active-matrix array as a skin-like temperature sensor module. Figure 3a shows the schematic illustration of the entirely stretchable sensor module, where the active-matrix transistor array was fabricated on a SEBS substrate using a transfer printing method with thermally evaporated Ag metallization for stretchable source, drain, and gate electrodes. An optical microscope (OM) image of the sensor module is shown in Figure 3b. The skin-like sensor module was thin and soft enough to fit smoothly on the inside of the left wrist (Figure 3c). All transistors arrayed in the module successfully worked, with minimal variations in field-effect mobility and their Vth, as shown in Figure 3d,e, respectively. All devices exhibited typical transfer curves with a reliability factor (r) of 0.99, representing a degree of overestimation or miscalculation of the organic transistor field-effect mobility (Figure 3f; Figure S21, Supporting Information). Typical output curves (Figure 3g) showed the Ohmic contact of the Ag metallization. The average NS-current of OTFTs in the active-matrix array was exponentially increased by temperature modulation from 0 to $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with different amplitudes and directions of applied strain (Figure 3h,i; Figure S22, Supporting Information). The fully stretchable subthreshold OTFT showed no degradation in subthreshold current compared with the single subthreshold OTFT on a rigid substrate, indicating that strain-induced changes in channel geometry and dielectric thickness rarely modulated hole transport in the subthreshold regime of our OTFT. The current temperature responses are almost identical to those of the single organic transistor fabricated on a rigid substrate, indicating that the subthreshold current of the active-matrix transistor array on strain was not affected by strain-induced changes in channel geometry and dielectric thickness. According to our research, this is the first demonstration of a stretchable, strain-effect-free subthreshold OTFT active-matrix array with high-temperature gain.

Based on the results, practical demonstrations were performed to study the feasibility of our skin-like temperature sensor module. Figure 4a shows a subthreshold current-time response curve in the subthreshold regime ( $V _ { \mathrm { { D } } } , \mathrm { { } } V _ { \mathrm { { G } } } = - 1 \mathrm { { V } } )$ of a sensor module pixel transistor following successive temperature changes. The current exponentially increased and decreased with respect to temperature during heating and cooling between O and $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ The sensor sensitivity was higher than $9 \% ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ^ { - 1 }$ , the highest value among previous transistor-based stretchable temperature sensors (Figure 4b). Power consumption is an important parameter for wireless and safe operation, considering most organic transistors require high driving voltage. Operating at - 1 V $\mathrm { \Delta } V _ { \mathrm { { D } } }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { G } }$ ), our OTFT consumed pW-level power (maximum $6 7 0 \ \mathrm { p W } )$ the lowest power consumption among those previously reported (Figure 4c). As shown in Figure 4d, sensor response times to both forward and reverse temperature step changes were also examined, indicating a response time of $3 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m s } }$ , the fastest response time among the flexible and stretchable transistor-based temperature sensors previously reported. Current-time response curves of the sensor module mounted on the inner part of a human wrist with different motions are shown in Figure 4e. The sensor exhibited consistent responses despite sequential bending, compressing, and twisting. Figure 4f shows a photograph of the deformed sensor module by a metal ball indentation. Each subthreshold current of the sensor module was converted into a temperature value with an accuracy of $9 4 . 9 \%$ at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ using the empirical equation from a reference NS-current-temperature curve in Figure S23 (Supporting Information) and a surface temperature mapping was successfully performed without any strain effect under 3D deformation at room temperature (Figure S24, Supporting Information). Figure 4g,h shows NS-current mapping of the sensor module under the cold and hot metal ball indentation, respectively (Figures S25 and S26, Supporting Information). A real strain, applied by the ball indentation, was a maximum of $45 \%$ as shown in Figure 4i and Figure S27 (Supporting Information). Figure 4j,k shows the temperature distributions converted from the NS-current mappings which were identical to the result of thermographic scanning with the calibrated infrared (IR) imaging camera (Figure 4l).

# 3. Conclusion

In this study, we presented an intrinsically stretchable subthreshold organic transistor for skin-like temperature sensors. Temperature-dependency of the hopping transport in the subthreshold transistor exhibited high-temperature sensitivity (over $9 \% \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ^ { - 1 }$ ) with the highest transconductance efficiency at a low operating voltage $( - 1 \mathsf { V } )$ , which led to ultralow power consumption $( < 1 \ \mathrm { n W } )$ . Moreover, the temperature-sensing performance of fully stretchable subthreshold transistors maintained high stability and resolution up to $100 \%$ strain, which enables this approach to solve the long-standing challenges related to sensitivity, power consumption, and strain-effect of wearable temperature sensors. Lastly, concerning subthreshold transistors, we demonstrated that the skin-like temperature sensor activematrix array could map the temperature distribution on dynamic surfaces.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: Poly[2,5-(2-octyldodecyl)-3,6-diketopyrrolopyrrole-alt-5,5- (2,5-di(thienyl)thieno[3,2b]thio phene)] (DPPT-TT) was purchased from Derthon. The molecular weight and polydispersity index (PDI) of the polymer were $\mathsf { M n } > 3 3 \mathsf { k }$ and ${ \mathsf { P } } { \mathsf { D } } { \mathsf { I } } = 3 . 0$ . Styrene-ethylene-butylene-styrene (SEBS) elastomers were provided from AsahiKASEI. H1062 ( $18 \%$ styrene content) and H1052, $2 0 \%$ styrene content) were used as blending components of the semiconducting layer, elastic substrate, and dielectric layer, respectively. All materials were used as purchased.

Device Fabrication-Rigid Substrate Based OFETs: To prepare the stretchable semiconducting film, DPPT-TT, and ${ \mathsf { S E B S } } \ H \mathsf { 7 0 6 2 }$ were dissolved in anhydrous chlorobenzene in 2:8 weight ratio to a concentration of $0 . 7 ~ \mathrm { w t . } \%$ . The solution was dissolved at $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ for $3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ . For the fabrication of semiconducting film, these solutions with filtration using $0 . 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ PTFE-D filter were spin-coated on octadecyltrimethoxysilane (OTS) treated Si wafer with $3 0 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ of ${ \mathsf { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ at 1000 rpm for 1 min, then annealed at $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ for $\rceil \mathsf { h }$ under $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ atmosphere glovebox $( \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } < 0 . 0 1$ ppm, $0 _ { 2 } < 0 . 0 1 ~ \mathsf { p p m } )$ . The dielectric layer was prepared by spin-coating SEBS H1052 solution $( 6 0 \mathrm { \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ in toluene) onto ITO (gate electrode, Initial resistance ${ \approx } 2 0 ~ \Omega$ glass substrate. The semiconducting films were then transferred using PDMS stamp from the OTS-treated Si water onto the SEBs/ITO glass substrate. Finally, the silver source and drain electrode with different metals were evaporated using a thermal evaporator (W: $1 0 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ L: $1 5 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$

Device Fabrication-Stretchable Temperature Sensing Active-Matrix OTFTs Array:: A stretchable substrate for a wearable temperature sensing active-matrix transistor array was prepared by casting $1 0 \ m L$ of SEBS H1062 Solution $1 0 0 \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 }$ in toluene) onto a slide glass. The thickness of the prepared elastic substrate was ${ \approx } 0 . 0 5 ~ \mathsf { m m }$ Then stretchable Ag $( 5 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ gate electrode was prepared on the SEBS substrate by thermal evaporating under a high vacuum (below $5 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ torr) using a patterning mask. The spin-coated stretchable dielectric layer (SEBS H1052 in toluene) and temperature-sensing semiconductor layer were sequentially transfer-printed from the OTs-Si wafer to a stretchable substrate with an $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ gate prepared. Then stretchable $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ $( 5 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ source and drain electrodes were fabricated by thermal evaporation using a patterning mask on a stretchable semiconductor layer.

Characterization-Temperature Dependent Electrical Characterization of OFET Devices:  The electrical characterization of all devices was performed using a Keithely 2400A Source meter with a customized LabVIEW (National Instruments) program. For a change of operating temperature, a seebeck effect heating probe station was connected with a Keithely 2400A Source meter. In particular, all electrical measurements were carried out under ambient conditions and had a stabilization time of 1 min for temperature saturation of the heating probe strain.

Characterization-Film Characterization:AFM images were acquired using MULTIMOE-8J. All UV/Visible/NIR absorption spectra were acquired using JASCO V-770. For UV-vis spectroscopic measurement, samples were prepared by spin coating on an OTS wafer, annealing, and then transferred onto cleaned glass through PDMS Stamp. To probe the effect of temperature on the formed aggregates, one sample was exposed to an environment of 0, 50, and $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ for 30 min, respectively, and then slowly returned to room temperature before each measurement.

Characterization-Real-Time Temperature Sensing of Temperature Sensing Array: The real-time electrical measurement of the temperature sensing array was performed using a Keithely 2400A. For measurement under deformation according to human motion on skin, an array in which source, drain, and gate electrodes were connected with a probe and gold wire, respectively, was attached to a human wrist. For 3D mapping of the $I _ { \mathsf { D } }$ to different temperature changed under stretched conditions, the array was cooled and heated by ice, and a vial containing how water with pressure after each edge of the array was fixed.
