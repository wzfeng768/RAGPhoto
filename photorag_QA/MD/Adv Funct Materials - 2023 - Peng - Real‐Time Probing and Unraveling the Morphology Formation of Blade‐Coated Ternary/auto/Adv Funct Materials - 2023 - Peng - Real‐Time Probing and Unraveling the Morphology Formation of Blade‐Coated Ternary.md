# Real-Time Probing and Unraveling the Morphology Formation of Blade-Coated Ternary Nonfullerene Organic Photovoltaics with In Situ X-Ray Scattering

Zhongxiang Peng, Yaowen Zhang, Xiaokang Sun, Wenchao Zhao, Fenggang Bian, Yanhou Geng, Long Ye,\* and Chunming Yang\*

Characterizing the bulk heterojunction (BHJ) morphology of the active layer is essential for optimizing blade-coated organic solar cells (OsCs). Here, the morphology evolution of a highly efficient ternary polymer:nonfullerene blend PM6:N3:N2200 under different blade coating conditions is probed in real-time by in situ synchrotron X-ray scattering and in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy. Besides, the morphology of blade-coated blend films at different conditions is detailed by ex situ X-ray scattering and microscopic imaging. The ternary blend flm exhibited optimized morphology, such as superior molecular stacking structure and appropriate phase separation structure, and boosted photovoltaic performance of the binary blend, as adding a second polymer component to the host polymer:nonfullerene system can balance nucleation and crystallization of polymers and small molecules, facilitating molecular rearrangement to perfect crystallization. Both binary and ternary blends obtained optimized morphology and photovoltaic properties at medium coating speed, mainly attributed to the movement of the polymer and small molecules at the long crystallization and aggregation stage. These findings help understand morphology formation under film drying and provide guidance for optimizing the morphology in blade-coated OSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Thanks to the unique advantages of lightweight, low-cost solution processing, sustainable printability, and prospective flexibility, organic solar cells (OSCs) are broadly considered potential renewable and green energy sources.[1-10] The top power conversion efficiency (PCE) of OSCs has surpassed $1 9 \%$ owing to the development of non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs).[1115] However, these achievements are all realized in spin-coated devices, which are hard to scale up to large-area solar panels. By comparison, blade coating is a more desirable printing method that can realize large-area preparation of OSCs, but the PCE of blade-coated devices often lags behind that of spin-coated devices due to the lack of insight into the morphological characteristics of blade-coated films.6-21] The morphology evolution kinetics of active layers prepared by bladecoating is slower than that by spin-coating, providing distinctly different morphologies in solid films.[22,23] For example, Zhao et al. reported that the solvent drying rate of blade coating is ${ \approx } 3$ times slower than that of spin coating, which promotes extra mixing of donor and acceptor.[24]

Optimizing active layer morphology, spanning from molecular stacking to nanoscale phase separation, is critical for device performance.25-30] Thus, elucidating the evolution and formation of blade-coated blend films to gain optimized morphology is fundamental to achieving high photovoltaic performance.31-33] The processing parameters (e.g., coating speed, temperature, concentration, gap) of blade coating have been used to modulate the microstructure and electrical properties of organic semiconducting small molecules and polymers, as well as the morphology and photovoltaic properties of small molecule donor:fullerene based blend films.3437] However, only a limited number of studies have been devoted to the blade-coating fabrication of top-efficiency ternary polymer:nonfullerene bulk-heterojunction blends, in particular those comprising Y-family nonfullerene small molecule acceptors.38] To our knowledge, real-time monitoring how ternary mixing affects the morphology evolution of blade-coated bulk-heterojunction films has not yet been well addressed. To this end, advanced characterization methods with high time and spatial resolutions are urgently needed.

To guide the optimization of printed OsCs and overcoming the present scaling lag of efficiency, we combined synchrotron radiation-based in situ grazing incident wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS, Figure 1a) and in situ ultraviolet-visible (UVvis) spectroscopy (Figure 1b) to probe the morphology development of a high-efficiency ternary blend PM6:N3:N2200[39,40] (as shown in Figure 1c) under varied blade coating conditions. The reference binary blend PM6:N3,[41-4] holding notable interest in the OsC community, was also studied as the control. We demonstrated that adding N2200 could balance the nucleation and crystallization of PM6 and N3, and facilitate molecular rearrangement to perfect crystallization. Thereby, the ternary blend represented optimized film morphology and better photovoltaic performance. In addition, both binary and ternary blends showed more extended molecular aggregation and crystallization at medium coating speed, resulting in optimized morphology and photovoltaic characteristics. This work is the first-ever in situ scattering study on revealing the morphology evolution and underlying microstructural origins of blade-coated high-performance organic photovoltaic blends of polymer:Y-series acceptor based ternary bulk-heterojunction blends via in-situ GIWAXS experiments.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Photovoltaic Performance

The normalized UV-vis absorption spectra of PM6, N3, and N2200 are depicted in Figure 1d. The absorption spectra of PM6 and N3 are complementary, and N2200 exhibits significant absorption peaks ${ \approx } 3 9 0 $ and $7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , which are good complements to PM6 and N3. The energy levels of materials are described in Figure S1a, Supporting Information. As the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of N2200 lies between those of PM6 and N3, charges can be efficiently transferred. Therefore, introducing N2200 in the PM6:N3 blend is expected to be advantageous to photovoltaic performance.

The bulk-heterojunction OsCs based on blade-coated PM6:N3:N2200 active layers with different coating conditions were fabricated with a conventional structure (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). According to a previous study,[39,40] the optimized composition of N2200 in the ternary blend was determined to be $1 0 \ \mathrm { w t . \% }$ of the acceptor component, and the blade-coated active layers were prepared in an ambient environment at the coating speeds of 5, 10, 20, and $4 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ , respectively. Chlorobenzene was chosen as the solvent in this work because it is a commonly used solvent with a high boiling point and slow solvent volatilization, which is conducive to the use of in situ characterization methods to reveal the morphology evolution during the blend film formation. Besides, no additives are used in this work because the introduction of solvent additives will make the morphology evolution more complex, which makes it difficult to analyze the morphology evolution of polymer and small molecules in detail.

# 2.2. Molecular Stacking and Texture

The molecular stacking and crystallization of blend films were measured by GIWAXS.45,46] GIWAXS two-dimensional (2D) patterns (Figure 2a) and line profiles (Figure 2b-c) showed that all blend films exhibited apparent $\pi - \pi$ stacking (010) scattering peaks in both out-of-plane (OOP) and in-plane (IP) directions. The (010) peaks in OOP were more pronounced than in IP, representing that the films exhibited preferential face-on orientation. The OOP (010) peak is located at $q \approx 1 . 7 6 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ which is a mixed $\pi - \pi$ peak of PM6, N3, and N2200, while the IP (010) peak is located at $q \approx 1 . 8 2 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ , close to the $\pi - \pi$ peak of N3. Besides, there were multiple sharp lamellar stacking scattering peaks in the low $q$ range, indicating a high degree of molecular stacking. To obtain detailed information on molecular stacking, we performed overlapping peak resolving of OOP (010) peaks, and the results are depicted in Table S2, Supporting Information. The $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance $[ d$ spacing) of PM6, N3, and N2200 in all blend films were almost consistent, indicating that the coating speed did not affect the $\pi { - } \pi d .$ spacing of PM6, N3, and N2200, and the introduction of the N2200 did not influence the $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance of PM6 and N3. As shown in Figure 2d-e, the coating speed and the addition of N2200 could affect the order of $\pi - \pi$ stacking. For both binary and ternary blend films, the $\pi - \pi$ stacking coherence lengths (CLs) of PM6, N3, and N2200 are maximum at the coating speed of $2 0 \ m m / \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ indicating this coating speed tends to lead to a more ordered $\pi - \pi$ stacking of the blend films. In addition, the - CLs of PM6 and N3 are larger in the ternary blends than in the binary blends, sug. gesting that the introduction of N2200 can further improve the order of molecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking, which is conducive for charge transport and photovoltaic performance.

The orientation parameters and relative degree of crystallinity $\mathrm { ( r D o C ) } ^ { [ 4 7 , 4 8 ] }$ were extracted from the pole figures and listed in Table S3, Supporting Information. Moreover, the relative number of face-on orientations of ternary blends was greater than that of binary blends. The face-on orientation of the molecules is favorable to the charge transport perpendicular to the substrate direction.[50,51]

# 2.3. Film Morphology

The surface and bulk morphologies of blend films were respectively acquired by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The AFM height and phase images (Figures 3a and s2, Supporting Information) showed that the ternary blend films exhibited apparent thick fibers and the binary blend films had fine fibers, and the surface root-mean-square roughness $( R _ { \mathrm { q } } )$ of ternary blends was higher than that of binary blends, which was attributed to the fiber structures of N2200. With the increase of the coating speed, the $R _ { \mathfrak { q } }$ of the binary blend films increased slightly, but the amplitude of variation was small, and the $R _ { \mathfrak { q } }$ of the ternary blend films changed little. Besides, the TEM images also proved that there were only fine fibers in the binary blends while incorporating N2200 into PM6:N3 could form thick fiber structures (see Figure 3b). As represented in Figure 3c-d and Table S4, Supporting Information, the characteristic size scale of binary blend films gradually decreased with increasing coating speed, while the characteristic size scales of ternary blend films first decreased and then increased. When the coating speed was $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ , the characteristic size scale $( \approx 1 1 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ of the ternary blend was smaller than that of the binary blend $( { \approx } 1 4 \mathrm { n m } )$

As a complement to the above microscopic measurements, the phase separation of blend films was further investigated by grazing incident small-angle X-ray scattering (GISAXs).[52- 54 The GISAxs 2D images and IP line profiles are depicted in Figure 4a,b. The line profiles were fitted, and the relevant parameters are described in Figure 4c,d and Table S5, Supporting Information.s5-57 With the increase in coating speed, the donor domain size of binary blend film gradually decreased, while the donor domain size of ternary blend films first decreased and then increased, reaching the minimum value at $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ For both binary and ternary blend films, the acceptor domain size decreased first and then increased with the coating speed, and the domain size was minimized at $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ In addition, the domain size of the acceptor of the ternary blends $( 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ is smaller than that of the binary blends $\left( 3 2 \ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ , representing that the introduction of N2200 can minish the acceptor domain. Appropriate domain size is propitious to exciton diffusion and dissociation.[58,59] The fractal dimension of ternary blend films is smaller than that of binary blend films, demonstrating that the loose aggregates of N2200 can extend over larger areas, which may promote exciton dissociation and charge transport.

# 2.4. Morphological Evolution Kinetics

To understand the kinetics of the film microstructure formation, the morphology was investigated in real time by in situ GIWAXS measurements.60-63] The time-dependent line profiles in OOP and IP directions and contour maps of binary and ternary blends with different coating speeds are represented in Figures S3-S5. There were isotropic scattering rings at $q \approx 1 . 3 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ in the early stages of drying, which is attributed to the scattering of solvent (chlorobenzene, classical liquid scattering). To avoid interference with solvent scattering, the IP (100) peaks of blend films with various conditions were fitted by the Gaussian function to gain the evolution of lamellar stacking $d$ spacing, peak area, and CL during film formation. As described in Figure 5a-h, there were four stages of the drying process, which are labeled with four different colors. Those stages comprise (I) dissolved state, (II) nucleation and growth, (III) solvent swollen state, and (IV) glassy state. In the first state, there were no (100) scattering peaks. In the second state, as the evaporation of the solvent and augment of solution concentration, polymers, and small molecules were reaching their solubility limit, and molecules started nucleation, as featured by the appearance of (1o0) scattering peaks. Then, as the solvent continued to evaporate from the blend films, the lamellar stacking distance $\lfloor d$ spacing) gradually decreased, the crystallinity (peak area) increased gradually and rapidly, and the CL gradually decreased. These comprehensive results indicate that aggregates were formed orderly in solution but were highly swollen by solvent. As the solvent evaporated, the solvent was expelled, causing more tightly stacking, decreased $d$ spacing, and decreased effective size of the aggregates. In the third state, most solvents had been evaporated, and crystallization had been completed, as the peak area remained unchanged. As the residual solvent was continually evaporated, the molecules packed more tightly, resulting in the continuous reduction of $d$ spacing. Meanwhile, the CL increased and then remained unchanged due to the movement and rearrangement (microcrystalline growth) of molecules. In the fourth state, all the solvents left blend films, and the morphology of the blend films was fixed. As featured, all morphological parameters remained unchanged. We observed that the second and third stages of the drying process for both binary and ternary blends at medium coating speed $( 2 0 \mathrm { m m } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ were relatively long, which may be beneficial to the improvement of the crystalline structure and increment of crys. tallinity. Furthermore, the ternary blend exhibited a longer CL increase process and smaller size of the initial solvent-swollen ordered aggregates than the binary blend, which may favor the increase in the order of molecular stacking and the perfection of microcrystallite.

To further understand the crystallinity and phase separation distinction caused by coating speed and introduction of N2200, in situ UV-vis absorption measurements were employed to illuminate the polymer and small molecules aggregation evolution during the flm formation.[64,65] The time evolution of UV-vis absorption spectra and the contour maps based on UV-vis absorption profiles are depicted in Figures S6 and S7, Supporting Information, respectively. The aggregation evolution of donor (PM6) and acceptor (N3) was investigated by analyzing the evolution of intensity and location of donor and acceptor absorption peaks. The intensity of the PM6 and $\mathrm { N } 3 ~ 0 – 0$ absorption peaks were normalized for convenient comparison. As demonstrated in Figure 6a-h, the film formation is formed in three stages. In the first stage, the intensity of N3 gradually decreased, the intensity of PM6 increased slowly or remained constant, and the peak locations of PM6 and N3 were almost unchanged, as an excess solution was removed, corresponding to the absorption of the solution. In the second stage, the intensity of PM6 enhanced dramatically, and the intensity of N3 declined rapidly and then enhanced dramatically. The location of absorption peaks of PM6 and N3 were observed to be redshifted rapidly, which was attributed to the aggregation behavior during the solution-to-film transformation. In the third stage, the intensities of PM6 and N3 remained constant, and the peak positions were almost unchanged, which corresponds to the post-film formation stage. The solution-to-film transformation stage of both binary and ternary blends at $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ is more extended than at other coating speeds, suggesting the sufficient time for aggregation and crystallization of polymers and small molecules. Besides, the N3 intensity of the binary and ternary blends at $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ exhibited a slow increasing process in the. initial post-film formation stage, indicating the slow aggregation behavior of N3 (continuative crystallization of small molecules). The above results may be profitable for the improvement of the microcrystalline and increment of crystallinity. This explains why both binary and ternary blend films exhibited the highest crystallinity and maximum molecular stacking order (microcrystalline size) at medium coating speed. Furthermore, for the binary blend, the start time of 0-0 peak red shift of PM6 was the same as that of N3 at low coating speed $( 5 { - } 1 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - } } ) _ { }$ indicating that the aggregation and crystallization of PM6 and N3 started at the same time. At medium/high coating speed $( 2 0 { - } 4 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } } )$ , the start time of 0-0 peak red shift of PM6 was earlier than that of N3, demonstrating that the aggregation and crystallization of PM6 were before that of N3. For the ternary blend, the start time of 0-0 peak red shift of PM6 was the same as that of N3 at all coating speeds, suggesting that PM6 and N3 aggregated and crystallized simultaneously. These results represent that the addition of N2200 could balance the crystallization of PM6 and N3, suggesting that the addition of N2200 can enhance the crystal size of PM6 and N3 and reduce the film crystallinity.

Based on the above results, the morphology evolution of polymer: nonfullerene small molecule binary and polymer: nonfullerene small molecule: polymer ternary blend films can be illustrated in Figure 7a-b, from which the influence of the third component polymer can be elucidated. In stage I, the polymers and small molecules are well dissolved in the blend solution. As the solvent evaporates, the concentration of the solute increases. Once the critical concentration of the polymer or small molecule is reached, some polymer or small molecule begins to aggregate in an orderly fashion. In stage II, the number of aggregates (crystallite) increases, and the stacking spacing of loose aggregates decreases with the removal of solvent, resulting in the decrease in the size of the aggregates. The introduction of the third component polymer could inhibit the generation of crystal nuclei, reduce the size of the ordered aggregates, and prompt the polymer and small molecule to begin crystallization simultaneously. In stage III, the polymer chains and small molecules are nearly frozen, and crystallization stop as most solvent has been evaporated. However, the polymer chains or small molecules in the residual solvent can still rearrange for perfecting crystallization, resulting in an increase in the order of aggregates. Besides, the stacking spacing of aggregates gradually decreases as the solvent continues to be evaporated. The addition of polymer facilitating molecular rearrangement helps to increase the order of molecular stacking. In stage IV, the drying process is complete, and all solvents have evaporated. As a result, ternary blend films have higher molecular packing order, more face-on orientation, lower crystallinity, and smaller phase separation sizes than binary blend films.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we have explored in detail the morphology evolution during the blade-coated film formation of high-efficiency binary and ternary polymer:small molecule blend by in situ GIWAXS measurements and in situ UV-vis absorption spectroscopy. Through probing and analyzing the morphology evolution in real-time, it was found that the solvent swollen state and solution-to-film transformation stage of the medium coating speed was more extended, which may be beneficial to improve the crystallization of the polymers and small molecules and enhance the crystallinity. Hence, both binary and ternary blend films at medium coating speeds exhibited optimized film morphological features, including more ordered $\pi - \pi$ stacking, higher crystallinity, more face-on orientation, and smaller phase separation size, together resulting in an optimal photovoltaic performance for blade-coated devices.
