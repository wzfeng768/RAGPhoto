# Direct Atomic-Scale Structure and Electric Field Imaging of Triazine-Based Crystalline Carbon Nitride

Wu Wang, Juan Cui, Zongzhao Sun, Lin Xie, Xiaoke Mu, Limin Huang, and Jiaqing He\*

Crystalline carbon nitrides (CNs) have recently attracted considerable attention owing to their superior photocatalytic activity. However, the electron-beamsensitive nature of crystalline CNs hinders atomic-resolution imaging of their Iocal structures by conventional (scanning) transmission electron microscopy ((S)TEM) techniques. Here, the atomic structure of a triazine-based crystalline CN, poly(triazine imide) (PTI) incorporated with lithium and chloride ions, is unambiguously revealed using the emerging imaging technique of differential phase contrast STEM under a low dose. The lightest-element Li/H configuration is resolved within framework cavities of PTI and significantly affects the electronic structure for photoabsorption. The atomic electric field of PTI crystal directly determined in real space provides a fundamental evidence for the chemical bonding of Li ions and adjacent atoms for the migration of photogenerated carriers. These results facilitate the comprehension on local atomic configuration and chemical bonding state of crystalline CNs and can lead to a deeper understanding of the photocatalytic mechanism.

# 1. Introduction

Crystalline carbon nitrides (CNs) prepared by the ionothermal method with the mixture of monomers and alkaline molten salts possess a superior photocatalytic activity compared to traditional, thermally synthesized CNs with amorphous and semicrystalline structure!1-4] as high crystallinity and long-periodic structure could effectively increase the charge migration rate, reduce the recombination probability of carriers, narrow the bandgap, and enhance the light-harvesting efficiency.[5-7] The intrinsic property of crystalline CNs is not only related to their polymeric network, but also determined by the coupled ions in their frameworks. It has been demonstrated that crystalline CNs composed of the heptazine unit possess a larger $\pi$ conjugated system and would be more active than crystalline CNs with the triazine unit in photocatalysis.3,8,9] The bandgap of crystalline CNs could be tuned by the stacking arrangement of conjugated layers.10] 1t was also found that potassium ions intercalated into space between conjugated layers of crystalline CNs enable to facilitate the migration of photogenerated carriers in adjacent two layers.8,11] Among crystalline CNs, poly(triazine imide) (PTI) incorporated with $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ and $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions has been considered as an ideal model for investigating the photocatalytic mechanism due to its highly crystalline, well-defined structure as well as the capability for photocatalytic overall water splitting.1,12,13] However, because of the unstable nature of PTI under the illumination of highenergy electron beam,14] its structure elucidation down to atomic scale is rarely achieved, which hinders a deep understanding on the photocatalysis with this material.

Recently, an emerging imaging technique, differential phase contrast scanning transmission electron microscopy (DPC-STEM),15-19] has demonstrated the capability with an low electron beam dose for solving atomic structures of beam-sensitive materials, such as zeolites,20] metal-organic frameworks,[21] even covalent-organic frameworks,[22] and for identifying hydrogen atom in metal hydrides.[23,24] Particularly, the contrast of integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC) STEM is approximately linear to the local projected electrostatic potential of specimen[16] and therefore roughly proportional to the atomic number Z instead of its square in the high angle annular dark field (HAADF) STEM, which drastically improves the capability in the light-element imaging. Moreover, the DPC-STEM technique can be applied to directly visualize the electric field of atoms and to elucidate local chemical bonding states within crystals.[17,25-27]

In this work, we clearly reveal the channel structure of PTI at atomic resolution by iDPC and differentiated differential phase contrast (dDPC) STEM, and also precisely identify atomic columns of superimposed C and N in triazine units with inter calated $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions. The lightest element $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } / H }$ configuration has been resolved in the framework cavities of PTI, which correlates to the electronic structure for photoabsorption. The atomic electric field distribution within PTI crystal is directly obtained from segmented-detector DPC images and indicates the coupling electric field between Li ions and triazine units to bridge adjacent $\pi$ conjugated systems for the migration of charge carriers.

# 2. Results and Discussion

PTI incorporated with lithium and chloride ions has recently received great attention owing to its photoactivity for water splitting. PTI consists of an infinite 2D network of triazine units connected via imide groups and the layers stack with an AB sequence, forming porous channels along the $c$ axis with $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions positioning centers,28,29] as shown in Figure 1a. Hydrogen of imide units and $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ions for maintaining the charge neutrality constitute a stochastic occupation of H and $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ in the framework cavities of PTI.[29] The synthesis of PTI sample followed the reported ionothermal approach[28] with dicyandiamide and a eutectic mixture of lithium chloride and potassium chloride. The as-prepared PTI has a high crystallinity (as indicated by X-ray diffraction result in Figure S1a (Supporting Information)) and is composed of hexagonal prism crystals with a size around $2 0 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , as shown in STEM and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figure 1b and Figure S1b (Supporting Information)). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) map (Figure S1d, Supporting Information) has indicated a uniform distribution of C, N, and Cl elements within PTI crystals. Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) measurement (Figure S1e, Supporting Information) further confirms the existence of Li as well as the $\mathrm { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -hybridized carbon and nitrogen atoms, which is consistent with previous reports.128,30] High-resolution ADF-STEM image (Figure 1c) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Figure S1c, Supporting Information) show a hexagonal lattice structure of PTI along the [001] zone axis. Bright spots in ADF-STEM image should correspond to intercalated $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions among the framework channels. The hexagonal prismatic PTI crystals consist of two {001} facets as basal planes and six rectangular {1o0} facets as side planes, as schematically illustrated in the inset of Figure 1b. However, the detailed structure of connected triazine units is still missing and lighter atoms (Li and H) within channels are not observed in this image.

Regarding this issue, we applied the aberration-corrected DPC-STEM imaging technique to reveal the atomic structure of PTI with its advantages of enhanced contrast of light atoms and of the low-dose imaging (the probe current of 2 pA used for acquisition). Focused electron probe is deflected by local electrostatic field (the gradient of the potential) of specimen during the propagation and the intensity redistributed probe is recorded by a segmented detector with four quadrants, as illustrated in Figure 1e. Two orthogonal components of DPC image were calculated from $\mathrm { D P C } _ { x } = \mathbf { A } - \mathbf { C }$ (Figure 1f) and $\mathsf { D P C } _ { \gamma } = \mathbf { B } - \mathbf { D }$ (Figure 1g). dDPC constructs an image that is proportional to the charge density in the sample, and the contrast of iDPC image is approximately linear to the atomic number $Z$ of material.[16] As shown in Figure 1h,i, the highly ordered atomic structure of PTI crystal with the well-defined channels along the [001] zone axis is clearly revealed by dDPC and iDPC images, where atoms exhibit a dark intensity on a bright background in the dDPC image (the positive charge marks as dark) and reverse in the iDPC image. Six triazine units are interconnected to form honeycomb-like frameworks and Cl anions occupy their centers, which is in agreement with the PTI structure model proposed in previous literatures.[28,31] By comparing the magnified ADF (Figure 1d), dDPC, and iDPC images (Figure 1j,k), we can know that the DPC-sTEM imaging technique is more suitable for elucidating detailed structures of PTI, which could also be analogous to other crystalline CNs.

Detailed structure of PTI at the atomic scale is further analyzed in Figure 2. Six columns of superimposed C and N atoms in staggeringly stacked triazine units along the $c$ axis are clearly identified by dDPC and iDPC images (Figure 2a,b).

Bridging N atoms for triazine units also show a fair contrast in these images. Additional features with lower contrast have been found between N atoms of bridged imide groups and Cl anions and refer to occupations of lighter elements (Li and H) within framework cavities. The features do not arise in all gaps between bridged N atoms and Cl anions, while there are full features for these gaps in simulated iDPC image (Figure 2c) with random occupations of $\mathrm { L i } / \mathrm { H }$ atoms in the 6c site (space group: $P 6 _ { 3 } m c )$ . The focus-thickness series iDPC simulation further indicates that the observed features of PTI agree well with its model with bright spots corresponding to atomic columns (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Intensity line profiles, as illustrated in Figure 2d, further demonstrate the construction of different atoms in PTI crystal. These experimental intensity profiles were further compared with the corresponding profiles from simulated iDPC image (Figure S3, Supporting Information), showing an approximate match. This confirms the reliability of observation on those light atoms in PTI structure. In order to verify the reliability of Li/H configuration observed in the experiment, we have performed the iDPC simulation with different contents (1:0, 0.75:0.25, 0.5:0.5, 0.25:0.75, and 0:1) of Li and H in 6c site of PTI structure. The Li and H atoms could be identified in all simulated iDPC images (Figure S4a-e, Supporting Information), along with their structures and intensity profiles between opposite bridged N atoms across the cavity of PTI (Figure S4f, Supporting Information).

The configuration of $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ and H contents within the framework cavities of PTI could modify the electronic structure and thus significantly affect its photocatalytic performance.13] However, the local architecture of Li and H atoms in PTI has not been addressed so far as both lithium and hydrogen are lightweight atoms with very low scattering probability, therefore hindering the delineation of structure-property relationships. With the merit of iDPC imaging technique for light atoms under the low dose, we unambiguously reveal the $\mathrm { L i } / \mathrm { H }$ configuration with three typical types in PTI individual crystal, as shown in Figure 2e-g. Intensity profiles (Figure 2h) along the defined circles (marked in Figure 2e-g) across gaps between bridged N atoms and Cl anions indicate that positions with higher intensity and lower intensity correspond to atomic columns mostly occupied by lithium and hydrogen, respectively. The relative intensity of lithium and hydrogen is further confirmed in iDPC-STEM simulation images (Figure S5a-c, Supporting Information) with these three configurations, in which the narrower peaks in the intensity profile (Figure S5d, Supporting Information) result from the inconsideration of actual thermal diffuse scattering, quantum nuclear effect, and the sample drift during acquisition. The apparent intensity discrepancy of lithium and hydrogen atomic columns in both experimental and simulated images verifies the observation on them in cavities of PTI crystal. The 6c sites are occupied by Li and H atoms with four, five, and six positions in observed three configurations, in which the H atoms were bound to the bridging nitrogen atoms while the Li ions induced the deprotonation of bridged imide groups.

Moreover, we have found that the most of $\mathrm { L i } / \mathrm { H }$ configuration is the one with five occupied 6c sites in the cavity of PTI from the statistics of these configurations (Figure S6, Supporting Information) with representative intensity profiles shown in Figure S7 (Supporting Information). The PTI structure with this $\mathrm { L i } / \mathrm { H }$ configuration is depicted in Figure 3a and has three types of N sites: N within the triazine ring (N-1), N binding to H (N-2), and deprotonated N (N-3) of imide linkage groups, as highlighted with different colors. With this PTI structure model, density functional theory (DFT) calculation was performed to investigate its electronic structure with projected density of states (pDOs) that provides the contribution of each individual atom to the band structure (Figure 3b). The main contribution of the valence band maximum is from Cl and $\textsf { N } \ 2 \mathrm { p }$ states, and the conduction band minimum composes of hybridized C and $\textnormal { N } 2 \textnormal { p }$ states. The contribution of N states to the band structure is further split by site. As shown in the resultant pDOS (Figure 3b), the N-3 site has a sharp contribution of states just below the valence band maximum in addition to the N-1 site, while there is no apparent contribution from the N-2 site. This variation of three N sites is also indicated by the partial charge density map of carriers in the energy range from Fermi level to the valence band maximum (Figure 3c). The contribution of the N-1 and N-3 sites could involve the photoabsorption of PTI crystal for generating the electron-hole pairs during photocatalysis. The combined analysis of iDPC-STEM image and DFT calculation emphasizes the crucial role of $\mathrm { L i } / \mathrm { H }$ configuration in PTI for clarifying the electronic structure that is strongly responsible for photoabsorption.

One of the critical factors for the photoactivity of crystalline CNs is the migration of photogenerated electrons and holes, which is dominated by the internal electric field within crystals. DPC-STEM technique has been developed for directly visualizing local electromagnetic fields inside materials from nanoscale to atomic scale.17,27] With the sub-A electron probe, the atomic electric field imaging of PTI crystal along the [001] direction is obtained from segmented-detector DPC images. As shown in Figure 4a,b, the image contrast of electric field strength map indicates the strength of (projected) in-plane electric field, and the color and intensity of electric field vector map correspond to the relative direction and strength of electric field at each position in the image. Since the electric field of the crystal is the superposition of atomic electric fields of isolated atoms that point radially outward from the nucleus, the sharp minima of projected electric field not only appear at the nuclear positions (P-sites) of atoms, but also could arise from high symmetry points of adjacent atoms, as depicted in Figure S8 (Supporting Information) with a schematic diagram. The electric field minima appear at the bridge midpoints (B-sites) of triazine units as the electric field of the adjacent atoms with the opposite direction nearly cancels out and thus forms a net zero projected electric field. Similarly, the minimum electric fields also locate on the symmetry points: the hexagon centers (S-1 sites) of triazine units and the midpoints (S-2 sites) of bridged imide groups. As shown in the projected electric field maps, all electric field minima are the dark dots. These obtained mappings demonstrate a sharp electric field of Cl anions within the framework cavities of PTI crystal and a localized, circle-shaped electric field of triazine units that are from overlapping of superimposed columns of C and N atoms.

The coupling electric field between Li ions and adjacent atoms has also been revealed in the enlarged maps with a local arrow vector in each position (Figure 4c,d). Two Li ions in the gap between Cl anion and bridged N atoms are indicated by the corresponding dDPC and iDPC images (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Electric field continues from the imide N atom toward the Cl ion and is clearly visible in the gap region where

Li ions locate, while there is a weak contrast for the region without Li ions (H is supposed to be there) as the electric field is canceled to be nearly zero by the outward field between Cl and the framework. We simulated the atomic electric field of PTI and the detail is included in the Experimental Section. The observed electric field distribution (along [001] axis) of PTI in the experiment agrees excellently with the simulation (Figure S10, Supporting Information) that also demonstrates the subtle feature of electric field at Li sites. Thus, both experimental and simulated data confirm the coupling electric field between Li ions and adjacent atoms in PTI structure.

DFT calculations were further performed to reveal local electronic structures of Li ions with adjacent atoms. The electronic local function (ELF) from DFT calculations can be used as an indicator of bond status between atoms,[32] where a high ELF value corresponds to a covalent bond and a low ELF value indicates an ionic bond. The calculated ELF result of PTI (Figure S11a, Supporting Information) shows that the ELF value between Li and adjacent $_ \mathrm { N }$ is quite small $_ { ( < 0 . 1 ) }$ , thus confirming the formation of $_ { \mathrm { L i - N } }$ ionic bond. The charge difference distribution map (Figure S11b, Supporting Information) further indicates the migration of electrons from Li to adjacent N and Cl. Therefore, the formation of this built-in electric field between Li and surrounding N not only extends the $\pi$ conjugated systems, but also could constitute an additional delivery path of charge carriers between adjacent triazine units, which could facilitate the migration of photogenerated electrons in corrugated layers of PTI (Figure 4e). The effective in-plane migration of charge carriers makes the side planes as active facets rather than basal planes for photocatalysis in hexagonal prism PTI crystal, which is a fundamental proof for the reactive facet of $\{ 1 0 0 \}$ planes in PTI crystals as discussed in previous research.11]

Moreover, quantitative comparison between experimental and simulated electric field strength maps was performed to verify the reliability of DPC-STEM observation. It shows a similar range for the intensity around connected triazine rings (Figure S12a, Supporting Information) and for the signal around H and Li sites (Figure S12b, Supporting Information) in experiment and simulation. But, the internal electric field strength of triazine rings is obviously weaker than their outer electric field strength, leading to a large discrepancy of simulated and experimental line profiles in Figure S12a (Supporting Information). This phenomenon is also well recorded in experimental electric field strength map (Figure 4a) of PTI crystal by DPC-STEM. The effect of thickness and defocus on the electric field strength map of PTI structure has also been investigated in DPC-STEM simulation, as shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information). The overall intensity of line profiles around connected triazine units and around Li and H atoms changes with the variation of thickness and defocus, where there is still a discrepancy of internal (corresponding to second/seventh peak) and outer strength (corresponding to first/eighth peak) of triazine rings (Figure S13a, Supporting Information) in simulation compared to the experiment data.

Inspired by the intercalation of Li ions into layered graphene (with aromatic rings) in lithium-ion batteries,33,34] we have further investigated the possibility of Li ions intercalated in-between triazine rings of PTI crystals by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements, which could provide local environment of Li ions in the structure. The $^ 7 \mathrm { L i }$ NMR spectrum (Figure S14a, Supporting Information) shows an axially symmetric shift tensor with the central transition peak split at O and $0 . 8 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ indicating two groups of local environments for Li ions.35,36]

The Li 1s XPS spectra (Figure S14b, Supporting Information) also displays two components centered at $5 6 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ corresponding to the $\mathrm { L i } { - } \mathrm { C l }$ and at $5 4 . 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ that can be assigned to Li bonded to N of triazine rings. The latter is similar to the finding of lithium-intercalated graphene.37] Both NMR and XPS results reveal the existence of intercalated Li ions in-between layers of triazine rings apart from the Li cooperated with Cl in the cavity of PTI structure. Previous studies also demonstrated the intercalation and deintercalation of Li ions in carbon nitride materials used as lithium-ion battery anode.38,39] The optimization of structure with intercalated Li ions was performed by DFT calculations. The resultant atomic architecture is illustrated in Figure S14c (Supporting Information). The measured Li content of PTI sample is $4 . 7 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ and also close to the Li nominal composition $( 5 . 6 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ of intercalated Li structure in Figure S14c (Supporting Information) compared to the structure without intercalated Li ions $( 2 . 9 \ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ . The source of intercalated Li ions could be from the eutectic molten salt (lithium chloride and potassium chloride) used in synthesis. Moreover, we have simulated atomic electric field maps based on the PTI structures without and with Li ions intercalated into the interstitial columns of triazine rings (Figure 5a,b). Figure 5c shows that the line profile of connected triazine rings in simulated electric field strength map with intercalated Li is largely close to the experimental one because the internal electric field of triazine rings could be partially canceled by the electric field of intercalated Li. This not only explains the discrepancy between experimental and simulated data in Figure S12a (Supporting Information), but also verifies the presence of intercalated Li ions between triazine rings of PTI crystals. The good agreement in the quantitative comparison of simulation and experiment (Figure 5 and Figure S12 (Supporting Information)) well support our DPCSTEM observation in PTI crystal, especially for Li and H atoms.

# 3. Conclusion

We have achieved the atomic-resolution imaging of triazinebased crystalline CN, the PTI crystal, by the DPC-STEM technique with the merit of high contrast of light elements and the low dose imaging. The honeycomb-like structure and intercalated Cl anions of PTI are clearly determined by dDPC and iDPC images. We further resolve the configuration of the lightest elements Li and H within framework cavities of PTI, which is of significant importance for clarifying its electronic structure for photoabsorption. Moreover, the atomic electric field of PTI crystal is directly imaged for the first time and indicates the coupling electric field between Li and adjacent triazine units, which constitutes an additional delivery path for the in-plane migration of photogenerated carriers in the corrugated layer. The quantitative comparison of simulated and experimental DPC-STEM images further verifies the presence of intercalated Li ions between triazine rings of PTI crystals. These local structures obtained by the DPC-STEM imaging technique not only facilitate our comprehension on the photocatalysis mechanism of PTI crystal, but also would be beneficial for designing efficient photocatalysts. Furthermore, this study demonstrates the sensitivity of DPC-STEM technique for investigating the atomic electric field and its capability for revealing the local chemical bond.

An energy cutoff of $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and a $k$ point mesh density of $2 \pi \times 0 . 0 3$ A using the I-centered Monkhorst-Pack schemel46] were employed. The force and energy convergence criteria were $0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { e V } \ \mathring { \mathsf { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ eV, respectively. The structure was relaxed before the self-consistent calculation.

DPC-STEM Simulation: The structure model of PTI was adopted from literature.28] Simulation of DPC-STEM image was performed by the multislice approach on the $\mu { \mathsf { S T E M } }$ software package[47] with a voltage of $3 0 0 ~ \mathsf { k V } ,$ a convergence angle of 25 mrad, and the high-order aberration coefficients adopted from the experiment (low-order aberration (A1, A2, and B2) coefficients set to zero). The quantum excitation of phonons model was adopted in the $\mu { \sf S T E M }$ for handling all thermal scattering effects in simulation. Four-sector ADF images were simulated with a collection angle of 5-18 mrad and then applied to generate the dDPC and iDPC images with the approach described in the literature.6] The iDPC-STEM image of PTI was then simulated with a focus-thickness series, as shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). The ${ \mathsf { C O M } } _ { x }$ and ${ \mathsf { C O M } } _ { \gamma }$ images were applied to simulate atomic electric field maps of PTI. All simulated images displayed in this work were convolved with a $7 0 \mathsf { p m }$ probe by a 2D Gaussian function.
