# In Situ Synthesis of Photoactive Polymers on a Living Cell Surface via Bio-Palladium Catalysis for Modulating Biological Functions

Ruilian Qi, Hao Zhao, Xin Zhou, Jian Liu, Nan Dai, Yue Zeng, Endong Zhang, Fengting Lv,\* Yiming Huang, Libing Liu, Yilin Wang,\* and Shu Wang\*

Abstract: Cell surface engineering with functional polymers is an effective strategy to modulate cell activity. Here, a biopalladium catalyzed polymerization strategy was developed for in situ synthesis of conjugated polymers on living cell surfaces. Through Sonagashira polymerization, photoactive polyphenyleneethynylene (PPE) is synthesized on the cell surface via cell-generated bio-Pd catalyst. The in situ formed PPE is identified by excellent light-harvest capacity and blue fluorescence on the surfaces of E. coli and C. pyrenoidosa. Besides imaging microbes for tracing the polymerization process, PPE also exhibits enhanced antibacterial activity against E. coli. It can also augment the ATP synthesis of C. pyrenoidosa through enlarging the light absorption and accelerating the cyclic electron transport of the algae. With this bio-metal catalyzed polymerization method, functional polymers can be synthesized in situ on the living cell surface.

Cell surface engineering is an important method for regulating cell functions, such as protecting cells against harsh environments,[l improving biosynthesis efficiency,[2] and endowing cells with more functionalities.3l Inspired by this promising method, several functional materials such as metal/semiconductor nanoparticles,[4] polymers,] and metalorganic complexes[] have been explored as coating materials on the cellular surface. To date, considerable research has been focused on cell surface modification by functional polymers owing to the increased availability of secondary interactions and derivation from grafted polymers. However, owing to the repulsion-caused low conjugation efficiency of polymer to cell surface, this modification usually need a large excess of reactive polymers.] To increase the utilization of polymers, an in situ polymerization strategy has recently been applied to live cells. Besides the high grafting density, the structure of in situ formed polymers also matches well with the charge distribution and biologically functional regions on cell surface, leading to a high binding affinity between polymers and living cells. The present in situ polymerization studies mainly focus on the radical polymerization,10] however, the inherent free radicals generated from initiators and monomers will induce potential cytotoxicity to cells. Moreover, the detection of polymer formation process on the surface is also difficult. It is urgent to develop novel surface polymerization methods to solve these key issues.

Pd-mediated cross-coupling reactions for labeling and drug synthesis has been realized in living cell systems,[11] which ensure the feasibility of this strategy on cell surface. Cell surface generally possesses multiple Pd binding sites,[12] including carboxyl, amino, and phosphate groups, which favor the formation of Pd nanoparticles. Furthermore, Pd nanoparticles formed on bacterial surface (bio-Pd) displayed higher catalytic activity in Heck reaction than colloid Pd.[13] In this work, we design and construct a new in situ polymerization system to modify cell surface with photoactive polyphenyleneethynylene (PPE), in which the Sonagashira polymerization is catalyzed by the in situ formed surface bioPd catalysts (Scheme1a). The synthesized PPE on cell surface is positively charged, therefore the electrostatic interactions facilitate the binding to the negatively charged cell surface. Since PPE emits blue fluorescence, the polymer formation process on cell surface can be easily traced and characterized. As shown in Scheme 1b, by virtue of the excellent light-harvesting ability of conjugated polymers,[14] their antibacterial activity against $E$ coli and the improvement of ATP synthesis of $C .$ pyrenoidosa make this novel in situ polymerization a promising strategy in cell surface engineering with simultaneous modulation of biological functions.

Cationic 1,4-bis(oxy-hexamethylene-trimethylammonium bromide)-2,5-diiodobenzene (M1) and 1,4-bis(oxy-hexamethylene-trimethylammonium bromide)-2,5-diethynylbenzene (M2) were chosen as monomers for polymerization (Figure 1a). The characterization of M1 and M2 are given in the Supporting Information, Figures S1 and S2. The positive charges of M1 and M2 can greatly increase their water solubility to ensure them polymerization in aqueous environment. Furthermore, the positive charges also favor the process of binding to negatively charged cell surface via electrostatic interactions, which put them in close proximity of the bio-Pd catalyst on the surface. Firstly, the Sonogashira polymerizations of M1 and M2 (molar ratio of $\mathbf { M 1 } / \mathbf { M } 2 =$ 1:1.3) in aqueous solution were studied. The polymer conversion efficiencies (relative to M2) were calculated by HPLC. As shown in Figure 1b, the polymer conversion efficiency was increased along with the increas of reaction time. At low monomer concentration $( [ \mathbf { M 1 } ] = 1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathbf { M } )$ , the conversion was relatively high and reached $3 2 \%$ and $4 9 \%$ after reaction for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ and $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ , respectively. While increasing monomer concentration up to $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ the conversion efficiency decreased to $2 1 \%$ at $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ and $2 9 \%$ at $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ . After polymerization, the hydrogen $\mathrm { ( H c ) }$ and the peak of $\mathrm { C - H }$ stretch $( 3 2 2 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ in the terminal acetylene were obviously decreased (Figure 1c,d), which indicated the consumption of M2. Meanwhile, the peak of $\mathrm { C } { \equiv } { \bf C }$ stretch shift from $2 0 9 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ to $2 2 7 6 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , which suggested the formation of bis-substituted alkynyl.[15] Both NMR and IR results confirmed the polymerization took place in the aqueous solution. Derived from the GPC (Supporting Information, Figure S3), the molecular weight of the resulting polymers formed at 100 and $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer concentrations are both about. $3 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 3 }$ (ca. 9 repeat units). Furthermore, the HPLC-MS analysis demonstrated the formation of conjugated polymers on cell surface (Supporting Information, Figure S4). Since the synthesized PPE emits blue fluorescence rather than monomers, the significant increase of fluorescence intensity of the reaction solution further indicated the formation of PPE (Supporting Information, Figure S5).

Following the formation of bio-Pd nanoparticles, the in situ polymerization reaction was subsequently carried out by adding monomers M1 and M2 to the solution of $E _ { \ast }$ coli coated with bio-Pd. The polymerization process was traced by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). A significant and dose-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed on the surface of $E _ { \ast }$ coli but not on $E .$ coli only treated with monomers (Figure 2e,f). Combined with the significant enhancement of fluorescent intensity on cell surface, it can be concluded that Pd-catalyzed Sonogashira polymerization took place on $E$ coli surface. Considering that the synthesized PPE possesses cationic charge and hydrophobic moieties that contributed to bactericidal ability,[17] the bacterial viability and cell morphology before and after polymerization reaction were tested and shown in Figure $2 \mathrm { g }$ and the Supporting Information, Figure S6. The control groups in the presence of Pd catalyst or monomers only had very less influence on the viability of $E _ { \ast }$ coli $( 9 0 \% )$ . With the monomer concentration increase from $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ to $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ , the bacterial viability after polymerization decreased to $2 8 \%$ Obvious membrane damages were also observed for this case (Supporting Information, Figure S6). The increased bactericidal ability at high monomer concentration might ascribe to the formation of polymers with high polymerization degree. Compared with the surface modification by coating with preformed or in situ polymerization using sodium ascorbate as electron donor (Supporting Information, Figure S7), the coating efficiency of in situ polymerization in the presence of HCOONa was slightly lower, but it exhibited a significantly difference to two kinds of bacteria ( $E .$ coli and P. aeruginosa). Obvious blue fluorescence and high bacteria killing was observed on $E _ { \ast }$ coli with strong reductive ability,[18] while rarely no fluorescence and low bacteria killing was shown on $P .$ aeruginosa without reductive ability. Based on the above results, it can be concluded that the in situ synthesized polymers can not only image the $E$ coli for tracing the polymerization process, but also exhibit good antibacterial capacity.

After successfully realizing the Sonogashira coupling on the surface of $E$ . coli, we extended the in situ strategy to modify the surface of $C .$ pyrenoidosa. The exothermic process. of titrating $\mathrm { P d } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ ions into $C .$ pyrenoidosa and large binding constant $( K _ { \mathrm { a } } = 3 . 2 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ : \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } )$ suggested that $\mathrm { P d } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ ions strongly bind with $C .$ pyrenoidosa mainly through electrostatic interactions (Figure 3a), same to that of $E$ coli. After sodium ascorbate reduction, the presence of Pd elements on $C .$ pyrenoidosa surface was identified by the EDS map (Figure 3b), and the nanoparticles with lattice space of $1 . 9 5 \bar { \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } }$ (200) were also observed (Figure 3c). XPS further confirmed the generation of $\mathrm { P d } ^ { 0 }$ species (Supporting Information, Figure S8). Then Pd-catalyzed Sonogashira polymerization was monitored by CLSM after adding monomers M1 and M2 (Figure 3d). With bio-Pd catalyst on C. pyrenoidosa surface, blue fluorescence was observed around the perimeter of red emission of $C .$ pyrenoidosa. It is noted that the intensity of blue fluorescence increased by 10 and 40 times than that of $C .$ pyrenoidosa treated with $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ and $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomers, respectively (Supporting Information, Figure S9). These results indicated that the Pd-catalyzed Sonogashira polymerization successfully took place on $C .$ pyrenoidosa surface. The in situ polymerization had slight influence on the viability of C. pyrenoidosa (Supporting Information, Figure S10). As ATP is an important product of photosynthesis, the influence of bio-Pd catalyzed Sonogashira polymerization on C. pyrenoidosa surface on ATP biological synthesis was studied. After polymerization reaction for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , the $C .$ pyrenoidosa solution was irradiated under white light for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and then the produced ATP was measured and the result was summarized in Figure 4a. Compared to C. pyrenoidosa without any treatment, $4 2 \%$ and $1 1 5 \%$ increase of ATP production were observed for the Pd-loaded C. pyrenoidosa treated with $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer and $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer, respectively. For $C .$ pyrenoidosa without Pd loading, upon treatment with $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer and $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer, only $1 1 \%$ and $2 2 \%$ ATP production increase were observed, respectively, which verified that the in situ synthesized polymers augmented the ATP synthesis of $C .$ pyrenoidosa. The augmentation mechanism of ATP synthesis of. $C .$ pyrenoidosa was further investigated. Our previous work has proved that conjugated polymer nanoparticles (CPNs) can augment the photosynthetic activity of chloroplast by broadening the light capture range and accelerating the electron transport rates in photosystem II (PS II) of chloroplast.[19] As shown in Figure 4b, the fluorescence emission spectra of reaction mixture overlapped well with the absorption of C. pyrenoidosa, which indicated that the light transformed from the in situ synthesized polymers could be utilized by C. pyrenoidosa for photosynthesis. The increase of absorption and trapped light energy per reaction center, ABS/RC and TR/RC, did confirm the enlarged light absorption and capture in the $C .$ pyrenoidosa with surface coupling (Figure 4c,d). To further confirm the effect of light harvesting by the in situ formed polymers, the ATP production of $C .$ pyrenoidosa under the irradiation of red light (which cannot excite the polymers) was measured (Supporting Information, Figure S11). The slight decrease of ATP under red light irradiation and $1 1 5 \%$ increase of ATP production under white light irradiation indicated that the enlarged light harvesting induced by the polymers play a major role in the increase of ATP production. However, the electron transport rate (ETR) of $\mathrm { P S } \amalg$ was largely declined by the in situ polymerization (Supporting Information, Figure S12a) and the corresponding opening degree of PS II reaction centers $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { q P } } )$ was also decreased (Supporting Information, Figure S12b). This was associated with the PS II photoinhibition caused by the in situ synthesized polymers which enlarged the light absorption of $C .$ pyrenoidosa.[20] Since both the activities of $\mathrm { P S } \amalg$ and PS I contribute to the photosynthesis efficacy, the energy conversion efficiency of PS I was measured. The effective quantum yield Y(I) of the bio-Pd loaded $C .$ pyrenoidosa exhibited obvious increase when tread with $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ monomer (Figure 4e). This result suggested that the increased ATP synthesis was related to the augmentation in PS I activity.

The mechanism of enhanced ATP synthesis was illustrated in Figure 4 f. Generally, light induced ATP synthesis is coupled with two pathways of electron transport: linear electron transport from PS II to PSI $( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } \  \ \mathrm { P S I I } \ $ plastoquinone, $\mathrm { ~ Q ~ } $ cytochrome $\mathbf { b } _ { 6 } \mathbf { f } \ $ plastocyanin, $\mathrm { P c } $ $\mathrm { P S } \mathrm { ~ I ~ } $ ferredoxin, $\mathrm { F d } ~  ~ \mathrm { N A D P ^ { + } }$ and cyclic electron transport around PS I $\mathrm { ( P S ~ I ~ \to ~ F d ~ \to ~ O ~ \to ~ b _ { 6 } f ~ \to ~ P c ~ \to ~ }$ PS I). The decreased PS II activity and increased PS I activity indicated that the $C .$ pyrenoidosa with surface coupling had an elevated cyclic electron transport which usually drove more ATP production. That is to say, the in situ polymerization on C. pyrenoidosa surface regulated the electron-transport pathways to produce more ATP.

In conclusion, we developed a new in situ polymerization strategy to modify cell surface with conjugated polymers for regulating cell activity. Taking advantage of the negative charge of cell surfaces, bio-Pd nanoparticles were loaded on cell surface after reduction by the surface enzyme or reductant. The in situ polymerization reaction on cell surface was catalyzed by the bio-Pd catalyst and the polymerization process was easily traced by fluorescence imaging technique. Besides exhibiting good antibacterial activity towards $E$ coli, the in situ formed photoactive conjugated polymers can also enhance the ATP synthesis of $C .$ pyrenoidosa by enlarging the light absorption and accelerating the cyclic electron transport. The proposed bio-Pd catalyzed polymerization provides a new avenue for cell surface engineering to regulate cell activity for desirable biological applications with in situ synthesized functional polymers.
