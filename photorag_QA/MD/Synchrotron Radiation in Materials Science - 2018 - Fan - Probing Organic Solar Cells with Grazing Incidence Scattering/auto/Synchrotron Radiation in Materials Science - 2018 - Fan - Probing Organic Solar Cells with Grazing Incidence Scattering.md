# 7.1 Introduction

The increasing demand for energy on a worldwide scale has put renewable energy sources in the focus of global interest. Considerable industrial and academic interest has been evinced in several different renewable energy sources. Among them, solar power is particularly attractive as it is abundantly available. Solar power is converted directly into electricity by photovoltaic devices, which makes these devices easy to use in real life applications. Among the different technologies used in photovoltaic devices, those applied for silicon solar cells have had successful development over many years. Thus, silicon-based photovoltaic devices have the largest market share today and, depending on the country, have started to significantly contribute to the production of electricity. Despite the big success of silicon solar cells, alternative technologies for photovoltaic devices have also attracted interest. Several nonsilicon technologies have been developed. Among them, the so-called organic solar cells mark a highly interesting class of photovoltaic devices. In short, organic solar cells have carbon-based active layers the use of which opens new possibilities, for example, for application and fabrication. Apart from silicon solar cells, organic solar cells cover a large variety of approaches to realize the photovoltaic effect. Among these are small molecule devices [1-7], polymer:small molecules devices [1, 8-17], all-polymer devices [1, 18-20], hybrid and dye sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) [1, 21-33]. There are several advantages that arise from the use of carbon-based molecules as an alternative technology. Organic solar cells are typically light-weight and flexible devices can be prepared [13, 34-38]. The complete device-processing route can be realized without the need for (ultrahigh) vacuum. Being based on wet chemical approaches (carbon-based materials are dissolved in liquids for processing), alternative technologies for spray coating and print techniques are accessible [5, 7, 13, 26, 39-44]. Moreover, a large variety of materials can be used for the active layer of organic soar cells and further progress can come from newly synthesized photoactive materials as well [10, 45]. In particular, the alternative processing and the potential use of print techniques give hope for low-cost production, in case the materials used can be sourced in large quantities. In summary, organic solar cells have emerged as a new competitive and alternative technology for the well-established silicon photovoltaic devices.

For triple-junction polymer solar cells Yang group [16] demonstrated power conversion efficiencies above $1 1 \%$ in 2014 and the reported efficiencies for other organic solar cells designs have also crossed the $1 0 \%$ limit [17, 46]. Despite ever-increasing efficiencies and large scientific efforts that have been recorded, the understanding of the fundamentals of organic solar cells is still limited. So far, it is well agreed among scientists, that the mechanisms are very different from light harvesting in silicon solar cells and that for the different types of organic solar cells different mechanisms can occur [1, 22, 32, 47]. The use of carbon-based materials yields a low dielectric permittivity and thus, low screening of the photo-generated charge carrier pairs. Therefore, an exciting, meaning a Coulomb-bound electron-hole pair, is generated by the absorbed light instead of having immediately free charge carriers as in silicon solar cells. The photoexcited states are confined to a small volume and the energy barrier imposed by the Coulomb interaction (on the order of 0.1 to 1eV) needs to be overcome to generate free charge carriers. As a consequence, in organic solar cells, the combination of a donor and acceptor material with properly chosen energy levels is necessary to separate the charge carriers. However, due to the short lifetime of the strongly bound exciton, the very limited diffusion length of the excitons is a key problem in devices. The characteristic exciton diffusion lengths reported are only on the order of $1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ [48-55]. This implies that the local morphology of the donor-acceptor interface will have a strong influence on the overall device performance. The morphology needs to be optimized in addition to the properly chosen highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of the donor and acceptor materials.

As a consequence, a single photoactive material (small molecule, polymer) is not sufficient for having a well-working organic solar cell, because photo-generated excitons cannot be split and no electricity can be harvested. It needs the additional presence of an acceptor layer, which is realized in device geometries with a planar interface between the donor and the acceptor. Such devices show similarities with the layer architecture of silicon solar cells. Electricity can be harvested, but efficiencies remain very moderate since not all photo-generated excitons can reach the donor-acceptor interface. For the full absorption of the incoming light, typically, layer thickness above $1 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ is necessary despite the high absorption coefficient of the organic materials used in organic solar cells. Compared with $1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ exciton diffusion length, only a small fraction of excitons created within the full film can be harvested and the bilayer geometry cannot reach high efficiency values. A way to overcome this problem is to replace the planar interface with a so-called bulk heterojunction (BHJ) geometry [8, 15, 56-59]. In a BHJ solar cell the active layer has a large interface between the donor and acceptor materials. So far, mostly self-organization processes have been used for the realization of the BHJ geometry. The obtained morphologies are still nonideal and nonequilibrium, which constrains the achieved efficiency values and introduces device instabilities. The tailoring of the BHJ geometry has been adopted in many different approaches and was in the focus of many research teams since it is commonly believed that an improved and optimized morphology will yield higher solar cell efficiencies. To establish a structure-function relationship for organic solar cells, it is necessary to characterize the structure of the active layers on length scales relevant for the exciton diffusion and splitting, and therefore, it is crucial to get information on the nanometer to several nanometer range [15].

The different conduction mechanisms in organic solar cells impose additional challenges to the optimized structure generation and its characterization. In particular, if based on conjugated, semiconducting polymers, the anisotropy of the charge carrier mobility is an important aspect. Most likely, conjugated polymers are semicrystalline and thus only part crystallizes while the rest remains amorphous. Because the crystalline phase has a much higher conductivity as compared with the amorphous phase, knowledge about both phases is required. High conductivity is found in the direction of $\pi$ -orbital overlap and along alternating single/double bonds [60, 61]. This results in high conductivity along particular crystallographic axes and low conductivity in other directions. In turn, the degree of crystallinity, the size of the crystal areas and the orientation of the crystallites are additional important factors in organic solar cells. As a consequence, structural information is required on the molecular level in addition to that on longer length scales [61].

Using advanced scattering techniques such morphology information from the molecular to the mesoscale can be gained. Complementary to the scattering experiments, real space imaging techniques can give valuable information. Optical microscopy provides an overview on large-scale structures and sample heterogeneities. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) give information about surface structures on a local scale with high resolution. However, surface structure information is only part of the morphology information required to fully understand organic solar cells, because the devices are functional stacks that consist of several layers. In particular, the donor-acceptor interface is of highest relevance and typically not located at the surface but buried inside the functional stack, independent of whether the device architecture follows a bilayer or BHJ geometry. The donor-acceptor interface and thus the inner morphology contribute to the charge carrier generation, whereas the surface does not. Several examples were reported, where the surface structure was shown to be very different from the inner morphology [42, 57, 62]. As a consequence, the use of advanced scattering techniques to probe the inner morphology of active layers or materials used in organic solar cells has gained very high attention. In particular, grazing incidence X-ray scattering (Gixs) is largely used for this purpose. Because organic solar cells are thin film devices, a standard transmission geometry has very limited use. From a thin film hardly any scattering signal can be gained, simply because of the small scattering volume. Moreover, the solid support (typically glass) is significantly thicker and dominates the transmission signal. Changing the scattering experiment from a transmission to a reflection geometry as well as using grazing incidence conditions solves this problem by the footprint effect.

As a result, thin films can be probed with sufficiently good statistics. Moreover, the wave guiding enhancement of the scattering intensity can be used in these experiments. Thus, the reason for resorting to grazing incidence is not just for a geometrical effect, but for exploiting the enhancement of the field by interference of incoming and outgoing fields and multiple reflections [63].

This chapter is based on the very successful invited progress report in [64]. The focus is on grazing incidence small angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS) and grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) [39, 63-68], although similar techniques using neutrons instead of X-rays are also emerging [69-71]. Both being advanced scattering techniques, GISAXS and GIWAXS, probe typical thin film structures that are relevant in organic solar cells. From a combination of GISAXS and GIWAXS, the structural information about all relevant length scales in organic solar cells can be gained. As a consequence, both methods are increasingly applied and the number of publications with GISAXS and/or GIWAXS on materials and films used in organic solar cell research is strongly increasing. Although valuable information for a deeper understanding of the complex morphologies and their effects in organic solar cells has already been achieved from both GISAXS and GIWAXS, more methods are still under development. As a consequence, it is foreseeable, that the use of GISAXS and GIWAXS will increase further. Along this line, the motivation of this review is, on the one hand, to summarize the basics of these scattering techniques and, on the other hand, to demonstrate the power of GISAXS and GIWAXS by a selection of examples from recent literature. Particular attention has been focused on GISAXS and GIWAXS studies on polymer-based structures. While the invited progress report in the journal Advanced Materials has laid special emphasis on the so far mostly used polymer poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT), the present review accounts for other conjugated polymer systems that have demonstrated to have higher power conversion efficiencies, as well.

This chapter has the following structure: First, the fundamental aspects of the GISAXS technique are introduced, followed by a description of GIWAXS also on a basic level. Next, examples for the use of GIWAXS and for the use of GISAXS in the analysis of structures relevant to organic solar cells are presented. A summary concludes the review.

# 7.2 Grazing Incidence Small Angle X-ray Scattering (GISAXS)

GISAXS has very strong similarities with common small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) using transmission geometry [72, 73]. A grazing incidence geometry is used instead of a transmission geometry [64-73]. All angles are probed with respect to the sample surface, which is located in the $( x , y )$ -plane. The absence of lateral structures in the sample gives rise to pure specular scattering, fulfilling the law of optics $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } = \alpha _ { \mathrm { f } }$ [75]. The presence of lateral deviations of the refractive index gives rise to nonspecular, so-called diffuse scattering, which fulfills $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } \neq \alpha _ { \mathrm { f } } .$ Thus, this diffuse scattering contains the desired information about the morphology of the probed film, for example, the active layer of an organic solar cell.

$$
\vec { q } = ( q _ { x } , q _ { y } , q _ { z } ) = \vec { k } _ { \mathrm { f } } - \vec { k } _ { \mathrm { i } }

$$

Thus, specular scattering occurs along the $q _ { z }$ direction only. It is probed by a scan along the $q _ { z }$ direction in reciprocal space, which is done in the classical reflectivity measurements [76, 77]. From the diffuse or off-specular scattering, lateral information is probed at $q _ { | | } = ( q _ { x } , q _ { y } ) \neq 0$ [73]. Owing to $q _ { x } \ll q _ { y }$ in the GISAXS geometry, in a GISAXS experiment mainly the $q _ { y }$ dependence is probed and the $q _ { x }$ contribution can be neglected. Thus, strictly speaking, lateral structures in the direction perpendicular to the $\mathrm { X }$ -ray beam are addressed with GISAXS [66, 68, 78].

The selected incident and exit angle $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } }$ and $\alpha _ { \mathrm { f } }$ have to be taken into account while determining the depth, from which the observed scattering originates. For $\alpha _ { \mathrm { { i } } } > \alpha _ { \mathrm { { c } } }$ the $\mathrm { X }$ -ray beam has a high penetration depth and averages over structures within the entire depth of the film. Thus, by tuning the incident angle, near-surface structures can be separated from those in the inner film. Regarding active layers as used in OPV applications, the GISAXS experiment with $\alpha _ { \mathrm { { i } } } > \alpha _ { \mathrm { { c } } }$ (full film information) is, however, frequently complemented with surface imaging techniques such as AFM [15, 42, 62]. In addition, the tuning of the incident angle allows for making use of the so-called wave guiding enhancement of the scattering intensity. In the dynamic regime, changes of angles smaller than $0 . 0 1 ^ { \circ }$ can result in the effective flux altering by a factor of 20 [63].

In a practical GISAXS experiment, the experimental settings are very similar to SAXS experiments, which underlines the overall similarity between both techniques. The use of a point-shaped X-ray beam is essential, which is defined by the collimation system [73]. Commonly, the complete flight path has to be evacuated to protect the background from air or window scattering, which can spoil the GISAXS signal significantly. With the sample-detector distance the probed $q$ range is set according to the requirements (e.g., what length scales should be measured?). A 2D area detector turned out to be extremely beneficial and thus is used in most GISAXS experiments [68]. The necessary corrections such as for the Ewald sphere curvature are minor as compared with GIWAXS. The direct beam is blocked with a beam stop to avoid its presence on the sensitive 2D detector. Depending on the incident angle, the specular peak position or the full central line of the detector around $\psi = 0$ are also blocked by either a point-shaped or a rod-shaped beam stop. For $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } \approx \alpha _ { \mathrm { c } }$ both the specular and the Yoneda peak overlap, which is disadvantageous for resolving large-scale structures since their scattering contribution is falling behind the beamstop and is hidden under the very strong specular intensity [78]. Still, GISAXS experiments with such settings are frequently performed, as the intensity is highest in such configurations, which might help to detect weak scattering features at large $q$ values. For $\alpha _ { \mathrm { { i } } } > \alpha _ { \mathrm { { c } } }$ the specular and the Yoneda peak are well separated along the $z$ -axis of the detector, which allows for higher resolution and in some cases an easier analysis as explained earlier. However, intensity drops very rapidly with increasing incident angle, thereby imposing limits for the selection of $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } }$ even for experiments at powerful synchrotron radiation sources. Sample exposure times (counting times) have to be optimized to avoid radiation damage by an intense X-ray beam and to gain sufficient statistics for data analysis. A particular setting depends strongly on the used instrument as well as on the sample to be analyzed.

With the progress of recent laboratory X-ray sources and set-ups, GISAXS experiments have become feasible at such settings [94-97] and thus, are no longer purely restricted to the use of synchrotron radiation, meaning they can be employed at large-scale facilities. This development will further increase the use of GISAXS as a powerful structure characterization method, although still a significant number of GISAXS studies make use of advanced synchrotron equipment and highly intense X-ray beams.

As an alternative to the hard X-ray radiation commonly used in GISAXS, the use of soft X-rays (below $1 \mathrm { k e V }$ ) and X-rays in the "tender" range $( 1 { - } 4 \mathrm { k e V } )$ have made tremendous progress [98]. With a change from hard to soft X-rays, meaning a decrease in the X-ray photon energy from about $1 0 \mathrm { k e V }$ to lower values, new possibilities arise. Given the high degree of specialization a change in the X-ray energy from $1 0 \mathrm { k e V }$ to about $1 \mathrm { k e V }$ requires the use of other instruments or even synchrotron sources for such studies. The main idea of using X-ray radiation with smaller energies is to work at the absorption edge of the material, which strongly affects the real and complex parts of the refractive index [99-102]. In order to avoid strong absorption, it is common to work in an energy regime just before the absorption edge or resonant peak, where the absorption part of the refractive index is very small. In the tender X-ray regime the naming tender GISAXS has started to establish. In the soft X-ray regime, GISAXS is so far called the grazing incidence resonant soft X-ray scattering (GI-RSoXs) [62]. Highly interesting for organic solar cells are the possibilities that arise from contrast tuning since complex systems based on more than two components can be approached by measuring at different X-ray energies, thereby having different contrast conditions. Moreover, both surface-sensitive and bulk-sensitive experiments can be realized by changing the energy of the X-ray beam [62]. As a consequence, it is easy to foresee that the number of GISAXS experiments not using hard X-rays will as well increase substantially.

# 7.3 Grazing Incidence Wide Angle X-ray Scattering (GIWAXS)

Grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXs) can be seen as an extension of GISAXS into the wide angle regime. The naming of GIWAXS stresses similarities with wide angle X-ray scattering (WAXs). However, still some confusion is found in literature regarding this naming. Sometimes, GIWAXS experiments are mistaken for GISAXS experiments, if the definitions agreed upon in the transmission experiments (SAXS to WAXS) are not applied [103]. In some articles, GIWAXS experiments are called 2D grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXD) [104], whereas usually a one-dimensional (1D) or point-like detector is used for GIXD measurements [80, 105]. Using a 1D detector enables a better movement on the Ewald sphere, which in turn gives a less-perturbed wide angle signal. Nevertheless, the use of a 2D detector reduces the overall required beamtime since large parts of $q$ space are measured simultaneously and no detector movement is required. As a consequence, such GIWAXS measurements are of particular use for kinetic studies.

In simple thinking, the change from GISAXS to GIWAXS is realized by decreasing the sample-detector distance from several meters to several centimeters. Bringing the 2D area detector closer to the sample position makes a larger angular range accessible with the same detector (size). Accordingly, each pixel on the detector collects intensity from a larger angular range, which turns into more signal, but also eventually more background in each pixel. The original GISAXS remains faintly visible in the center of reciprocal space, if not blocked by a beamstop.

In the GIWAXS geometry, several data corrections on the 2D patterns have to be performed to account for factors such as the detection efficiency, the solid angle variation, the flat-field, and the polarization of the X-ray beam [106]. The detector efficiency needs to be corrected for medium path attenuation as well as the detector sensor absorption as both depend on the detector angle. At larger angles the distance between the sample and the detector pixel measuring the intensity is larger and as a consequence, larger medium path attenuation occurs. The same effect occurs inside the detector and affects the absorption of X-ray photons by the detector. The solid angle correction accounts for a purely geometrical effect, since the measured intensity on a pixel is proportional to the solid angle subtended by that pixel. The flat-field correction accounts for the different sensitivity of the individual pixels of the detector and very likely is already automatically corrected by the detector software. In case polarized X-ray beams are used, as for instance synchrotron radiation is linearly polarized in the horizontal plane, the observed scattering field depends on the detector angle as well and polarization corrections have to be applied.

In the analysis of GIWAXS data, the information about the crystalline part of the active layer under investigation is commonly extracted. The complexity of this analysis depends on the degree of order of the probed film. Figure 7.2 summarizes four frequently observed scenarios of GIWAXS pattern in active layer materials for organic solar cells. In the case of a highly crystalline film with an orientation of the crystals parallel to the substrate surface, well-pronounced Bragg peaks appear on the 2D detector (Figure 7.2a). If a parallel and perpendicular orientation of crystallites is present in the film, Bragg peaks appear along the surface normal (vertical direction) and in horizontal direction (Figure 7.2b). Having a more textured film with domains oriented with an angular distribution around the horizontal alignment, the Bragg peaks along the vertical direction will broaden (Figure 7.2c). In case of powder-like films with a large degree of orientational disorder of the crystallites, these Bragg peaks smear out into Debye-Scherrer-like rings (Figure 7.2d). Many examples of active layer materials show GIWAXS patterns similar to the last two cases (Figure $\mathrm { 7 . 2 c , d } ,$ , if polymer materials are involved. This reflects the limited crystallinity and rather high disorder in such films. In contrast, small molecule films can exhibit a very high degree of crystallinity and can form complex crystal structures, which gives rise to a large number of diffraction spots on the 2D detector. In such cases, the analysis can be performed with software packages, as illustrated for example, by Breiby et al. [107]. Particular attention is thereby put to textured thin polycrystalline films on supporting substrates.

Likely, the analysis is performed on selected cuts from the 2D GIWAXS data. Instead of line cuts, as applied in GISAXS, sectors from the 2D data are integrated. The analysis stays simple if focusing on Bragg peak positions and widths via the Scherrer formalism. However, care has to be taken for conjugated polymers. Rivnay et al. have shown that the simple analysis, using the Scherrer equation, does not properly account for deviations of the lattice constant caused by, for example, the soluble alkyl side chains of conjugated polymers, such as P3HT [108]. The para-crystalline nature of the conjugated polymers gives rise to $g$ -values that are close to that of glass, hence the appearance of broadening Bragg peaks. Thus, for additional analysis, higher order Bragg peaks need to be used to overcome such problems of the analysis [108].

Similar to GISAXS, in GIWAXS the incident angle can be varied and different information is accessible:

1. $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } < \alpha _ { \mathrm { c } }$ (active layer): information about crystalline structures in the near-surface region of active layer   
2. $\alpha _ { \mathrm { c } }$ (active layer) $< \alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } < \alpha _ { \mathrm { c } }$ (support): information about the crystalline structures in the entire active layer   
3. $\alpha _ { \mathrm { c } } ( \mathrm { s u p p o r t } ) < \alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } \mathrm { . }$ information about crystalline structures in the active layer and of the support.

The third case $( \alpha _ { \mathrm { c } } ( \mathrm { s u p p o r t } ) < \alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } )$ typically suffers from a strong background from the support, in particular if amorphous materials, such as glass or polymer foils are used.

Concerning the experiment, the use of 2D detectors, compared to 1D or point-shaped detectors, has the advantage of collecting the intensities from several Bragg reflections simultaneously. This saves time during data acquisition and can be very beneficial for laboratory experiments using weak sources in order to achieve reasonable overall counting times. Moreover, in experiments where high-flux synchrotron radiation is used and radiation damage may occur to the sensitive conjugated polymer, using a 2D detector is beneficial due to shorter counting times. In spite of several benefits, the use of a 2D detector in grazing incidence geometry gives rise to some image distortions, which need to be accounted for in the analysis [109]. The 2D detector misses a part of the reciprocal space extending in the vertical direction. Figure 7.3 shows this by means of 2D GIWAXS data from a thin, as-cast thin, semicrystalline P3HT film [110]. With additional measurements the missing information can be obtained. For example, a geometry which locally satisfies the specular condition, for example, by tilting the sample with the incident angle to the Bragg angle of interest (e.g, originating from the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking), can be used in such a case [109]. Thus, GIWAXS suffers from additional problems beyond the easy corrections for detector efficiency, polarization, and absorbance [109].

As an alternative representation, the 2D GIWAXS data can be transformed into so-called sector plots [111]. A sector plot is a representation in a Cartesian coordinate system, which results from mapping the spherical geometry of reciprocal space into a Cartesian grid. As explained, for example, in Ref. [111] the initial data set in the common 2D GIWAXS presentation is broken up into a large number of equally spaced radial and azimuthal bins.

In a practical GIWAXS experiment, the experimental settings are very similar to GISAXS experiments, despite the shortened sample-detector distance. Slit sizes and collimation distances are comparable to having sufficient resolution for the analysis of Bragg peak widths. Again, a point-shaped X-ray beam is used and flight paths should ideally be evacuated although the sample-detector distance is shorter. A beamstop blocks the direct beam and parts of the GISAXS signal. Additional beamstops might be of use to block very strong Bragg reflections in case of highly oriented films. The alignment of the sample with respect to the incident angle becomes more difficult due to the seriously reduced $q$ resolution on the 2D detector. In combined GISAXS/GIWAXS experiments this problem is overcome by aligning the GISAXS geometry first and then switching the detector configuration to GIWAXS for the measurement. Again, the details of the setting depend on the instrument used and on the samples to be analyzed.

# 7.4 Probing the Active Layer Morphology with GlwAXS

To a very large extent GIWAXS is used to probe thin films and active layer morphologies of organic solar cells to determine the absence or presence of crystalline structures. The orientation of crystallites with respect to the substrate and the crystallographic parameters of the crystalline components are extracted from such measurements. With respect to conjugated polymer based organic solar cells, P3HT is among the most studied model systems. It is extensively studied as a homopolymer and in blends with electron acceptor materials such as the fullerene derivative [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) [42, 110-128]. Currently, the active layer material P3HT:PCBM is still among the most frequently studied materials in organic solar cells, although interest in other conjugated polymers is significantly growing [129-133]. In particular, since other combinations of conjugated polymers and electron acceptor materials have shown much higher solar cell efficiencies [131-133]. As a consequence, for P3HT and P3HT:PCBM, a large collection of GIWAXS (and GISAXS) data exists in literature.

Thus, the crystal structure of P3HT is well known and revisited in many studies, which include pure P3HT homopolymer as a reference system [110-128]. Commonly, the constitution of the conjugated polymer is of high importance. The polymer can consist of repeat units that are derived from a random isomer of the monomer (regiorandom) or of the same isomer (regioregular). Since regiorandom P3HT shows insufficient electric conductivity, regioregular P3HT is used in these investigations [104]. For a sufficiently low polydispersity and high regioregularity P3HT forms lamellar crystals via a $\pi - \pi$ stacking of the thiophene rings, which stack in a way that the alkane side chains can crystallize [111-125, 134-136]. These crystalline regions are separated by amorphous domains. Using GIWAXS, (or WAXS for volume samples) the unit cell of the homopolymer P3HT $( a , b ,$ and $c$ , see Figure 7.5a) can be probed. Typically, several higher order Bragg peaks are found (see Figure 7.5b), which allow for determination of the crystal type. The long-period $L _ { \mathrm { p } }$ introduced by the distance between lamellar crystalline and amorphous domains (see Figure 7.5a) causes scattering at smaller angles and can thus be seen by GISAXS or SAXS, if bulk samples are probed. The scattering contrast arises from the difference in the scattering length density between amorphous and crystalline P3HT. With the width of the crystalline domain $d _ { \mathrm { c } }$ and the width of the amorphous domain $d _ { \mathrm { a } }$ we find $L _ { \mathrm { p } } = d _ { \mathrm { a } } + d _ { \mathrm { c } }$ [125]. A combined SAXS-WAXS dataset for P3HT showing all these scattering features is seen in Figure 7.5b (with indexing) [125].

Owing to the reference plane introduced by the substrate (bottom electrode) for thin films of P3HT (or any conjugated polymer), the orientation of the P3HT crystallites with respect to the substrate surface gains additional importance. Two main configurations of the P3HT crystallites, namely edge-on and face-on, can be distinguished from GIwAXS [134]. The edge-on orientation refers to the chain backbone with the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking direction parallel to the substrate and the side chains normal to the substrate. In the face-on orientation, the $\pi { - } \pi$ stack is directed perpendicularly to the substrate and therefore, the side chains and the backbone chain are aligned in the plane of the substrate. Owing to the anisotropic conductivity, which is rather high along backbone chains and the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the edge-on configuration, the charge transport is best parallel to the substrate surface [134]. This implies conflicts for the exchange of charge carriers with the electrodes in an OPV device, which are stacked perpendicularly to the substrate. Thus, the face-on orientation is desired in OPV applications.

Figure 7.3 shows a characteristic 2D GIWAXS pattern for a semicrystalline P3HT film. Bragg peaks from the edge-on orientation (along (010)-direction) and face-on orientation (along (100)-direction) are both seen. Owing to the reciprocal space presentation with the $q _ { x y } = \sqrt { ( { q _ { x } } ^ { 2 } + { q _ { y } } ^ { 2 } ) }$ and $q _ { z }$ coordinate, the initially rectangular detector pattern on the pixel axis is perturbed as explained in the previous section. For a more detailed analysis, typically, regions from the 2D GIWAXS data are integrated as illustrated by the red boxes in Figure 7.3. Changes in the intensity from these regions can be linked with changes in the crystallinity of such films [110]. However, extraction of the absolute degree of crystallinity is rather difficult and not feasible from the GIwAXS data itself. Instead, the scattering experiments need to be complemented with other measurements, such as UV/vis spectroscopy to access this information [117].

Since the interface between the conjugated polymer P3HT and the electron acceptor (e.g., PCBM) is of outmost importance for the OPV device performance, many investigations have addressed this topic [42, 104, 117, 125, 135, 136]. Owing to the limited miscibility of P3HT and PCBM, three phases have to be considered since P3HT:PCBM is not a simple two phase system as many of the cartoons drawn in publications might suggest [117, 136-139]. A pure crystalline P3HT phase, a mixed three-component phase of amorphous P3HT, molecularly dissolved P3HT and a pure PCBM phase, which is amorphous or consists of very small crystallites, have to be considered (see Figure 7.6), which increases the complexity [136]. Ruderer et al. reported a linear dependence of the molecularly mixed amount of PCBM as a function of the total amount of PCBM in the film. Moreover, the authors observed, that $1 8 \mathrm { v o l \% }$ PCBM is molecularly dissolved in case of a 1:1 mixture of P3HT:PCBM, which shows that this amount is not negligible [117]. Kohn et al. deepened the understanding of the miscibility and aggregation of PCBM by systematic loading studies [125]. With respect to the scattering experiments, it is notable that the molecularly dissolved PCBM gives rise to a partial index matching of the X-ray scattering contrast, which makes the detection of the three phases difficult for some mixing ratios. In general, the crystallization of P3HT into lamellar domains sets the main length scale in the P3HT:PCBM structure formation. PCBM is displaced into the amorphous intra-lamellar regions, thereby swelling the lamellar stack. This structure formation is driven by crystallization, which is intrinsic to most semicrystalline polymers, and is followed by the enrichment, segregation, and crystallization of PCBM [125].

In more detail, all length scales depend on the exact used materials. For example, Huang et al. probed the influence of different fullerene types, $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ on the morphology and function of BHJ solar cells [121]. Using GIWAXS (see Figure 7.7) and GISAXS, two types of nanostructures were reported: (i) intercalated PCBM molecules at the boundary of the crystalline P3HT domains and within the amorphous P3HT domains and (ii) aggregated PCBM clusters in the PCBM domains. In GIWAXS, the (100) Bragg peak of the edge-on P3HT crystallites was recorded (see Figure 7.7a). Its position shifts with incorporation of PCBM where the shift depends on the type of PCBM $\mathrm { \ P C } _ { 6 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ or $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M } ,$ . Thus, due to the larger size of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ as compared with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ the lamellar spacing of the P3HT crystalline domains is larger in case of incorporated $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ . Moreover, higher order Bragg peaks are not found for $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ indicating a more perturbed structure. These structural differences reflect in differences in the achievable device efficiencies [121].

Given the high importance of the overall crystallinity and of the crystalline orientation for the device efficiency, attention was focused on the tailoring of P3HT films and P3HT-based active layers in many investigations [110, 114-119, 123]. For example, the impact of different solvents used in the film preparation was determined, as it is frequently reported that the solvent used in the deposition of the active layer strongly influences the device performance. Solvents with higher boiling point allow for a better organization of the molecular structure due to a slower evaporation [42, 140, 141]. Verploegen et al. compared chloroform and chlorobenzene as solvents for spin coating P3HT and P3HT:PCBM films [110].

Figure 7.8 shows the corresponding 2D GIWAXS pattern. It illustrates the impact of the solvent as well as the mixing ratio on the crystallinity of the films. P3HT as a homopolymer is already influenced by the used solvent. If spin coated from chlorbenzene, P3HT exhibits a stronger preferential orientation as compared with chloroform and edge-on orientation of the crystallites is dominant [110]. In contrast, spin coated film from chloroform show a pronounced (020) Bragg peak, indicating a large amount of face-on oriented crystals. Mixing P3HT with PCBM generally reduces the crystalline order for films out of both solvents. The used solvent seems not to have any impact on pure PCBM films. For both solvents amorphous PCBM or a random orientation of very small PCBM crystallites is observed (see Figure 7.8). Ruderer et al. used four solvents with different boiling points (chloroform, toluene, chlorobenzene, and xylene) in dissolving and spin coating active layers of P3HT and PCBM [42]. An increased crystalline size for high boiling point solvents was observed as a result of a better pre-ordering during the spin coating and an enhanced crystallization due to residual solvent in agreement with the work by Verploegen. In vertical direction, crystallite sizes were reported to increase from $1 2 . 6 \mathrm { n m }$ (chloroform) to $2 2 . 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (xylene). The horizontal crystal sizes followed the same trend [42].

In addition to the solvent used during the flm preparation, so-called post-production treatments of the films are commonly performed. While thermal annealing is a standard procedure [142-150], solvent vapor annealing is less frequently done [151-154]. Annealing at different temperatures and for different times, including different ways of cooling the sample to room temperature, was reported for thermal annealing protocols. For example, Huang et al. studied the influence of the cooling rate for thermal pre- and postannealing [149]. Similarly, in solvent vapor annealing also very different protocols can be found in the literature. Owing to the differences in the exact experimental settings, the film preparation, and the used conjugated polymer P3HT (differences in molecular weight, polydispersity, and end-groups), the reported values might give the impression of conflicting results. Thus, comparison between different experimental findings remains difficult and a direct copying of post-production treatment protocols from literature has limited success for achieving highly efficient devices.

In situ experiments making use of GIWAXS, as reported for example by Ver. ploegen et al., help toward a more fundamental understanding of the processes occurring during the annealing [146]. The same holds for in situ GIWAXS measurements during solvent vapor annealing [110]. Verploegen et al. observed, for example, that both, chloroform and tetrahydrofurane annealing causes swelling of the P3HT, accompanied with a decrease in the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distance and an increase in the crystalline order [110]. In addition, tetrahydrofurane induces crystallinity in the PCBM component. Other solvents, as for example hexane, induce swelling of the P3HT as well but do not allow for a re-arrangement of the poly. mer backbone and therefore do not improve the crystallinity of the P3HT. Compared with thermal annealing, solvent vapor annealing does not strongly alter the domain orientation and, consequently, an initially prepared favorable (face-on) orientation can be conserved more easily.

In all these kinetic studies, typically, selected intensity regions of the 2D GIWAXS data are integrated and the intensity evolution is shown as function of time. The time resolution is given by the smallest data acquisition time and thus is directly linked to the available intensity of the used X-ray source. The same holds for kinetic investigations that are performed during the film formation. Although as-cast films are very unlikely to give rise to high-performance solar cells, such studies help for a better understanding of the multiple complex pro. cesses that transform the initial polymer solution into the dry blend film. For the most frequently applied processing technologies, namely spin coating and blade coating, such in situ studies have been reported very recently [39, 155, 156]. Concerning the GIXS set up, these experiments are rather demanding. The equipment used for film preparation needs to be mounted onto the corresponding instrument at a synchrotron radiation source. Thus, the blade coater or spin coater needs to be mounted at the sample position. For example, Sanyal et al. investigated in situ the influence of the substrate temperature on the structural evolution of P3HT:PCBM films during the deposition via doctor-blading [155]. Temperatures between 10 and $8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ were examined. It turned out, that the lowest probed temperature was giving the best results. The authors reported a high degree of $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking at $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with a broader orientational distribution of the P3HT together with an improved nanomorphology in terms of smaller domain sizes [155]. In case of spin coating, the process was followed by Chou et al. [39].

Figure 7.9 shows the kinetic evolution of the GISAXS and GIWAXS signal together with the change of film thickness [39]. From the GIWAXS signal the strong (100) Bragg reflection from the edge-on oriented P3HT lamellar structure was integrated to enable a high time resolution of $1 0 \mathrm { H z }$ . Four characteristic stages are detected, as illustrated from (a) to (d) in Figure 7.9. Within the first second of the spin coating process, the strong decrease in thickness due to the flung-off of the solution, causes a strong increase in the intensity. At that stage neither P3HT nor PCBM show any signatures of crystallinity (stage (a) in Figure 7.9). Next, a texturing of the P3HT lamellae parallel to the sample surface is faintly seen (stage (b) in Figure 7.9), followed by a strong and fast (less than 1 s) P3HT crystallization and PCBM aggregation after 5.5 s (stage (c) in Figure 7.9). In this stage, the final film thickness and morphology are reached. Thus, the final seconds of the spin coating change neither the morphology nor the thickness (stage (d) in Figure 7.9). The onset of film formation and its duration show both a dependence on the mixing ratio of P3HT and PCBM. At a 1: 1 mixture the fast onset of crystallization and the longest duration of film formation were reported [39]. Thus, changes in the mixing ratio alter already the initial film formation.

Despite the complex full functional stack, which is required to have a working organic solar cell, many investigations are focused on the active layer only, or probe bilayer systems, which comprises the active layer on top of the system-relevant blocking layer (e.g., on top of a PEDOT:PSS layer). Huang et al. compared GIWAXS signals from P3HT:PCBM films on silicon with and without the blocking layer [121]. The GIWAXS patterns show differences, which cannot originate solely from PEDOT:PSS contributions. Bragg peak positions are slightly shifted and Bragg peak widths are broadened in the presence of PEDOT:PSS. Thus, the details of the crystalline P3HT structure are affected by the underlying PEDOT:PSS layer.

GIXS measurements that determine the morphology in complete devices are extremely rare today. Guo et al. used GISAXS and GIWAXS to investigate layer by layer the evolution of characteristic structures through the OPV device based on P3HT:PCBM [157]. Recently, this work was extended by Wang et al. to devices based on P3HT:PCBM with an inverted geometry [158]. Both investigations included the metal top contacts. With respect to the molecular arrangement, the analysis of the GIWAXS data is straight forward, because each crystalline species can be identified by its characteristic Bragg reflections. Thus, Bragg peak from electrode materials or from the blocking layer can be well separated from those of the active layer. Guo et al. observed that thermal load caused by the top contact deposition can influence the P3HT crystal structure, depending on the sample history, whereas the PCBM crystallites remained unchanged [157]. Such observations show that it might become necessary to extend the investigations to full functional stacks instead of focusing only on the active layer.

In addition to the large body of work related to the system P3HT:PCBM, the high-performing low-band- gap polymers have attracted considerable attention. Among the conjugated polymers probed with GIwAxS are, for example, poly[(4,4-didoecyldithieno $[ 3 , 2 - b ; 2 ^ { \prime } , 3 ^ { \prime } .$ -d]silole)-2,6-diyl-alt-(2,1,3-benzothiadia zole)-4,7-diyl] (denoted PCPDTBT) [103], [2,6-(4,4-bis(2-ethylhexyl)-4H-cyclo penta[2,1-b;3,4- $b ^ { \prime }$ |dithiophene)-alt-4,7-(2,1,3-benzothiadiazole)] (denoted PCPDTBT) [111, 159-161], poly((4,4-octyldithieno(3,2-b:20,30-d)silole)-2,6- diyl-alt-(2,1,3-benzothiadiazole)-4,7-diyl) (denoted PSBTBT) [139] and poly [[4,8-bis[(2-ethylhexyl)oxy]benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']dithiophene-2,6-diyl][3-fluoro-2- [(2-ethylhexyl)carbonyl]thieno[3,4-b]thiophenediyl]] (denoted PTB7)[132, 133, 162-165] as well as other low-band-gap polymers such as poly[4,8-bis (2-ethylhexyloxy)benzo(1,2-b:4,5- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { \mathit { b } } ^ { \prime }$ )dithiophene-alt-5,6-bis(octyloxy)-4,7-di (thiophen-2-yl)(2,1,3-benzothiadiazole)- $^ { 5 , 5 ^ { \prime } }$ -diyl], poly[(4,40-bis(2-ethylhexyl)- dithieno[3,2- $. b { : } 2 ^ { \prime } { , } 3 ^ { \prime }$ -d]silole)-2,6-diyl-alt-(2,1,3-benzothiadiazole)-4,7-diyl], and poly[2,3-bis-(3-octyloxyphenyl)-quinoxaline-5,8-diyl-alt-thiophene-2,5-diyl] as probed by Andersen et al. [95]. In particular, systems based on PTB7 have gained high interest due to the high solar cell efficiencies reached for the devices. Among them, Zhang et al. achieved an averaged efficiency of $1 0 . 5 \%$ for a ternary organic solar cell based on two donors, including one polymer (PTB7-Th), one small molecule $\left( p \mathrm { - D T S ( F B T T H _ { 2 } ) _ { 2 } } \right) _ { 2 }$ , and one acceptor $( \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } )$ [164].

Figure 7.10 shows the two-dimensional GIWAXS patterns of the active layers based on PTB7- $\mathrm { \cdot T h { : } P C _ { 7 1 } B M }$ for different weight ratios of $p \mathrm { - D T S ( F B T T H _ { 2 } ) _ { 2 } }$ as well as line cuts from these data [164]. A highly ordered face-on orientation of the polymer molecules resulted from the formation of an alloy structure, which facilitates the enhancement of charge separation and transport and the reduction of charge recombination, thereby enabling very high device efficiencies. Thus, a high crystallinity and the face-on orientation of conjugated polymers could be obtained by forming an alloy with two miscible donors [164]. Other electron accepting polymers, having weak accepting-strong accepting (WA-SA) and strong accepting-strong accepting (SA-SA) monomer alternation, were synthesized for studies of structure/property relationship in all-polymer solar cells [165]. However, the reached efficiencies were typically below the one using small molecule acceptors. In addition so-called donor-acceptor polymers such as PBDTTT (poly(4,8-bis-alkyloxybenzo[1,2-b: $4 , 5 \AA { - } b _ { \mathrm { ~ \tiny ~ . ~ } } ^ { \prime }$ |dithiophene-2,6-diyl-alt-(alkylthieno[3,4-b]thiophene-2-carboxylate)-2, 6-diyl) (denoted PBDTTT) [166] are now gaining high interest. For example Guo et al. investigated the roles of crystallinity, interchain interaction, and exciton delocalization on ultrafast charge separation pathways in donor-acceptor copoloymer blends based on PBDTTT [166].

Many observations made from these novel conjugated polymers match with the basic principles derived from the P3HT-based model systems. For example, a fullerene-dependent miscibility was reported for PSBTBT mixed with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ as well [139]. Collins et al. found low crystallinity for PSBTBT with octyl side chains in contrast to high crystallinity for dodecyl and ethylhexyl PSBTBT [139]. Differences in the quality of the polymer crystalline parts being necessary to achieve good device efficiencies are turning out to be a characteristic of the individual conjugated polymer systems. Owing to the large number of newly synthesized conjugated polymers more progress can be expected. Anyhow, the information on the crystalline regions and their orientation will remain very necessary and thus, GIWAXS measurements will be of continuing interest for the field.

# 7.5 Probing the Active Layer Morphology with GISAXS

In addition to the molecular arrangement of the materials, the nanomorphology is of importance for the organic solar cell performance. Thus, many investigations using GIWAXS simultaneously address this large length scale regime with additional GISAXS measurements. This includes demanding experiments, such as kinetic investigations [39, 156], and the structure characterization of full OPV devices [157, 158].

Focusing, again, on the P3HT-based active layers with fullerene-based electron acceptors for OPV applications, the limited miscibility of P3HT and PCBM causes phase separation as outlined above [15]. The differences in the scattering length densities of P3HT crystals, the P3HT:PCBM mixed phase, and PCBM crystals make this domain structure visible in the GISAXS experiment. In contrast to the molecular level, however, the domains are much less ordered and polydisperse in size. As a consequence, typically, no pronounced Bragg peaks from a structure factor are found, rather weak and broadly smeared intensity features are observed. Figure 7.11a shows the example of GISAXS data from a P3HT:PCBM film. Wu et al. followed in situ with GISAXS the thermal annealing during the first $6 0 s$ [116]. The initial film exhibits a broad scattering feature at about $q _ { y } \approx 0 . 2 5 \mathrm { n m ^ { - 1 } }$ which becomes more pronounced during the annealing due to the structural evolution of the PCBM clusters. The data were modeled with polydisperse spheres, using a Schultz size distribution to account for the polydispersity and a structure factor from an effective one component system of hard spheres. The resulting size distribution functions are seen in Figure 7.11b. The initially prepared film exhibits PCBM clusters with a diameter of $7 \mathrm { n m }$ . These grow to $1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ during heating toward $1 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . In the subsequent annealing at $1 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $6 0 s$ , the size of the PCBM clusters increases further to $1 7 . 5 \mathrm { n m }$ [116]. Compared with thermal annealing, solvent vapor annealing yields smaller domains and commonly alters the film morphology less seriously [110].

To account for the increase in intensity at very low $q _ { y }$ values, Wu et al. additionally used a Debye-Buche correlation function in the model fit shown in Figure 7.11a [116]. A similar increase in intensity toward low $q _ { y }$ values was, for example, reported by Huang et al. as well [121]. A GISAXS experiment with higher resolution performed by Ruderer et al. showed that, in addition to the small PCBM clusters, a larger domain type structure on the order of hundreds of nanometers exists, which causes this rise in intensity [42]. The corresponding GISAXS data are shown together with fits in Figure 7.12.

The structural feature of large domains was confirmed in an additional grazing incidence small angle scattering experiment using neutrons (so-called grazing incidence small angle neutron scattering, GISANS) [117]. In the full DWBA-based analysis using IsGISAXS modeling, it was seen that these large structures appear with only a very low probability, smaller than $0 . 0 5 \%$ Thus the nanometer-sized PCBM clusters dominate the structure [117]. The high resolution toward large in-plane structures was achieved by Ruderer et al. by using an incident angle above the critical angle of all involved materials, so that no beamstop blocked the low $q _ { y }$ region. Horizontal line cuts from the 2D GISAXS data were taken at the critical angle of PCBM to enhance the sensitivity to the PCBM structures. Data from as-spun and annealed films were compared for different solvents used during the spin coating. Depending on the solvent, the broad scattering feature at about $q _ { y } \approx 0 . 2 5 \mathrm { n m ^ { - 1 } }$ is seen. In cases when chloroform was used as the solvent, the scattering feature is present in as-cast samples and disappears during annealing, whereas for toluene, chlorobenzene, and xylene solutions it appears (or becomes more pronounced) after annealing (see Figure 7.12). Thus, PCBM cluster formation is solvent dependent [42].

In addition, Ruderer et al. combined GISAxS with X-ray reflectivity and imaging techniques to gain a depth-dependent information on the morphology [42]. For the different solvents (chloroform, toluene, chlorobenzene, and xylene) used during the spin coating, different pronounced morphologies were found. Figure 7.13 shows a sketch of these morphologies by restricting to the PCBM and P3HT phases only, because for X-rays the scattering contrast conditions are less favorable for the detection of the mixed P3HT:PCBM phase. This phase is much better seen with the neutron scattering method GISANS [117]. Compared with the reported current-voltage (Iu) curves, it is obvious that in case of chloroform the material distribution along the normal surface would match an inverted solar cell but not the normal device architecture. Thus, no solar cell could be obtained from such an active layer in a normal device architecture. For the three other solvents, similar device performance was found. This shows that all these structures suffer from structural deficits, which, however, differ, depending on the solvent used. In case of toluene, the lateral structures match the exciton diffusion length of about $1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ for P3HT best. However, PCBM is found at the bottom electrode, which short-cuts part of it. In case of xylene, the lateral structures have coarsened and still some shorts are found. For chlorobenzene, the material is best distributed along the surface normal, but lateral structures are already quite large. As a consequence, for each of the different morphologies, it would require a different strategy of modifying the structure to gain an increase in OPV performance.

Comparing these findings with other reports seems to result in conflicting observations, as, for example, chloroform can give rise to efficient OPV devices. Again, the differences in the detailed sample preparation and used materials (molecular weight, polydispersity, and end-groups of P3HT) cause such differences in the observations. The initially prepared films have a nonequilibrium morphology, which strongly depends on the exact preparation protocol. Such nonequilibrium morphology is transferred via deliberate post-production treatments into another nonequilibrium morphology. The resulting morphology will depend on the starting conditions (initial morphology) and the exact path of post-production treatment. Given the complexity in assembling a functional stack into a working organic solar cell with the many layers and handling steps, small differences are very likely to occur, giving rise to different final morphologies. This underlines the necessity to have access to GISAXS and analyze the actually prepared active layer morphology. In this context, it should be noted that the surface structure as seen with AFM does not match at all with the inner structure as probed with GISAXS for some active layers, such as P3HT:PCBM [42]. Therefore, the more easily accessible sample characterization via AFM is not helpful toward the detection of the inner film morphology of OPV films.

To what extent the additional layers of the organic solar cell influence the structure of the active layer is under debate. Many investigations have looked only at the active layer or at the active layer on top of the blocking layer. Given the high sensitivity of the nonequilibrium morphology, the presence of the selective blocking layer might have an influence on the morphology of the active layer, since spin coating on a polymeric layer such as PEDOT:PSS can be different to spin coating on glass (or other substrate materials such as silicon). However, Huang et al. compared GISAXS data from P3HT:PCBM films on silicon with and without a PEDOT:PSS blocking layer (thickness $2 0 { - } 3 0 \mathrm { n m }$ [121]. Within the probed $q _ { y }$ range from 0.01 to $0 . 2 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ , no pronounced difference in the GISAXS data was observed. Liao et al. observed no differences in the GISAXS signal between PCPDTBT:PCBM films prepared on silicon and PEDOT:PSS prepared on silicon [159]. As a consequence, the PEDOT:PSS layer does not seem to have any strong influence on the nanoscale structure of these active layers. To what extent other selective blocking layers, for example, the ones used in inverted solar cell geometries, will influence the morphology of the active layer cannot be extrapolated from the results related to the use of PEDOT:PSS.

Nevertheless, in a real solar cell, the blocking layer is placed on top of the bottom electrode, which is most likely made of ITO in a normal device architecture. The ITO layer has a rather large surface roughness, which is replicated through the blocking layer. Thus, the blocking layer also resembles this roughness at its surface. For GISAXS, this ITO roughness imposes a challenge. It gives rise to a very strong GISAXS signal, dominating the entire scattering of layers placed on top [121]. The assignment of structural features to one of the layers is no longer as straight forward as it is in GIWAXS, which focuses on material-sensitive Bragg reflections. The sensitivity enhancement offered by the Yoneda peak can only be used in the case that the individual Yoneda peaks of the involved materials are sufficiently separated along the $q _ { z }$ axis [65, 74]. Guo et al. followed the functional stack build-up with GISAXS measurements for each individual step [157]. The characteristic lateral structure found for P3HT:PCBM on top of the glass-ITO-PEDOT:PSS stack is comparable to structures seen on PEDOT:PSS films without the presence of ITO. In contrast, the further assembling of the solar cell, meaning the addition of the top contacts, has a clear influence. Structures coarsen during the evaporation of the metal contacts [157]. Most likely, this is because of the thermal load applied during the top contact deposition, which will depend strongly on the equipment used. Nevertheless, the experiments by Guo et al. propose that actual structures present in working OPV devices might be larger than the ones detected in the simple active layer films, if the top contact deposition acted like an additional heat treatment.

In addition to the investigation of static active layers or full solar cell devices, kinetic studies have emerged in the past years. For example, the morphology formation was followed in situ with GISAXS (and GIWAXS) during printing of active layers for organic solar cells by Proller et al. [167]. With a slot-die coated P3HT:PCBM films were printed. Figure 7.14 shows the recorded in situ GISAXS data. As common in time-resolved studies, a mapping presentation shows the temporal evolution of the GISAXS intensity (Figure 7.14a), whereas the fit quality is best seen in the individual line cuts (Figure 7.14b). Five stages of structure development were identified. All five stages show the same characteristics for samples prepared at different temperatures and thus are independent of the dry. ing conditions. The two observed growth rates differ from the ones previously examined in solution cast or doctor bladed films [167].

The development of order in BHJ films formed from P3HT:PCBM under the influence of two common additives, 1,8-octanedithiol and 1-chloronaphthalene, was studied with in situ GISAXS by Richter et al. [168]. During film formation the impact of introducing processing additives was followed with in situ GISAXS, revealing the importance of solvent quality in the deposition process.

In addition to the emerging in situ studies addressing the film fabrication process, a pioneering experiment GISAXS was applied in situ during the operation of a P3HT:PCBM-based OPV cell by Schaffer et al. [169]. Thus, again a fully functional stack was under investigation with GISAXS. For this experiment a solar simulator test chamber was mounted to a synchrotron radiation instrument. IU-curves and GISAXS were recorded simultaneously and based on a simple geometrical model, the observed nano-morphological changes were directly correlated with a loss of short-circuit current. These in-operando experiments provided a direct evidence of morphological degradation in a polymer solar cell and thereby complement the knowledge about current generation and aging in OPV devices [169]. More recently, this in-operando study was extended by the same group to an inverted device architecture. Wang et al. confirmed the high stability of this device architecture and demonstrated that the morphological degradation is strongly retarded, which was attributed as the main reason for device stability [170].

As for GIWAXS, GISAXS is, of course, not only found for morphology analysis in P3HT:PCBM films. Again, all types of conjugated polymers in combination with electron acceptor materials have been probed with GISAXS, as, for example, PCPDTBT [111, 159, 160, 171], PSBTBT [132], PTB7 [71, 132, 133, 162, 172-174], and other low-band-gap polymers [95]. Also, for these more advanced conducting polymer systems, kinetic GISAXS studies were reported. Liu et al. used a mini-slot-die coating system to fabricate organic solar cell devices and followed the film evolution in situ during the printing with GISAXS [175]. In the experiment, the semicrystalline low-band-gap conjugated copolymer of diketopyrrolopyrrole and quaterthiophene (DPPBT) was used as the donor material and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as the acceptor. Printing was done with parameters that are applicable to large-scale processes; however, due to the specially designed coater small quantities of the materials could be used and the in situ study became feasible [175].

Irrespective of the system under investigation, the nanoscale morphology of the active layer films shows a high degree of disorder which gives broadly distributed intensity features in the GISAXS experiments. Such characteristic features of the GISAXS patterns of films relevant for organic solar cells change in case of artificially structured active layers. Via artificial structuring, length scales in the regime accessible by GISAXS are introduced. For example, Wiedemann et al. combined nanoimprinting and lamination techniques to structure the donor-acceptor interface in the nanometer scale. Using an anodic aluminum oxide stamp, a nanostructure was imprinted into a P3HT film [176]. The used stamp had pores with $5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ diameter and $1 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ distance between neighboring pores. A PCBM layer was laminated on top and GISAXS was used to probe the interface. No evidence of air pockets or other defects at the interface were detected, which explained the improved OPV device performance due to large donor-acceptor interface area and direct percolation pathways [176].

Given the high resolution of GISAXS, larger artificial structures are detectable as well [177-182]. 1D grating structures impose a high demand on the alignment with respect to the X-ray beam, since the grating lines need to be oriented parallel to the X-ray beam in order to avoid complex, asymmetric GSIAXS patterns. This structural anisotropy is very different to the structures typically resulting from donor-acceptor domain formation. The phase separation of the donor-acceptor materials causes isotropic structures with characteristic lateral length scales being present in all lateral directions. Thus, these samples do not require any particular alignment of structural features with respect to the X-ray beam.

Moving from hard X-ray energies to soft X-ray energies in GISAXS geometry is called GI-RSoXS and has recently started to gain significant interest due to the new possibilities that arise from the changed X-ray beam energy [62]. Conceptually, the scattering experiment is performed under very similar conditions; however, the changed X-ray energy forces high vacuum conditions due to high absorption of soft X-rays in air and reduces all geometric sizes such as the sample-detector distance due to the larger wavelength. The strong advantage of GI-RSoXS is the possibility to work near absorption edges such as the carbon $K$ edge (e.g., if the X-ray energy is altered from 283 to $2 8 4 \mathrm { e V } ,$ ) and thereby strongly change the contrast conditions. For example Pfadler and coworkers found contrast between P(NDIO2OD-T2) and P3HT at $2 8 3 . 8 \mathrm { e V } ,$ whereas no contrast was seen with standard hard X-rays [130]. As a consequence, it became possible to see the inner morphology of a nanoimprinted $\mathrm { P } ( \mathrm { N D I O 2 O D - T } 2 )$ film which was overcoated with P3HT to get a nanostructured donor-acceptor junction [130].

Figure 7.15 shows the corresponding GI-RSoXS data. The bare nanoimprinted P(NDIO2OD-T2) film gives rise to very pronounced scattering features since the contrast between polymer and vacuum is very high. The nanostructured P(NDIO2OD-T2) film overcoated with PS or P3HT shows a significantly reduced contrast but still a signature from the nanostructured buried interface is found. The higher order structure factor contributions from the honeycomb lattice were suppressed depending on the actual contrast conditions. Slightly changing the X-ray energy caused a loss of contrast conditions and no more characteristic features from the structure factor were observable.

# 7.6 Summary

The large interest in organic solar cells has resulted in an increasing number of investigations that address the morphology of active layers used in these devices up to the structural characterization of full functional stacks. While AFM only gives information about the surface structure and AFM and SEM are very local probes, the advanced scattering techniques GISAXS and GIWAXS give access to a full understanding of the inner film structure. Depending on the used X-ray beam size, the illuminated sample area is in the order of the electrode areas used in organic solar cell test devices. This is extremely helpful for correlating the structure and function of these devices. Since the structures are very sensitive to the actual preparation protocol and the used materials in case of conjugated polymer based solar cells, GISAXS and GIWAXS are essential tools for structure characterization and should be routinely applied for structure determination.

If applied in combination, GISAXS and GIWAXS give access to all relevant length scales from the molecular level up to mesoscopic structures. Since GISAXS and GIWAXS are nondestructive methods, kinetic investigations can be performed, providing a deeper understanding of the structure formation, for example, during film formation and device preparation. Moreover, organic solar cells can be followed during operation, which contributes to a more complete understanding of aging and functioning.

More focus might be necessary to investigate the impact of assembling the functional stack on the device performance of a final working solar cell. Besides the impact of the bottom layer (selective blocking layer) on the structure, the top contact deposition can additionally influence the morphology. This can introduce additional discrepancies between solar cell function and structures determined from simple active layers.

Typically, GISAXS and GIWAXS experiments are performed at powerful X-ray sources, as found at modern synchrotron facilities. The high X-ray flux has the disadvantage of potential radiation damage. Typically, polymer materials and in particular conjugated polymers, are sensitive to radiation damage that emerges either directly from the X-ray radiation or indirectly due to secondary electrons created by the X-ray beam in substrate materials. Thus, the high X-ray intensity can cause modifications of the initial polymer structure. These modifications include breaking of sensitive bonds, which, depending on the type of polymer, can yield to cracking of chains and reduction of the molecular weight. Alternatively, it can also give rise to cross-linking and hence an increase in the molecular weight. Both effects seriously affect the kinetics of the system under investigation. As a consequence, a very careful analysis of the maximum exposure time before radiation damage sets is common in synchrotron based GISAXS and GWAXS experiments.

In addition, the use of synchrotron radiation for GISAXS and GIWAXS limits its use since such measuring times are commonly distributed via a proposal review beamtime allocation. With the latest generation of powerful laboratory X-ray sources, GISAXS and GIWAXS on OPV relevant structures have become feasible in laboratory experiments, as well. The low X-ray flux, as compared to the ones at synchrotron sources, however limits these measurements to the investigation of static samples. The investigation of kinetic evolutions of morphologies is only feasible at synchrotron radiation instruments. GISAXS and GIWAXS frame rates of $1 0 \mathrm { H z }$ have been realized, allowing to follow fast processing, such as spin coating, in real time. Given the ongoing developments in next generation synchrotron instrumentation, more fascinating high-speed kinetic investigations are expected to come in the research area of organic solar cells.

Moreover, synchrotron radiation allows for tuning the energy of the X-ray beam and thereby the wavelength used in the GISAXS and GIWAXS experiments. GI-RSoXS additionally opens new possibilities by making use of larger wavelengths in the soft X-ray regime. Given the nonlinearity of the dispersion corrections and, thus, of the refractive index in the X-ray regime, changes in X-ray energy can allow for tuning contrast conditions. For polymer-based samples such measurements can give contrast to systems which would not have sufficient contrast in the hard X-ray regime. Moreover, ternary systems will become accessible, as several measurements at different energies can be performed. While such experiments are well established for hard matter samples, its potential is not yet explored sufficiently in the field of organic solar cell research. In future, such type of GISAXS and GIWAXS experiments, which make use of several different X-ray wavelengths, will likely appear more frequently. Gi-RSoXS will enable deeper insights into more complex active layer morphologies, which get more and more into the focus of actual research.
