# A donor–acceptor liganded metal–organic framework showcases the hydrogen-bondenhanced sensing of N-heterocyclic explosives†

Junjie Wang, Yao Cheng, Jie Zhou $\textcircled{1}$ and Weihua Tang $\textcircled{1}$

DOI: 10.1039/d1tc03197j rsc.li/materials-c

The sensitive detection of explosives is urgently required for human security and social stability. Luminescent metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are attractive but it is challenging to develop versatile all-in-one MOF-based sensors. In this work, we report a novel 3- fold interpenetrating structure Zn(II)-based MOF (ZnMOF-1) for detecting 1,1-diamino-2,2-dinitroethylene (FOX-7)-like emerging energetic compounds. Featuring 2,7-di(pyridin-4-yl)-9H-fluoren9-one as a donor–acceptor structure ligand, our crystalline ZnMOF-1 achieves a limit of detection of FOX-7-like compounds as low as 0.14 ppm, with a highest fluorescence quenching constant of $\mathbf { 3 . 2 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } M ^ { - 1 } }$ . The detection mechanism occurring via strong host–guest interactions was found to be intensified due to the hydrogen bonds formed between the MOF and the explosives. Our study is instructive for the design of highly sensitive luminescent MOF sensors for emerging polar donor–acceptor structure explosives.

The detection of trace explosives and high energetic materials is thus of great significance in many fields, and it is closely related to the wellbeing of all mankind. Recently, new detection methods for trace explosives using optical sensing materials have been explored, where fluorescence/phosphorescence signal changes are readily employed for the rapid probing of explosives owing to their high sensitivities.3 Compared to other explosives sensors, luminescent metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) stand out due to their unique features, including permanent porosity, highly tunable pore size and apertures, host–guest interactions, and welldefined crystalline structures. $^ 4 \mathrm { ~ I n ~ } 2 0 0 9$ , Li and his coworkers pioneered the development of a highly fluorescent microporous $[ \mathrm { Z n } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { b p d c } ) _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { b p e e } ) ]$ MOF for the detection of 2,3-dimethyl-2,3- dinitrobutane (DMNB) and nitrotoluene (DNT) in the vapor phase, where the MOF features $\mathrm { Z n ( \pi ) }$ coordinating with $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { . } }$ - biphenyldicarboxylate and 1,2-bipyridylethene ligands.5 A library of luminescent MOFs was then successfully developed for the detection of traditional nitro explosives such as trinitrophenols (TNP), trinitrotoluene (TNT) and cyclotrimethylenetrinitramine (RDX).6

In the recent pursuit of new insensitive explosives with high energy densities, researchers have been fascinated with nitrogen-rich heterocyclic materials like the emerging 1,1- diamino-2,2-dinitroethylene (FOX-7) series of energetic derivatives.7–9 Such explosives are usually designed by incorporating amino functionalities into heterocyclic rings or constructing nitrogen-rich heterocyclic fused-ring structures, such as TetrazFOX,7 DDNP,8 BDBT-2,9 and HANTP.10 Featuring a typical push–pull ethylene skeleton bearing both nitro and amino functionalities, FOX-7-like energetic compounds provide an intrinsic platform for constructing both intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. They can achieve high thermal and chemical stability with well-controlled insensitivities, making them promising alternatives to the RDX explosive used today.11 In this regard, we have recently explored a new synthesis of FOX-7-like energetic derivatives, where a nitro group of FOX-7 was substituted with a nitrogen-rich heterocyclic ring (e.g. a tetrazole, triazole, or triazine ring).7 Different from traditional electron-deficient nitro-compounds, the tetrazolebased TetrazFOX and triazole-based TriazFOX feature electrondonating amino groups and electron-withdrawing substituents (both a nitro group and heterocyclic ring). Their fully $\pi$ -conjugated electronic structures endow them with overall planar frameworks, leading to advanced insensitive energetic materials.

The sensitive detection of typical nitroaromatic compounds (NACs), in most cases, is realized via an electron transfer mechanism. For new emerging FOX-7-like N-heterocyclic explosives, however, the electron transfer and energy-transferinduced fluorescent quenching mechanisms cannot achieve sufficient sensitivities. In order to differentiate from conventional NAC explosives, we need to explore new probes to achieve the trace detection of such heterocyclic explosives. The intrinsic donor–acceptor (D–A) structures existing in many heterocyclic explosives (e.g. FOX-7-like ones) induce intramolecular charge transfer from the D to A moieties, producing effective dipole moments. Such dipole–dipole interactions have been used for explosive vapor detection via an optical waveguide technique, where coplanar conjugation frameworks of high electron density attract electron-deficient nitro-explosives.12 Trace explosive detection with specially designed fluorescent MOFs featuring D–A structured ligands to introduce dipole–dipole interaction is a wise strategy to develop sensitive sensors. The careful and elaborate screening of organic ligands is thus crucial to obtain the expected MOFs.13 A second key concern for our detection of these emerging explosives is their readily formed hydrogen bonds among nitrogen-rich heterocyclic compounds. Consequently, fluorescence quenching occurs with all the abovementioned guest–host interactions between fluorescent MOFs and electron-deficient explosive molecules.

In this work, we report a fluorescent ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ based MOF (termed ZnMOF-1 for its triple interconnected metal–organic frameworks) with the elegantly designed D–A structured 7- di(pyridin-4-yl)-9H-fluoren-9-one (DPyF) as the ligand, which showcases a rigid electron-rich backbone as a good electron donating block and pyridine and carbonyl functionalities as accepting units. In this case, the luminescent MOF can readily generate multiple host–guest interactions including hydrogenbonding, dipole–dipole and $\pi - \pi$ interactions to achieve the sensitive detection of N-heterocyclic explosives, especially FOX-7-like compounds (Scheme 1).

# Results and discussion

# Synthesis and structural characterization

ZnMOF-1 was synthesized via a one-step solvothermal condensation14 with DPyF as the key starting material (Fig. S1–S3, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ). Typically, DPyF and $\mathrm { Z n } \big ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } \big ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ were mixed with biphenyl- $^ { \cdot 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -dicarboxylic acid $\left( \mathbf { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { C } \right)$ with a molar ratio of 2 : 2 : 1 in DMF. After sonication, the fully dissolved mixture was sealed and left to react at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \mathrm { ~ 4 8 ~ } \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ , affording the resultant MOF, i.e., $\mathrm { Z n } _ { 2 } \big ( \mathrm { B P D C } \big ) _ { 2 } \mathrm { D P y F } _ { \mathrm { 3 } }$ , (abbreviated as ZnMOF-1) in over $78 \%$ yield (Fig. 1a). The as-obtained ZnMOF-1 was then activated by a solvent exchange process before being dried for further structural characterization. The MOF forms as nice crystals and appears yellow in daylight while bright green upon exposure to $3 6 5 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ UV light (Fig. S4, ESI†). The crystalline bulk can also be observed in SEM and HRTEM images (Fig. S5 and S6, ESI†). The FT-IR spectra of both ZnMOF-1 and DPyF show the characteristic vibration of the carbonyl $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { C } = \mathbf { O } )$ group at $1 7 2 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , indicating its freestanding conformation and no coordination with ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ in ZnMOF-1 (Fig. S7, ESI†). The MOF also shows excellent thermal stability, indicated by its high decomposition temperature of

$3 4 1 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (corresponding to $5 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ weight loss) according to TGA (Fig. S8, ESI†).

The crystal structure of ZnMOF-1 was further characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) techniques. Single-crystal XRD data revealed that ZnMOF-1 is formed on the basis of $\mathbf { Z } \mathbf { n } _ { 2 }$ $( - \mathbf { C O O } ) _ { 4 } ( - \mathbf { P y } ) _ { 2 }$ pillared paddlewheels as the secondary building units (SBUs), which have six coordinates linked through $\mathbf { B P D C } ^ { 2 - }$ and DPyF (Table S1, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). In the SBU, two $\mathrm { Z n ( \pi ) }$ ions are found to be ligated with four O atoms from carboxylate groups and one pyridine N atom to adopt distorted tetrahedral geometries. Each SBU further links four adjacent SBUs through $\mathbf { B P D C } ^ { 2 - }$ ligands to construct 2D layer structures, which are staked by a pyridine-based DPyF linker to generate 3D frameworks (Fig. 1a and b). The 2D layer structures stack along the out-of-plane direction in a three-fold interdigitated fashion, resulting in three interpenetrating 3D frameworks (Fig. 1c and Table S1, ESI†).15 The short distances between the mutually interpenetrated scaffolds of ZnMOF-1 allow for the formation of hydrogen bonds between the oxygen atom of the carbonyl group and hydrogen atom of pyridine from another frame $[ d ( \mathrm { C - H } \cdot \cdot \cdot \mathbf { O } ) = 2 . 5 0 \mathrm { ~ \AA } ^ { - }$ ] (Fig. S9 and Table S2, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ).16 Obviously, due to the interpenetration of the frameworks, ZnMOF-1 has a complex 3D network structure and abundant pores (Fig. S10, $\mathrm { E S I \dag } ]$ ). As shown in Fig. 1d, the structure contains 1D channels running along both the $^ { b }$ and $c$ axes. The channel has dimensions of $\sim 5 . 3 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } \times 1 5 . 1 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ along its cross section. The powder XRD patterns match well with the simulated single-crystal XRD one, confirming the purity of ZnMOF-1 (Fig. S11, ESI†). The permanent porosity of ZnMOF-1 was confirmed using PLATON analysis, which revealed its estimated total solvent-accessible volume to be $4 1 . 2 \%$ . Our MOF was found to have an experimental pore volume of $0 . 4 1 { \mathrm { ~ c m } } ^ { 3 } { \mathrm { ~ g } } ^ { - 1 }$ , quite close to the calculated value $\left( 0 . 3 9 \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 3 } \mathrm { \ g } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ . More excitingly, a Brunauer– Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of $6 7 0 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ was calculated through ${ \bf N } _ { 2 }$ absorption/desorption measurement at $7 7 \ \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ (Fig. S12, $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ).

ZnMOF-1 is constructed by $\mathrm { d } ^ { 1 0 }$ metal centers $( \mathsf { Z n } ^ { 2 + } )$ and $\pi \cdot$ conjugated aromatic ligands (DPyF), and its excellent fluorescent characteristics make it promising for the detection of nitro-containing energetic materials. We explored its photoluminescence (PL) behaviors in common organic solvents, which included acetonitrile (MeCN), toluene (MB), chlorobenzene (CB), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), dichloromethane (DCM), tetrahydrofuran (THF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), methanol (MeOH) and water. The emission peak intensity of ZnMOF-1 varies dramatically with the solvents used (Fig. 2a and Fig. S13, ESI†). Among them, the MOF dispersion in MeCN was found to afford the strongest PL intensity, hence we used MeCN as the dispersant for all following detection experiments. Surprisingly, ZnMOF-1 emitted weakly in proton solvents like $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ and methanol, mainly due to their suppression effect on intramolecular hydrogen bonds among MOFs that reduced the emission. The excitation and emission spectra of ZnMOF-1 and the ligand DPyF in MeCN solutions were recorded at ambient temperature. As depicted in Fig. 2b and Fig. S14 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , ZnMOF-1 and DPyF have the best emission at around $5 1 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and $5 3 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ upon excitation at $3 2 8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and $3 6 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively. Obviously, ZnMOF-1 exhibits a blue shift of $\sim 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the maximum emission peak compared to DPyF, which may be attributed to the perturbation caused by the complexation of DPyF with ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 ^ { + } }$ . 17 Meanwhile, compared to DPyF, the apparently enhanced emission intensity of ZnMOF-1 may be explained by the formation of 3D rigid backbone frameworks and hydrogen bonds among them, which would limit ligand motion and thus enhance emission.13,18

# MOF detection of explosives

The excellent luminescent property of ZnMOF-1 motivated us to explore its ability to detect nitro energetic materials.4c,19,20 The explosive detection capability of ZnMOF-1 was evaluated using two series of nitro-compounds including typical NAC explosives and FOX-7-like molecules, such as nitrobenzene (NB), nitrotoluene (NT), 3-NP (3-nitrophenol), 4-nitrophenol (4-NP), 1,4-dinitrobenzene (1,4-DNB), 2-nitro-2-(1H-1,2,4-triazol-5-yl)- 1,1-ethenediamine, (TriazFOX), and 2-Nitro-2-( $2 H \cdot$ -tetrazol-5- yl)-1,1-ethenediamine (TetrazFOX); their molecular structures and sizes are provided in Fig. S15 and Table S3 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ . Obviously, all explosive molecules can easily enter the channel and interact with ZnMOF-1. First of all, the detection of NAC explosives was studied, where the emission peak intensity of ZnMOF-1 decreased with the increasing addition of explosives $\mathbf { \hat { 5 } m M }$ , $1 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ each) (Fig. S16 and S17, ESI†). Among them, 4-NP and 3-NP have a much stronger quenching effect than the other nitrobenzene explosives, such as NT, NB and 1,4-DNB. Specifically, the emission of ZnMOF-1 was quenched by over $7 8 . 2 \%$ after adding $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of 4-NP (corresponding to $0 . 2 3 8 \mathrm { \ m M } )$ , which is twice as efficient compared to the other explosives (Fig. 2d). The fluorescence quenching of ZnMOF-1 by NAC explosives follows the order: $4 \mathrm { - N P ~ > ~ 3 \mathrm { - N P } ~ > ~ 1 , 4 \mathrm { - D N B } ~ > ~ }$ $\mathbf { N T } > \mathbf { N B }$ . The result illustrates that ZnMOF-1 can detect NAC explosives, but the detection sensitivity is dramatically improved for nitrophenols in comparison to other analogues without hydroxy functionality, mainly due to the lack of hydrogen bonding between the explosives and ZnMOF-1.

The hydrogen-bonding-enhanced sensitivity and selectivity of ZnMOF-1 was further explored in the detection of FOX-7-like energetic materials.7 These molecules feature multiple functionalities (e.g. $\mathrm { - N H } _ { 2 }$ , –NH) that can construct hydrogen bonds. As shown in Fig. 2d and 3, the emission peak intensity of ZnMOF-1 decreased by $5 4 . 3 \%$ after adding $2 0 \mu \mathrm { L } \left( 0 . 0 4 9 \ : \mathrm { m M } \right)$ of the TriazFOX solution, and surprisingly it decreased by over $9 0 . 6 \%$ upon the addition of $8 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ $\mathbf { 0 . 1 9 2 \ m M } )$ . Similarly, the fluorescence of ZnMOF-1 was quenched by $8 9 . 5 \%$ with the addition of $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ $\mathbf { \left( 0 . 2 3 8 \ m M \right) }$ of the TetrazFOX solution. The detection sensitivity of ZnMOF-1 for FOX-7-like compounds is much better than those for NAC explosives. In order to evaluate the detection sensitivity of the explosives, the quenching efficiency was quantitatively calculated using the Stern– Volmer (SV) equation:1c

$$
I _ { 0 } / I - 1 = K _ { \mathrm { S } } [ A ]
$$

where $I _ { 0 }$ is the initial fluorescence intensity, $I$ is the fluorescence intensity after adding the explosive solution, $[ A ]$ is the molar concentration of the explosive, and $K _ { S }$ is the quenching constant $( \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } )$ . As shown in Fig. S18 and S19 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ and Table 1, the quenching efficiency of the explosive in MeCN by ZnMOF-1 was found to follow the following order: TriazFOX $>$ TetrazFOX $> 4 \mathrm { - } \mathrm { \mathrm { N P } } > 3 \mathrm { - } \mathrm { \mathrm { N P } } > 4 \mathrm { - } \mathrm { \mathrm { N P } } > 1 , 4 \mathrm { - } \mathrm { \mathrm { D N B } } > \mathrm { \mathrm { N T } } > \mathrm { \mathrm { N F } }$ . ZnMOF-1 exhibits the best sensitivity for TriazFOX, indicated by its highest $K _ { S }$ of $3 . 2 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ . The quenching constant of TetrazFOX was calculated to be $2 . 0 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ , slightly lower than that for TriazFOX. Due to the hydrogen-bonding intensified detection of nitrophenols by ZnMOF-1, the $K _ { S }$ values are relatively lower than those for the FOX-7-like compounds, such as $1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ for 4-NP and $2 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 3 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ for 3-NP. In contrast, the non-selective detection of nitrobenzenes by the MOF was confirmed by their lowest $K _ { S }$ values $\sim 1 0 ^ { 3 } \ \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 }$ . The limit of detection (LOD) for each explosive was calculated using the equation $\mathrm { L O D } = 3 { S _ { \mathrm { b } } } / { K _ { \mathrm { S } } } , ^ { 2 1 }$ as detailed in the $\mathrm { E S I } , \dag$ and the results are summarized in Table 1. The LOD for TriazFOX and TetrazFOX was found to be as low as 0.14 and $0 . 3 2 \mathrm { \ p p m }$ , respectively. Compared to the NAC explosives, the FOX-7-like compounds exhibit higher $K _ { S }$ values $\left( > 2 0 0 0 0 \right)$ and lower LOD values $\left( < 0 . 3 2 \mathrm { p p m } \right)$ . These results demonstrate that the newlydeveloped ZnMOF-1 is a promising sensor for the detection of new explosives like TriazFOX and TetrazFOX.

# Detection mechanism study

In order to better understand the origin of the selective detection, the sensing mechanisms were revealed for ZnMOF-1.22 By observing the Stern–Volmer plots above (Fig. 2c and Fig. S18, $\mathrm { E S I \dag } ]$ ), we found that in the presence of the Fox-7-like compounds, the Stern–Volmer plots obviously curved upwards instead of behaving in a linear manner, which indicated that the quenching process is induced by multiple mechanisms.23 To date, electron transfer between sensors and analytes accounts for most sensing mechanisms in explosive detection.24 Generally, for electron-deficient nitro-explosives, the electrons from the electron-rich framework transfer to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the electrondeficient nitro-compounds. The lower the LUMO energy of the nitro-compounds, the more easily the electrons are transferred to the electron acceptors. As shown in Fig. 4a, the LUMO and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of all nitroexplosives were calculated using density functional theory (DFT). The LUMO levels $_ { ( - 1 . 5 7 }$ to $- 3 . 5 0 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ are arranged in descending order from the FOX-7-like to NACs, which shows how easily electrons can be transferred to these analytes from ZnMOF-1 during fluorescence quenching. Among them, TriazFOX exhibits the highest LUMO energy level while 1,4-DNB has the deepest one due to the existence of two nitro groups. If electron transfer is the dominant sensing mechanism for ZnMOF-1, TriazFOX would show the lowest sensitivity while the highest would be observed for 1,4-DNB. But the experimental results are in sharp contrast, indicating that the fluorescence quenching of ZnMOF-1 is not dominated by an electron transfer mechanism. The same behavior can be observed for NACS, where nitrophenols with higher LUMO levels exhibit better sensitivity than the nitrobenzenes. The reason for this may be that the electron transfer is a short-range process driven by $\pi - \pi$ stacking in D–A systems. For the FOX-7-like and nitrophenol explosives, their $\mathbf { D - A }$ structures are rich in functionalities that can form hydrogen bonds with D–A structured ZnMOF-1, resulting in strong host–guest interactions between the MOF and explosives (Scheme 1). The hydrogen bonds dramatically weaken the $\pi - \pi$ stacking within the MOFs, resulting in the detection of the explosive not being dominated by electron transfer.

To rule out the possibility of an energy transfer mechanism25 for the sensing of explosives by ZnMOF-1, we conducted a correlation study on their optical properties. Generally, resonance energy transfer occurs from the fluorophore to the non-emissive analyte if we can observe the effective overlapping between the analyte’s absorption band and the fluorophore’s emission one.24 However, such overlaying can be observed between the UV-vis absorption spectra of the explosives and the emission spectrum of ZnMOF-1 (Fig. 4b), which indicates that energy transfer also cannot account for our ZnMOF’s detection of the explosives.

An insightful investigation was conducted on the intramolecular interactions between the FOX-7-like explosives and ZnMOF-1. As is well known, the TriazFOX and TetrazFOX series of FOX-7-like compounds have both electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups, while ZnMOF-1 possesses the D–A structured DPyF ligand. Since both electron transfer and energy transfer mechanisms are not decisive for the detection of ZnMOF-1, its fluorescence quenching should relate to the intramolecular interactions between the explosives and MOF ligands. We first calculated the electrostatic potentials and the dipole moment of DPyF and the different explosives using the DFT method (Fig. 4c and Table 1). DPyF has a dipole moment of 4.125 Debye. And TriazFOX exhibits the highest dipole moment of 6.598 Debye among the explosives due to its high electron cloud density with amino functional groups. The order of the dipole moments of the seven explosives is as follows: TetrazFOX $>$ TriazFOX $> \mathrm { 3 ^ { - } N P } > \mathrm { 4 ^ { - } N P } > \mathrm { N B } > \mathrm { N T } >$ 1,4-DNB. Clearly, the FOX-7-like compounds and nitrophenols form stronger dipole–dipole interactions with ZnMOF-1. More importantly, the hydrogen-donating amino groups from the FOX-7-like molecules and hydroxyl groups from the nitrophenols can form hydrogen bonding with carbonyl groups or oxygen atoms in the metal clusters to strengthen the host– guest interactions. In our case, ZnMOF-1 can form the strongest host–guest interactions with the FOX-7-like compounds while it forms the weakest interaction with 1,4-DNB (0.0002 Debye dipole moment).

In order to better understand the interactions, we selected TriazFOX and 1,4-DNB to calculate the host–guest interaction of explosives with ZnMOF-1 using DFT calculation with the GGA-PBE method and double-numeric polarized basis set. Several initial binding modes were supposed, and the three most stable models were found to be constructed from the mutual effects of $\pi - \pi$ interaction, hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interaction (Fig. 5a and b, Fig. S20 and S21, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ). As shown clearly, a binding energy of $- 0 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ would be generated when only $\pi - \pi$ interaction occurs between TriazFOX and the fluorenone core in ZnMOF-1 (Fig. S20a, $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ). In comparison, a lower binding energy of $- 0 . 5 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ is obtained when only $\pi - \pi$ interaction occurs at the pyridine end of ZnMOF1 (Fig. S20b, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). In contrast, if only hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interactions occur (Fig. S20c, $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ), a binding energy of $- 0 . 3 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ is afforded and this leads to a distance of 2.007 Å between the H atom (from $\mathrm { - N H } _ { 2 }$ in TriazFOX) and O atom (from carbonyl group in $\mathbf { B P D C } ^ { 2 - }$ ligand). Furthermore, the optimized model (as shown in Fig. 5a) exhibits an increased binding energy of $- 0 . 7 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and slightly enlarged distance of $2 . 0 7 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ between the H atom and the O atom, a length within the range of classic hydrogen bonds. This result indicates that hydrogen bonds form between ZnMOF-1 and TriazFOX. The charge density mapping further demonstrates that the dipole– dipole interactions between $\mathrm { - N H } _ { 2 }$ and the metal cluster lead to polarization of both TriazFOX and ZnMOF-1 (Fig. 5b and Fig. S21, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). The improved binding energy may be caused by strong host–guest interactions including hydrogen bonds and dipole–dipole interactions, which weaken the $\pi - \pi$ stacking of the MOFs and provide additional driving forces for molecular recognition.

In a comparison study, similar theoretical modeling indicated that 1,4-DNB interacted with ZnMOF-1 only through $\pi - \pi$ interaction around the fluorenone core, and dipole–dipole interactions and hydrogen bonding were absent, leading to a lower binding energy $\left( - 0 . 7 0 ~ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ (Fig. 5c and d). The optimized interaction results demonstrate that dipole–dipole interaction and hydrogen bonds can provide much stronger interactions between the MOF framework and energetic compounds, dramatically improving the selectivity and sensitivity of the sensors for trace detection. It is evident that the best detection is for FOX-7-like compounds, due to the intensified detection mechanism via strong host–guest interactions, followed by nitrophenols, while the worst is for nitrobenzenes. In addition, the relatively better fluorescence detection of 4-NP than 3-NP may be attributed to steric hindrance.

# Hydrogen-bond-intensified detection

In order to further verify the effect of hydrogen bonding on the fluorescence quenching, we added a small amount of water to the ZnMOF-1 suspension and found a significant drop in the MOF’s fluorescence intensity (Fig. S22, ESI†). The suppression of hydrogen bonds by water can also be confirmed by the almost completely disappeared emission from the ZnMOF-1 suspension with water as the dispersing solvent (Fig. S13, ESI†). The addition of water prevents the hydrogen bonds from forming in the 3-fold interpenetrating 3D MOF frames and increases their non-radiative energy loss, resulting in a dramatically decreased fluorescence. Owing to the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, the FOX-7-like compounds and nitrophenols interact with carbonyl groups (from $\mathbf { B P D C } ^ { 2 - }$ ligand), which break the hydrogen bonds between the interpenetrating MOF skeletons with $2 . 5 0 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ distances and form stronger hydrogen bonds to reduce the fluorescence emission. As a result, the $K _ { S }$ values for detecting FOX-7-like compounds and nitrophenols increase with an increased concentration of explosives and the non-radiative loss (Fig. S18, ESI†). In a word, the construction of strong host–guest interactions between the MOF and analytes via highly polar D–A structures and hydrogen bonds accounts for the selective detection by our MOF of the emerging FOX-7-like energetic compounds.26

# Conclusions

In summary, a new luminescent ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + } .$ -based MOF featuring the D–A structured ligand of 2,7-di(pyridin-4-yl)-9H-fluoren-9-one has been successfully synthesized for the selective detection of emerging FOX-7-like compounds. Our crystalline MOF exhibits a three-fold interpenetrating structure to generate $\sim 5 . 3 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } \times$ 15.1 Å channels. Strong host–guest interactions, including hydrogen bonds and dipole–dipole interactions, account for the detection mechanism of ZnMOF-1 toward FOX-7-like energetic compounds and nitrophenols. The highest quenching constant is $3 . 2 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ , while the limit of detection can reach $0 . 1 4 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ in the sensitive detection of FOX-7-like explosives using our newly developed MOF. Our research provides promising guidelines for the structural design of highly efficient MOF sensors for the detection of explosives with hydrogen-bond-intensified host–guest interactions.

# Synthesis and activation of the MOF (ZnMOF-1)

$\mathrm { Z n } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ (59.498 mg, 0.2 mmol), biphenyl- $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ - dicarboxylic acid (48.446 mg, 0.2 mmol) and DPyF ( $3 3 . 4 3 8 ~ \mathrm { m g } , 0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } )$ were dissolved in $1 0 \ \mathrm { m L }$ of DMF. The mixture was sonicated for half an hour until the reactants were completely dissolved. After transferring to a sealed vial, the reaction mixture was allowed to react at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ before cooling to room temperature. The solid product was filtered and washed with DMF three times to obtain the product as a yellow solid ( $1 1 0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ m g } , 7 8 . 1 \%$ .

Activation of ZnMOF-1 was conducted according to a literature reported method.27 Firstly, the product $\mathrm { ( 5 0 m g ) }$ was immersed in $2 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m L }$ of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ three times for 1 hour (20 minutes each time). Then, the MOF was immersed in $2 0 \ \mathrm { \ m L }$ of petroleum ether for 1 hour, and the solvent was changed every 20 minutes. After the solvent exchange was completed, the petroleum ether was decanted and separated from the material, which was then vacuum dried for 12 hours.

# Fluorescence sensing method

The ZnMOF-1 suspension was prepared by adding $4 \mathrm { ~ m g ~ }$ of finely ground active MOF to $4 \mathrm { m L }$ of acetonitrile and sonicating for 30 min. 2 mL of fresh sonicated MOF suspension was added to a quartz cuvette. The cuvette was then excited at $3 2 8 \mathrm { n m }$ , and the emission was measured between $3 4 5 { - } 6 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The initial fluorescence emission $\left( I _ { 0 } \right)$ of the suspension was measured first, repeated three times, and the average was taken to obtain the baseline reading of the initial fluorescence emission of the MOF suspension. $2 \textrm { m L }$ of the suspension was titrated with $5 \mathrm { \ m M }$ explosive solutions (TriazFOX, TetrazFOX, 4-NP, NB, 1,4- DNB, NT, etc.), and $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of a $5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ solution of the specific analyte was added to the suspension in increments until $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the explosive was added, and the fluorescence emission was measured $( I )$ . After adding $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ aliquots of the analyte, three sets of data were measured and the average value was taken. The $I$ value was averaged and then used to calculate $\left( I _ { 0 } / I - 1 \right)$ the quenching percentage (QP).

# Theoretical calculations

All theoretical simulations and calculations were performed by Multiwfn, Guassview 5.0.9 and material studios.29 Molecular structures, sizes, HOMO–LUMO energies, and surface electrostatic potentials of each molecule were calculated using DFT with the B3LYP/6-31G (d,p) basis set. The interaction between ZnMOF-1 and the explosives was calculated using the DMol3 package of material studios. A double-numeric polarized basis set and DFT semi-core pseudopots for the core-treatment were selected. The general gradient approximation with the Perdew– Burke–Ernzerhof function (GGA-PBE) was applied for the electronic structure. The conductor-like screening model (COSMO) was used to simulate a $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C N }$ solvent environment. Intermolecular weak interactions were semi-empirically corrected by the Tkatchenko-Scheffler scheme. The convergence tolerances of energy, maximum force and maximum displacement for structural optimization were $1 . 0 \times { { 1 0 } ^ { - 5 } }$ Ha, $0 . 0 0 2 \mathrm { \ H a \ } \mathring { \mathbf { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ and 0.005 Å, respectively. The self-consistent field (SCF) density convergence tolerance was $1 \times { 1 0 } ^ { - 6 }$ . The binding energy $( E _ { \mathrm { b i n d } } )$ of a molecule (A) over the MOF material was defined as:

$$
E _ { \mathrm { b i n d } } = E \big ( \boldsymbol { A } + \mathbf { M O F } \big ) - E \big ( \mathbf { M O F } \big ) - E \big ( \boldsymbol { A } \big )
$$

where $E ( A \ + \ \mathbf { M O F } )$ , $E ( \mathrm { { \bf M O F } ) }$ and $E ( A )$ were the energy of A binding on the MOF, the energy of the clean MOF, and the energy of $A$ , respectively.
