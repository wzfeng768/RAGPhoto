# Four-Terminal Tandem Based on a PM6:L8-BO Transparent Solar Cell and a 7 nm Ag Layer Intermediate Electrode

Francisco Bernal-Texca\* and Jordi Martorell

To overcome transmission and thermalization losses from single-junction solar cells, the two-terminal tandem cell configuration is one of the most widely followed approaches. Unfortunately, the need to match the current from both cells, in addition to the fabrication of an interlayer with excellent optical and electronic performances, makes such two-terminal configurations a challenging approach. Herein, the fabrication of a four-terminal tandem incorporating a front transparent cell made using a $7 n m A g$ back electrode and a PM6:L8-BO blend which exhibits a bandgap close to the optimal according to the detailed balance theory is reported. For the opaque back cell in such tandem, a PTB7-Th:O6T-4F blend is used. A $1 6 . 9 4 \%$ power conversion efficiency is achieved when such two cells are configured in a four-terminal device separated by a ${ \tt W O } _ { 3 } / { \tt L i F } / { \tt W O } _ { 3 }$ multilayer providing an optimal light absorption distribution among the two cells.

To overcome the shortcomings associated with the two-terminal tandems, an alternative is the four-terminal approach that consists of piling up a transparent cell with an opaque cell, being both cells electrically disconnected.13-16 In that configuration, current matching is no longer a constraint parameter and, in principle, the bandgaps for the two cells of the tandem can be selected to optimize photon absorption and conversion of light to electrical energy without the constraint of electrical current matching.

# 1. Introduction

Tandem organic solar cells (OsCs) composed of front and rear subcells can extend the solar spectrum utilization via absorbing high-energy photons using a wide-bandgap material in the front subcell and low-energy photons using low-bandgap materials in the back subcell.11-7] In a two-terminal tandem OsC, the current density $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ is equal or lower than the minimum value of the subcells and consequently an adequate current balance among both subcells is needed.[8-10] The fabrication of such twoterminal tandem OsCs is also a demanding task due to the requirement of a suitable interconnection layer that combines

For the four-terminal tandem in the current work, we considered four different donor-polymer/nonfullerene acceptor blends whose bandgaps range from 1.26 to $1 . 7 5 \mathrm { e V }$ Chemical structures and absorption features of each material are presented in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information (SI). Provided the performance of the front cell is not dramatically affected by the back cell, for such front cell, one should select a blend with a bandgap close to the range where the power conversion efficiency (PCE) is maximum according to the Shockley-Queisser detailed balance model.17l As indicated earlier, for the four-terminal configuration, there is no need for current matching and as a consequence, the front cell, corresponding to the cell with the higher gap and larger open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ , should provide the maximum power conversion possible. In other words, the bandgap for the front cell should be close to the one that optimizes a singlejunction cell, shown in Figure 1a. Note also that, as seen in Figure 1b, in the wavelength range where the external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) of the front and back cells may overlap, such EQEs for all four blends considered in this work are all similar. Then, the optimal choice would be to select a blend, providing a bandgap as close to $1 . 4 0 \mathrm { e V }$ . Such a bandgap would maximize the PCE of the four-terminal because more light would reach the back cell than if a bandgap close to $1 . 1 0 \mathrm { e V }$ the other PCE maximum predicted by the detailed balance model, were to be chosen for the front cell. This is in agreement with numerical models based on the detailed balance that predicts a close-to- $1 . 7 0 \mathrm { e V }$ bandgap for the front cell in an optimal four-terminal configuration.i8,19] However, a key parameter to take into account in r elation to the bandgap is the voltage loss. When such FQYs are taken into account and are used to compute the PCE using a modified Shockley-Queisser model that accounts for the cell architecture as well as the absorption profile of the blend, it would lead to conclude that the optimal blend for the front cell would be PM6:ITIC-4F.20] That blend would provide a similar PCE than the PM6:L8-BO but it would allow more light to reach the back cell. However, the actual experimentally measured voltage loss does not coincide with the one predicted by the model. In fact, as shown in Figure S3 of the SI, the voltage loss for the PM6:L8- BO blend is ${ \approx } 2 1 0 \mathrm { m V }$ smaller than the one for the PM6:ITIC-4F, which correlates to the rather limited loss in PCE for the PM6:L8- BO blend shown in Figure 1a. Such reduced voltage loss for PM6:L8-BO cannot be explained by the modified Shockley-- Queisser model. It may be linked to a large charge carrier mobility associated with a large structural order.[21] It may also be plausible that such structural order leads to a fill factor (FF) larger than $7 5 \%$ for the opaque PM6:L8-BO cell, as shown in Table 1. In any event, such a reduced voltage loss leads to a PCE for the PM6:L8-BO cell which is considerably larger than the PCE obtained by the cells fabricated using the other blends tested. This makes the PM6:L8-BO the optimal choice for the front cell. Consequently, the best blend for the back cell is PTB7-Th:O6T-4F. Alternative options such as, for instance, using the PM6:L8-BO blend in the back cell would yield a smaller PCE for a four-terminal, as shown in Table S3 of the SI.

# 2. Four-Terminal Tandem Solar Cell Design and Implementation

Once the blends of the two cells in the tandem have been selected, a proper light absorption distribution among such two cells is key to achieve the highest PCE. Ideally, the back electrode for the front cell and multilayer separating both cells should exhibit a high transmission in the wavelength range, where the blend of the back cell exhibits the largest absorption. Given the $1 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ limitation for the thickness of the front cell, is beneficial to integrate a multilayer that reflects photons within the wavelength range absorbed by the blend of the front cell. Contrary to the two-terminal device, in such four-terminal devices, the optical features of the layers can be to a large extent decoupled from the electronic ones. To find the optimal optical performance for the front cell, back electrode, and photonic multilayer, we used a standard inverse problem-solving approach combined with the transfer matrix formalism.22] Such a numerical approach, considering the respective optical properties of each layer (see Figure S6 of the SI), resulted in a configuration consisting of the thin Ag layer followed by a photonic multilayer composed of three dielectric layers alternating $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ and LiF.

As shown in Figure 2a, the Ag-based electrode plus the $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { L i F } / \mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ structure exhibits a high transmission in the $7 5 0 \mathrm { - } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ range and a high reflectivity in the $5 0 0 { - } 7 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ range. In the very-low-wavelength range, the low reflectivity seen is less desirable but the effect is limited provided the number of photons in that range is relatively low. As shown in Figure 2b-d, the use of the multilayer leads to optimal EQEs when the adequate light distribution among the two cells in the four-terminal is reached by properly adjusting the thicknesses in the ${ \tt W O } _ { 3 } / { \mathrm { L i F } } /$ $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ dielectric structure. As shown in Table S3 of the SI, where the current densities with and without the ${ \sf W O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { L i F } / { \sf W O } _ { 3 }$ dielectric structure are compared, the cells with the EQEs obtained when using the multilayer exhibit enhanced current densities.

A good light transmission to power the back cell requires the fabrication of an ultrathin and robust Ag electrode on top of the front cell. Such an electrode has to exhibit an optimal balance between conductivity and optical transmission. In this work, we were able to fabricate a transparent electrode, as the last layer of the front cell, with a thickness of only 7 nm. Such an electrode is the thinnest ever reported when compared to top Ag electrodes for transparent solar cell applications, which typically range from 9 to $1 5 \mathrm { n m }$ [23-26] When growing ultrathin metal films on top of a dielectric layer like, for instance, a $\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ hole transporting layer, metals tend to form island-like clusters instead of a percolated surface.27 To avoid this, we deposited an ultrathin Au seed layer prior to the evaporation of the Ag contact. Figure 3a shows the computed and experimental transmission spectra of the top Ag $( 6 - 8 \mathrm { n m } )$ -based electrode, where light transmission above $5 0 \%$ in the $6 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ range was reached for $7 \mathrm { n m }$ of Ag. On the other hand, Figure 3b shows the sheet resistance as a function of the Ag thickness, which for the case of $7 \mathrm { n m }$ , the value is ${ \approx } 1 8 \Omega \mathrm { s q } ^ { - 1 }$ A only slightly above of the sheet resistance from conventional ITO electrodes $( 1 5 \Omega \mathrm { s q } ^ { - 1 } )$ . It is also relevant to evaluate the impact of the Ag layer thickness on the final performance of the front cell. With that purpose, we performed experiments to examine the impact of the Ag thicknesses on the resulting shunt resistance (Rsh) from a transparent single-junction cell based on the PM6:Y6 blend. To reduce the contribution from bimolecular recombination when measuring the effective Rsh, we extracted such value from $I { - } V$ measurements performed under only room. illumination conditions (see Figure S7 of the SI). As shown in Figure S8 of the SI, the thickness of the layer has some impact on the Rsh value. However, such Rsh is seen to be smaller than the one that would be obtained from an ITO-based transparent cell only when the Ag thickness falls approximately below $8 \mathrm { n m }$ This is another indication that no significant losses are expected in the overall performance when using ultrathin Ag layers as top electrodes in transparent OSCs. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the top electrode from Figure 3c-e show very good homogeneity with a root mean square roughness (Sq) of less than $1 \mathrm { n m }$ , indicating a suppressed dewetting of the surface when growing Ag layer with thicknesses ranging from 6 to ${ 8 } \mathrm { n m }$

The transparent front cell is set to harvest the high-energy photons while the filtered light enters the back cell. Such filtered light is composed mostly of low-energy photons as seen in the transmission spectra of the front cells shown in Figure S9 of the SI. To quantify the performance of the four-terminal device, the two cells were stacked one on top of each other, and the electrical measurements were performed for each cell separately. Table 2 and J-V characteristics (see Figure S10 of the SI) indicate that when the front cell changes from opaque to transparent cell, no significant losses are observed for the FF or $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ confirming the high electrical quality of such electrode. As the bandgap of the transparent front cell decreases, the amount of light reaching the back cell also decreases, causing a systematic enhancement of the FF for the back cell. Simulated and experimental EQEs in Figure 4b-d showcase the complementary photon-to-electron conversion covering a range that extends from 300 to $1 0 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ When the transparent front cell is PM6:L8-BO, the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ values obtained by integrating the experimental EQE spectra are 18.25 and $9 . 2 8 \mathrm { m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ for the front and back cells, respectively. The PCE of the champion four-terminal device was $1 6 . 9 4 \%$ exceeding the one from the best single-junction cell, for the PM6:L8-BO and PTB7-Th:O6T-4F as transparent front and filtered back cells, respectively.

# 3. Conclusion

We conclude that for organic blends exhibiting similar EQEs in the regions where their respective EQEs overlap, top-level performance four-terminal OsCs can be achieved when the transparent front cell is fabricated with a bandgap close to the one that would provide the maximum efficiency according to models based on the detailed balance theory. However, there are other important considerations such as, for instance, relative. $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ losses that may play a determining role. We achieved an optimal electrical performance combined with the largest light absorption when a $1 . 4 2 5 \mathrm { e V }$ bandgap front cell is combined with a $1 . 2 5 9 \mathrm { e V }$ bandgap back cell, using an ultrathin $( 7 \mathrm { n m } )$ Ag back electrode for the front cell followed by a photonic multilayer structure that properly distributes the light among the two cells in the tandem. A front cell with high light transparency was achieved without any significant reduction in the FF and $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } ,$ when such a cell is compared to its opaque counterpart. Finally, it is worth noting that for all three four-terminals considered, the PCE of the tandem is larger than the one from the corresponding best single junction. This stresses the soundness of the approach followed.
