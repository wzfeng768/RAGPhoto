# Growth and Manipulation of Organic Semiconductors Microcrystals by Wet Lithography

Denis Gentili, Fabiola Liscio, Luigi Mariucci, Luca Beverina, Manuela Melucci, Stefano Toffanin, Silvia Milita, and Massimiliano Cavallini\*

As fabrication and positioning of micro- and nanocrystals grow in importance in a wide range technological applications, there is an increasing requirement for the development of a shared technological platform that is able to process materials from solutions on large areas. Here, the application of lithographically controlled wetting (LCw) for the manipulation and positioning of single crystals directly on devices is reported. In the LCW, a stamp, consisting of a metallic grid, is positioned in contact with a liquid thin flm spread on a substrate; under these conditions, the capillary forces pin the solution to the stamp protrusions, thus splitting the continuous film in separated droplets or channels. As the solvent evaporates and the solution reaches the saturation, the solute precipitates onto the substrate within the menisci, giving rise to a structured thin film, in correspondence of the protrusion of the stamp. The possibility to achieve a patterning of crystals of conjugated molecules with a defined shape and with controlled size directly on device is demonstrated and pattern of crystals are investigated using polarized optical microscopy, atomic force microscopy, X-ray diffraction; they are also electrically characterized.

# 1. Introduction

In the recent years, organic semiconductors (OSs) have attained impressive electrical performance for both p-type and n-type OSs, indeed polymers and small molecules with charge carrier mobility even higher than $1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ are not more unusual and have been proposed for the fabrication of high-performance electronics devices.[1] Thanks to this improvement in devices, also the circuits have achieved the performances potentially exploitable for industrialization. Limiting the consideration to the simplest circuits, such as inverters, very high capability with gain higher than 100 are often observed in many systems.[2]

The recent improvement of these performances can be attributed to several synergic factors, both related to chemical design, e.g., the synthesis of new molecular structures that tune the energy levels3] and the molecular self-organization capabilityl4l (for example exploiting the hydrogen bond networks[] and by processing, e.g., optimizing the fabrication methods,[l functionalizing the electrodes,7] introducing new device architectures,3a] optimizing the gate dielectric,8] by micro- and nanostructuration.[2a,9] with a different impact, all that strategies have contributed to the improvement of the electrical performance of several OSs, however, despite some exceptions, up to now the main strategy to achieve the best performance is the use of single crystals or highly crystalline films.

The enhanced charge transport capability with respect to that generally observed for thin films of the same materials is recognized to be due to the drastic reduction of molecular disorder, traps, and defects that are the main causes of poor performance of OSs.

The main issue to the practical use of crystals in organic electronics concerns the device fabrication, indeed, most of the fabrication technologies in organic electronics are based on thin films[10] that provide only a limited control of the size and positioning of the crystalline domains.[6b] These limitations are detrimental to exploit the intrinsic properties and full potential of organic semiconducting crystals.

Devices based on single crystal are usually fabricated by two ways: i) by growing the electrodes around a pre-formed crystal,(2b ii) by growing the crystals directly on to a test pattern.11] Both these approaches are very efficient for material characterization at laboratory scale, however they cannot be considered for a possible up-scaling due to the low throughput and the poor control of devices reproducibility in term of geometrical parameter (i.e., length and width of the channel).

As fabrication and positioning of micro-/(nano-) crystals grow in importance in a wide range of areas from microelectronics to biotechnology through photonics, sensing and information storage technology, there is an increasing requirement for developing a shared technological platform able to process materials from solutions on large area. A few approaches to control the crystal growth on devices and at large area have been proposed in recent years, including the exploitation of Marangoni effect,[12] wetting on patterned surfaces,[13] solution growth of aligned organic single crystals,2c] ink-jet printing combined with anti-solvent precipitation.[2d]

The proposed approaches are exploitable only for a limited number of compounds with specific properties; furthermore, only ink-jet printing has the technological robustness to be upscaled (while still maintaining the limitation typical of serial methods in terms of output).

Here we are reporting on the application of lithographically controlled wetting (LCW) for manipulating and positioning single crystals directly on devices. LCw is an elegant parallel method for the fabrication of highly reproducible nanopatterns with precisely controlled dimensions, and position.[14]

LCW was applied to pattern several materials such as $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I I } } .$ based spin crossover compounds[15] and discotic liquid crystals,[16] in which the crystallinity (both order and orientation) determines materials performances and functionality,[17] while here, we propose LCW to manipulate at microscale materials able to form large crystals. The method is general and works with very different solvents (i.e., with different properties such us hydrophobicity, volatility, etc.); however, the specific experimental conditions, which depend on solvent, shrinking rate, temperature, and stamp nature, must be specifically optimized for each compound.

The process allows the control of positioning of the nuclei of crystallization within the meniscus that remain pinned under the stamp protrusions, therefore with an accuracy that depends on the size of the stamp motifs.

Here, we show that LCW allows to be made-up crystals patterns on prefabricated devices with controlled positioning on the surface. Moreover, we demonstrated working devices using a known material such as 2,7-di-tert-[1]benzothieno[3,2-b]benzothiophene taken as reference systems.l1a] Thin deposits were investigated by means of polarized optical microscopy (POM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), X-ray diffraction (XRD) in specular and off-specular geometries, and they were electrically characterized.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Fabrication

A scheme of the process, whose detailed protocol is reported in ref.[18] is shown in Figure 1.

In the LCW, a stamp (which could consist of a metallic grid) is positioned in contact with a liquid thin film spread on a substrate, in these conditions, the capillary forces pin the solution to the stamp protrusions splitting the continuous film in separated droplets or channels. As the solvent evaporates and the solution reaches the saturation, the solute precipitates onto the substrate within the menisci, giving rise to a structured thin film, in correspondence of the protrusion of the stamp. Figure 1 shows a schematic representation of the LCW process.

![](images/92cefe907bbe2f18a87e7f88c7ae014dcd7f1d7bfce4c27ce3a8c9e5aa57f8b0.jpg)  
Figure 1. Scheme of lithographically controlled wetting (LCW).

The concentration of the solution and the stamp-surface distance are crucial for the efficiency of the process. Since the process does not rely on specific interactions between the molecules and the surface, LCW is of general application in a large variety of soluble materials.

As the crystallization from a solution proceeds by a nucleation, in which the molecules of a solution start to gather into stable clusters (i.e., nuclei) and proceed with the crystal growth step, LCW allows the control of the nuclei positioning and the confinement of the crystallization in micrometric cavities.

This confinement leads to the decrease of the evaporation rate of solvents and to the decrease of the molecular diffusion converting energy for optimizing the crystallization process. Both continuous and isolated crystals can be grown on several kinds of surfaces. We applied LCW to thieno(bis)imide end-functionalized terthiophene (1),[19] which is a multifunctional material that exhibits n-type charge transport and conformational polymorphism-driven solid-state fluorescence[20] and to 2,7-di-tert-[1]benzothieno[3,2-b]benzothiophene (2), a recently introduced, high-performing p-type semiconductor.[1a] Scheme 1 shows the chemical structures of 1 and 2.

We choose compounds 1 and 2 as model compounds because they are currently subject of intense research in our and several other groups and tend to form crystalline aggregates when deposited on surfaces. However, our approach could in principal being extended to many other materials exhibiting spontaneous crystallization tendency in thin deposits as well as to materials that are able to crystallize only under confinement.[17]

# 2.2. Thin Deposits

When deposited from solution, 1 shows two types of crystal morphologies, viz. fiber-like (fiber) and platelet-like (platelet) crystals, which correspond to two different polymorphs.[20b]

The crystals size depends on the shrinking rate, therefore acting on the nature of the solvent and the experimental conditions (i.e., drop casting in air versus drop casting in saturated atmosphere of solvent vapor and nature of the solvent), size and crystal composition can be tuned in the ranges of $2 <$ size ${ < } 1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ (i.e., ${ < } 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ by drop casting in air from chloroform and ${ > } 1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ by drop casting in saturated atmosphere from toluene).

Both platelets and fibers exhibit birefringence under POM with crossed polars. In particular, the platelets show the typical behavior of optically anisotropic materials with extinction at precise orientations, i.e., when the crystals are oriented with along one direction of polarizers. Figure 2 shows an optical image and XRD spectra of thin deposit of 1 obtained by drop casting on $\mathrm { S i } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ surfaces from different solvents.

XRD measurements (Figure 2c) confirm the crystalline nature of thin deposits and the presence of two polymorphs related to the different morphologies.[20b]

The presence of the same Bragg peaks in specular and offspecular scans indicates that both fiber and platelet crystals are randomly oriented. Moreover, we observe a main peak family attributed to the d-spacing of $2 . 3 7 \mathrm { n m }$ for platelets and $2 . 1 3 \mathrm { n m }$ for fibers, indicating the different molecular packing along the molecular long axis.

As compound 1 the morphologies of 2 thin films strongly depend on the shrinking rate.

When deposited from volatile solvents $\mathrm { ( C H C l _ { 3 } ) }$ in air, 2 forms a variety of structures whose shape range from plates hexagonal structures to dendritic structures. AFM shows very flat platelet crystals. The thickest ones $( > 2 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ appear colorless differently from thinner deposits whose color depends on the crystals thickness, as occurs in the case of interference colors.

Under POM, thinner crystals $( < 3 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ show a slight birefringence, which significantly increases for thicker crystallites. No preferential direction of extinction was observed in these crystallites by rotating the polarizer.

When deposited from low volatile solvents such as 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB), 2 forms larger crystals (mean size ${ > } 1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { m } _ { i }$ which exhibit the same characteristic of thin deposits. Figure 3 shows an optical image and XRD pattern of thin deposit of 2 obtained by drop casting on Si/SiO2 surfaces from volatile solvent (i.e., chloroform) and low volatile solvents (i.e. toluene).

# 2.3. Deposition in Confinement

Figure 4 shows an optical image and the corresponding XRD pattern of compound 2 deposited by LCW from DCB solution using a feature less stamp. This condition provides only a vertical confinement of the system, without the control of spatial less stamps, stamps with very large features (typically ${ > } 1 0 0 \ \times$ $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } )$ or with thin features that do not allow the formation of pinned menisci).

Using materials that have a strong tendency to crystallize, such as 2, the vertical confinement is enough to induce crystals orientation. In this condition, 2 forms large crystals (mean size ${ > } 1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ with hexagonal shape (Figure 4). Differently from cast deposits, each crystal appears very homogeneous and extremely flat (r. m. s. roughness on top of crystals ${ < } 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , as evidenced by the line profile of the AFM image). The thicker crystals appear colorless while the crystals thinner than $3 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ appear homogeneously colored.

XRD pattern confirms the high crystallinity of the deposit adopting "RT-phase," furthermore, the only presence of (h00) reflections indicates the very high [100] texturing of the film also corroborated by the very narrow rocking curve, RC, collected around the (1o0) reflection. A schematic view of the molecular packing orientation is depicted in Figure 4e.

Spatially controlled patterning of compounds 1 and 2 has been obtained by LCW using highly volatile solvents, such as chloroform or acetone.

The use of volatile solvent allows the formation of the nuclei only when the solution is pinned under stamp motif, therefore the crystallization occurs only in controlled places. The faster solvent evaporation, which normally favors the formation of smaller crystal domains, is partially compensated by the presence of the stamp that reduces the evaporation rate, allowing the formation of larger and possibly oriented crystals with respect the use of drop casting.

The grown in confinement does not affect the crystalline degree of compounds 1, but the crystalline phase. Indeed, looking at the first Bragg peaks at small angles, the relative intensity of the two polymorphs changes and a new reflection appears at an angle corresponding to interplanar distances of $2 . 1 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (labeled with pink squares in Figure 5d,e). This behavior is expected to be due to different shirking rate that occurs because the presence of the stamp, as observed in different study in which the percentage of polymorphs in cast films was controlled by the shrinking rate. A detailed study of patterning in polymorphs is under investigation and will be subject of a future study. LCW allows the formation of crystalline domains in a controlled position; moreover POM shows an increase of more than two times in the size of crystallites. The solvent was evaporated at room temperature in air.

# 2.4. Devices

In order to prove the efficiency of the method, we demonstrated a working field effect transistor by manipulation of crystals of 2. In this case, we patterned continuous structures in order to efficiently connect the electrode of test pattern, the use of these configurations allows us to eliminate the use of alignment step that is necessary in case of patterning of separated structures.

Patterned 2 crystals were used as active layer of organic field-effect transistors (OFETs). OFETs with patterned 2 crystals were fabricated in a bottom-gate, bottom contact architecture; crystals were directly grown on interdigitated gold source and drain microelectrodes, prefabricated on bare or octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS)-treated $\mathrm { S i } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ substrates, using a featureless stamps or a metallic grid (see Experimental Section for details). Figure 6 shows an optical microscopy image of the devices fabricated on interdigitated gold electrodes using a metallic grid.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we proved that an unconventional wet lithography such as LCW can be applied directly to a soluble material to yield a patterned of crystals. The procedure is simple, versatile, and reproducible, and allows one to control the positioning and in some cases the orientation of crystals. We demonstrated that in this manner, it is possible to achieve a patterning of crystals of conjugated molecules with a defined shape and with controlled size directly on device. We prove the efficiency of the process by fabricating an OFET, which exhibit performances comparable with literature.

Here, we explicitly discuss the application of our process to target derivatives 1 and 2-molecules possessing very different electronic structures-however the process can be extended to other soluble molecular materials and polymers to finely control their crystal positioning directly on the device substrate thus to ultimately enhance their performances and or range of applications.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: Thieno(bis)imide end-functionalized terthiophene (1) and 2,7-di-tert-[1]benzothieno[3,2-b]benzothiophene (2) were synthesized according to the reported procedure in refs.[19] and,[1a] respectively.

Spectroscopic-grade quality solvents were purchased from Sigma-- Aldrich and square nickel grid 500 mesh from SPI supplies. Sylgard 184 (polydimethylsiloxane) silicone elastomer base and curing agent were purchased from Dow Corning.

Featureless Stamp Preparation: The elastomeric polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps were prepared by replica molding[9c of a silicon wafer. After curing for $^ { 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ PDMS stamps were peeled off, cleaned by sonication in ethanol for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ , and dry in N2.

Substrate Cleaning and Preparation: Silicon wafers $( 7 \times 1 \ c m ^ { 2 } )$ with native or thermally grown silicon dioxide layer $2 0 0 \mathsf { n m }$ thick) are used as substrates. All substrates were cleaned by sonication in acetone for $\mathsf { l o } \mathsf { m i n }$ then in 2-propanol for $\mathsf { l o } \mathsf { m i n }$ , and dry in a stream of nitrogen prior to use.

Compounds 1 and 2 were patterned on the substrates by drop casting $5 \mathrm { - } 7 0 ~ \mu \iota$ of a $2 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ chloroform (or DCB) solution on the substrate, then a $0 . 5 \times 0 . 5 ~ \mathsf { m m } ^ { 2 }$ copper (or nickel) square grid (SP) supplies, 500 mesh) is gently placed on the top and the cast solution (in this particular case, in which not particular alignment is necessary we used a floating grid), then is allowed to evaporate at room temperature for $3 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ . After the complete evaporation of the solvent, the grid is gently removed. A detailed protocol of the technique is reported in ref.[18]

Morphological Characterization: Optical microscopy images were recorded with a Nikon i-80 microscope equipped with epi-illuminator, and cross polars. AFM images were recorded with a commercial AFM (MultiMode 8, Bruker) operating in tapping or peak-force mode in ambient condition. Image analysis was done using the open source SPM software Gwyddion-www.gwyddion.net.

XRD Characterization: Crystal structure has been characterized by performing specular and off-specular scans and rocking curves by using a SmartLab-Rigaku diffractometer, equipped with a rotating anode - $\mathsf { C u } ~ K _ { \alpha } \lambda = 7 . 5 \bar { 4 } 7 8 0 \ \bar { \mathsf { A } } )$ followed by a parabolic mirror, to collimate the incident beam, and a series of variable slits (placed before and after the sample position). Off-specular scans were carried out by recording 20 scans with the incident angle fixed to $0 . 8 ^ { \circ }$

Device Fabrication: For bottom-gate, bottom-contact OFETs, heavily n-type doped silicon wafers with a thermally grown silicon dioxide layer $2 0 0 ~ \mathsf { n m }$ thick $( C _ { \mathrm { i } } = 1 7 . 2 5 ~ \mathsf { n F ~ c m } ^ { - 2 } ,$ are used as substrates. Gold sourcedrain interdigitated electrodes with different channel lengths $( \mathsf { L } = 2 0$ - $4 0 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ and nominal widths $( \mathsf { W } = 7 7 ~ 2 0 0 , 2 2 ~ 4 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ are pre-patterned on the substrates. Before crystal formation, the substrates were cleaned by sonication in acetone for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ , then in 2-propanol for $1 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ , and dry in a stream of nitrogen and heated at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ for $7 5 ~ \mathsf { m i n }$

OTS-treated $\mathsf { S i } / \mathsf { S i O } _ { 2 }$ substrates were prepared by a solution phase deposition. In brief, after treatment in UV-ozone for 2 min, the substrates were immersed for $1 5 \ \mathsf { m i n }$ in a $1 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { m }$ OTS toluene solution. Then, substrates were removed from the OTS solution, thoroughly rinsed with toluene, and dried under a nitrogen flow.

Compound 2 was patterned on the substrates by drop casting $5 { \mathrm { - } } 1 0 ~ \mu \ L$ of a $2 0 \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 }$ chloroform solution on the substrate, then a $0 . 5 \times 0 . 5 ~ \mathsf { m m } ^ { 2 }$ nickel square grid (SPI supplies, 500 mesh) or a featureless PDMS stamp is placed on the top and the solvent is allowed to evaporate at room temperature for $3 0 \mathsf { \ m i n }$ . Afterwards, the grid/ stamp is removed and device is transferred in a vacuum chamber for electrical characterization.

Electrical Characterization: All the electrical measurements were performed in vacuum ( $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ mbar), using a Keithley 2612 source measure units. The field effect mobility in the saturation regime $( \mu _ { \mathsf { s a t } } )$ was calculated using the equation $I _ { \mathsf { D } \mathsf { S } } = ( \mathsf { W } / 2 L ) \mathsf { C } _ { \mathsf { i } } \mu _ { \mathsf { s a t } } ( V _ { \mathsf { G } } - V _ { \mathsf { T } } ) ^ { 2 }$ where W and L are channel widths and lengths, respectively, $C _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the capacitance of the insulating ${ \mathsf { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ layer, and $V _ { \intercal }$ is the threshold voltage extracted from the square root of the drain current $( I _ { \mathsf { D } \mathsf { S } } ) ^ { 1 / 2 } )$ versus gate voltage $( V _ { \mathsf { C } } )$ characteristics for a fixed drain voltage $( V _ { \mathsf { D S } } )$ . As shown in Figure 6, the patterned 2 crystals are mainly confined in the position corresponding to the edges of the metal grid, implying that the effective device channel width is much less of its nominal value. In order to evaluate the field-effect mobility values, the effective channel widths were estimated on the basis of optical microscopy images (average value $5 \%$ of the geometrical width).
