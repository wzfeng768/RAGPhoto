# Current developments of eco-friendly organic field-effect transistors: from molecular engineering of organic semiconductors to greener device processing

Gyeong Seok Lee,†a Hyeok-jin Kwon,†b Tae Kyu An $\textcircled{1}$ \*c and Yun-Hi Kim $\textcircled{1}$ a

Replacing environmentally damaging toxic halogenated/aromatic hydrocarbon organic solvents commonly used in solution-processed organic field-effect transistors with more sustainable green solvents has in recent years become a subject of various studies. In the current review, we summarize the properties of solvents used to process organic semiconductors and relate these properties to the toxicities of the solvents. And then, the research efforts to avoid using toxic organic solvents are reviewed, in particular the efforts involving molecular engineering of organic semiconductors achieved by introducing solubilizing side chains or substituents into the backbone and with synthetic strategies to asymmetrically deform the structure of the organic semiconductors and random copolymerization, as well as efforts involving the use of miniemulsion-based nanoparticles to process organic semiconductors.

# 1. Introduction

Organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) based on solutionprocessed organic semiconductors (OSCs) are key components of next-generation electronic devices.1,2 Interest in OFETs has surged due to their several advantageous features, including their flexibility and facile large-area processability, and their significantly improved electrical performances are expected to foster the development of next-generation devices.3–6 Despite these remarkable advances in OFET performance, much research still needs to at least be considered—and preferably carried out—in order to deploy and commercialize OFETs as practical electronic devices. OFETs do present several problematic issues that need to be considered or improved, including reliability, high operating voltage, bias stress stability, long-term stability, and solvent processing.7–11 Various investigations are being conducted to improve upon these issues, with these investigations including morphological, electrical, and structural analyses of solution-processed OSC films coated by using organic solvents.12,13 Organic semiconductors are highly soluble in organic solvents such as chloroform and chlorobenzene—and hence, when dissolved in these solvents, can be made to form smooth and uniform OSC films.14 Moreover, the field-effect mobility levels of the most recently developed solutionprocessed OSCs exceed the minimal industrial requirements and effectively compete with levels of mobility from amorphous silicon-based FET devices.15

Realizing next-generation electronics with OFET devices, however, remains hampered—by concerns about the toxicity of the organic solvents used, as these solvents are hazardous to the environment and human health.16,17 But while use of toxic organic solvents increases undesirable environmental costs, the use instead of eco-friendly solvents would decrease negative human and environmental impacts at the mass-production stage.17–19 Substituting toxic organic solvents in the OFET fabrication process with eco-friendly solvents is thus a very urgent task, one that can advance the safety of industrially produced organic electronics. Therefore, the ability to process OSCs using environmentally benign non-toxic solvents such as tetrahydrofuran (THF), ethanol, or even water, can make OSCs much stronger candidates for being mass produced and then used in industrial electronics applications.20

Unfortunately, there has been not much research on environmentally benign processing involving eco-friendly solvents with OFET devices. Most OSCs are soluble in solvents such as chlorinated organic solvents that, unfortunately, are toxic—and are poorly soluble in green solvents such as ethanol and water. This issue is highly related to the Hansen solubility parameters (HSPs), considering three factors: cohesive energy densities of dispersion, polarity, and hydrogen bonding.21 The well-known principle of ‘like dissolves like’ and Hansen solubility parameter can offer a direct indication of dissolving the OSCs in certain solvents.22 According to this, common halogenated solvents (e.g. chlorinated organic solvent) show high energy densities of dispersion values and low polarity and hydrogen bonding values. Also, aromatic solvents show high energy densities of dispersion values because of $\pi - \pi$ stacking, while non-aromatic solvents tend to exhibit lower values of energy densities of dispersion. In contrast, water and alcohols possess low energy densities of dispersion values and extremely high polarity and hydrogen bonding values, which can lead to stability issues with OSCs when exposed to atmospheric humidity.23

This solubility problem results in lower performances of devices made from hydrophobic organic semiconductors processed using green solvents than of OFET devices coated from toxic organic solvents.24 Therefore, research in the field of OSCs needs to yield a breakthrough eco-friendly fabrication technology without sacrificing field-effect mobility, and some eco-friendly fabrication strategies are available. In this review, we focus on the concepts for the design and synthesis of various OSCs dissolved in environmentally benign solvents and for miniemulsion procedures based on OSCs dispersed in environmentally benign solvents for OFET applications. We also summarize the properties of solvents used to process OSCs, and relate these properties to the toxicities of the solvents.

# 2. Review of solvents used to process organic semiconductors

A solvent generally refers to a chemical substance that can dissolve a solute using intermolecular interactions without effecting any chemical modification, and most solvents used in industrial procedures are in a liquid state. In this respect, the choice of solvent used in OFET fabrication is as important as the processing technology selection (e.g. zone casting, droplet-pinned crystallization, confined evaporative capillary flow methods, and solution-shearing). First and most fundamentally, the solvent must be capable of dissolving the semiconductor(s) in order to obtain a sufficient concentration for forming a homogeneous thin film of the desired thickness and wetting the substrate surface.25 Secondly, the selection of solvent strongly determines the selfassembly properties of the $\pi$ -conjugated OSC materials and the morphological/crystal outlook or packing density of the thin films.26,27 Generally, OSC films having high film texture and large grain size display high electrical performance in OFETs.7,28

In addition, selecting an appropriate solvent in accordance with the process formula can produce better thin films for OFETs. For example, directional crystalline growth methods rely on the gradient of surface energy and/or solute concentration at the contact edge of the meniscus of the applied solution. In this method, the solvent type considerably influences the surface energy of the solution and rate of change in the solute concentration during processing. Indeed, Chen et al. described the influence of the solvent (dichloromethane, chloroform, heptane, toluene, chlorobenzene, M-xylene, mesitylene, tetralin), processing temperature, solution concentration, and processing speed in a meniscus-guided coating process using a small molecular OSC (2,7-Dioctyl[1]benzothieno[3,2-b][1]benzothiophene; $\mathrm { { C _ { 8 } } \mathrm { { - } } }$ BTBT).29 Thus, the solvent selection has a significant impact on the thin film morphology determination step. Also, the solvent should have a suitable viscosity and boiling point level for large-area fabrication,30 as solvents with extremely low boiling points $\left( T \ll 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } \right)$ are not practical for FAB environments due to safety concerns.31

Halogenated solvents possess superior physical properties, such as adequate viscosity, surface tension, and high boiling points, over those of non-halogenated solvents, thereby enabling the formation of uniform thin films. Therefore, most solvents used to process OSCs are halogen-containing or aromatic solvents, but their use is gradually decreasing due to their inherent toxicity.20,32,33 In fact, due to the serious harmful effects that halogenated solvents have on human health and the environment, many countries and organizations have introduced strict regulations on the use of these toxic solvents. A typical example is the list of Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) established by the European Union (EU) and European Chemicals Agency (ECHA). As of 2022, a total of 224 chemicals—including $N _ { : }$ , N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), $o \mathrm { \cdot }$ -toluidine, nitrobenzene, 1,4- dioxane, 1,2-dichloroethane, and trichloroethylene—are on this list.34 Chemical species classified as SVHC are administered under strict supervision and need prior approval for their production, sale, and use. And to realize large-scale production, which is the ultimate goal of the OSC industrial commercialization, green solvent processing is absolutely essential. Therefore, it is necessary to consider a non-toxic solvent that can be safely used while also not degrading the excellent properties of the OSCs.

Although there is no clear definition of a ‘‘green’’ solvent, many agree that such solvents should have minimal environmental impact, pose low health hazards, and be safe for workers throughout their entire processing. Qualitative assessments of solvent safety and environmental impact are provided by the ‘‘Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labeling of Chemicals’’ (GHS) through its ‘‘Hazard and Precautionary Statements’’. In addition, various organizations, such as the American Chemical Society Green Chemistry Institute Pharmaceutical Roundtable and IMI: CHEM21, and companies like Pfizer, offer similar information on the quantitative classification and sustainability of a wide range of solvents.35–38 Therefore, understanding the roles of solvents used in the processing can aid in interpreting published solvent selection guidelines (SSGs) derived from quantitative classification in the context of green solvent processing. Many SSGs have been reported to assist in the selection of solvents used in industrial and research applications including OSC research. Herein, we decided to define a green solvent based on these grounds.

In this review, solvents are categorized as halogen solvents, non-halogen solvents, non-aromatic solvents, and green solvents. Each solvent group was reviewed for the purpose of helping investigators select the optimal solvent for organic semiconductor processing. It is hoped that this review will enable sustainable developments to be made in the environmentally benign processing of OSCs (Table 1).

# 2.1. OFETs processed using halogenated solvents

Over the past few decades, numerous OSCs that exhibit superior performance based on high mobility have been developed.39–48 Most of these semiconductors were prepared by processing them using a solvent containing a halogen atom, especially chlorine, with chloroform and chlorobenzene being prime examples of such a solvent, as shown in Fig. 1. These semiconductors showed efficient charge transport ability and high-efficiency electron mobility due to their high solubility in the halogenated solvent and resulting smooth formation of interfaces during the fabrication procedure. Miao and co-workers reported fabulous results, specifically an electron mobility as high as $2 7 . 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ under vacuum measuring conditions, for an n-type OFET made of an OSC containing the halogenated tetraazapentacene 4ClTAP (structure shown in Fig. 2) that was processed using methylene chloride and acetone as co-solvents.47 Our group modified the side chains of diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP)-based polymer semiconductors to improve their charge mobility levels, and achieved a high hole mobility of over $1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ .40 Anthopoulos and co-authors reported an OSC consisting of a blend of the small-molecule $\mathrm { C } _ { 8 }$ -BTBT and the polymer $\mathrm { C _ { 1 6 } I D T }$ -BT (structures shown in Fig. 2) to take advantage of the high charge carrier mobility of the small molecule and superior convenience of the polymer for solution processing.49 Bao and co-workers devised a novel fabrication method called off-center spin-coating using a solution containing 1,2-dichlorobenzene $\left( o \mathrm { - D C B } \right)$ .45 When adopting $\mathrm { C _ { 8 } { - } B T B T }$ as the semiconductor material, this OFET device showed an ultra-high hole mobility of up to $4 3 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , attributed to the highly aligned, meta-stable crystal packing structure. The Liu group prepared the semiconducting polymers PDVT-8 and PDVT-10 (structures shown in Fig. 2) using DPP and highly $\pi \mathfrak { r }$ extended thiophene-vinylene-thiophene (TVT) as repeating units.43 These polymers were synthesized by carrying out traditional Stille cross-coupling polymerization with chlorobenzene as the solvent. And their OFET devices were fabricated using $o$ -DCB. The Hasegawa group introduced the concept of antisolvent crystallization to the inkjet printing procedure to utilize the high crystallinity of the small molecule $\mathrm { C } _ { 8 }$ -BTBT.48 A high-quality single crystal of $\mathrm { C } _ { 8 }$ -BTBT was realized by adopting $o$ -DCB and DMF as the dissolving solvent and antisolvent, respectively, and a high hole mobility of $1 6 . 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \bf V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \bf \Phi } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved. Ong and co-workers reported an approach involving the development of a DPP-DTT-based polymer (structure shown in Fig. 2) with a very high molecular weight, of over $1 1 0 . 0 \mathrm { \ k D a } . ^ { \mathrm { ~ \scriptsize ~ 4 ~ } }$ 4 This approach aimed to improve the charge transport by providing improved intergrain connectivity, and indeed eventually enabled a hole mobility of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \bf V } ^ { - 1 } { \mathrm { \bf \Phi } } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ . To date, only chlorinated solvents like $o { \mathrm { - D C B } }$ and 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (TCB) have been found to be effective at dissolving polymers with high molecular weights, which is unfortunate since these solvents display adverse effects. Noh and co-workers showed p-type OFETs with a hole mobility of up to $1 4 . 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and n-type OFETs with an electron mobility of $7 . 0 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ by carrying out a solution processing using TCB as the solvent (as shown in Table 2).41,50

The reported high field-effect mobility in OFETs values are comparable to or exceed those of amorphous silicon and inorganic-based thin-film transistors. However, it should be noted that some of these reported values may be overestimated. The calculation of field-effect mobility in OFETs typically assumes an ideal OFET with Ohmic contacts (i.e. contact resistance value equal to $_ { 0 } \Omega$ ). However, most OFETs prepared in reality have contact resistance values higher than $_ { 0 } \Omega$ , which is mainly induced by the energy difference between the source/ drain electrode and OSCs and known as the Schottky barrier.51 In such cases, the resistance value in OFETs can be expressed as the sum of the contact and channel resistance values. Therefore, the mobility may be underestimated if the channel resistance is more dependent than the contact resistance, or overestimated if the contact resistance is more dependent than the channel resistance.52 Thus, if contact engineering processes are not performed during device manufacturing (it means that most OFETs show the Schottky junction), the field effect mobility is overestimated by the contact resistance (the kink with Odrain current with gate voltage, and mobility values associated with it).53 It is true that these overestimated mobility values are prevalent in the OFET literature, but it is also true that such reported literature that applied halogenated solvents produced excellent morphological structures in the production of OSC thin films.

# 2.2. OFETs processed using aromatic solvents

In an attempt to develop high-performance OSCs processed using a benign solvent, various research groups have reported interesting experimental results using non-halogen solvents (Fig. 3). All of these non-halogen solvents, however, are aromatic solvents, which are also toxic. In this chapter, we review OFETs processed using these aromatic solvents not substituted with halogens. Our group reported a few strategies for developing high-performance OFET devices with solution processing using a tetralin. By deploying polymers incorporating DPP and thieno– vinylene–selenophene (TVS)/selenophene–vinylene–selenophene (SVS)/bithienothiophene (BTT)/thienothiophene (TT), and various derivatives of these compounds, all of which display high planarity and rigidity, high-performance OSC devices displaying hole mobilities of over $6 . 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were realized.54–57 Bao and co-workers introduced a method enabling a polymer to be processed in tetralin or $p$ -xylene.58 To enhance the solubility of the polymer, they incorporated a branched non-conjugated flexible linker, namely $\mathrm { C } _ { 5 } \mathrm { D E }$ , into the conjugated DPP polymer.

The resulting polymer was found to be 70-fold more soluble in 2-MeTHF (2-methyltetrahydrofuran) than was DPPTVT, which does not include this linker group.58 Also, the Bao group reported using a mixed solvent consisting of tetralin and $p$ -xylene for controlling the morphology of solution-processed polymer OFETs.59 Torrent and co-workers used the nonchlorinated solvent tetralin to deposit organic semiconducting layers for p-type OFETs.60 And they suggested that blending polystyrene (PS) with OSCs facilitated their processability in thin film formation. Also, inserting insulating polymers here was shown to enhance the crystallinity and homogeneous morphology. Yang and co-workers developed high-performance furancontaining conjugated polymers (PTDPPTT-Si, PFDPPTT-Si as shown in Fig. 4) for less toxic solution processing using nonchlorinated solvents such as toluene and xylene.61 Kim, Vaccaro and co-workers investigated the ability of binary solvent systems based on green additives to form pre-aggregated solutions and solution-processed organic semiconductor films for OFETs, with the aim of minimizing the use of toxic solvents.62 Li and coworkers synthesized novel p-type DPP-based semiconducting polymers with very high molecular weights $\left( M _ { \mathrm { n } } \ \approx \ 1 3 8 \ \mathrm { k D a } \right)$ . Notably, OFET polymers showed hole mobility levels as high as $1 2 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ when processed from toluene with a DPE solution.63,64 Naito et al. studied the effect of using aromatics solvents such as toluene, xylene and mesitylene on the spincoating fabrication of $\mathrm { C } _ { 8 }$ -BTBT films.65 Qiu et al. reported azaisoindigo-based polymers with a siloxane side chain for OFETs processable with toluene.66 Here, use of a linear hybrid side chain was shown to improve the solubility of the OSCs.66 There are also a few cases reported on using fluorinated planar conjugated polymers for flexible OFETs and treatment with aromatic solvents. Here, fluorinating the backbone improved the charge transport and charge carrier mobility of the OSC (Table 3).66,67

# 2.3. OFETs processed using non-halogenated, non-aromatic solvents

To address eco-friendly processing of OSCs for OFETs, several groups have reported results of experiments using nonhalogenated, non-aromatic solvents (Table 4) such as tetrahydrofuran (THF), 2-methyl tetrahydrofuran (2-MeTHF), and cyclopentylmethyl ether (CPME), although even these solvents are not completely free of toxicity issues (Fig. 5). THF is a colorless highly volatile liquid. The molecule is an ether $\left( \mathbf { R } { - } \mathbf { O } { - } \mathbf { R } ^ { \prime } \right)$ having a pentagonal ring structure with four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom. It has a strong chemical polarity, and hence is used as a solvent for a wide range of solutes; it also mixes well with water and with organic solvents, and hence is sometimes mixed with other solvents with low reactivity as well as used alone, to increase the overall solvent properties. It has excellent solubility compared to the solvents previously mentioned, and various reports related to processing with THF have been presented. 2-MeTHF has a polarity similar to that of THF, so it can be chosen for dissolving almost any high-molecular-weight polymer material.58,70,71 Vaccaro and co-workers developed an OFET device with a relatively low charge mobility of $0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { \ 2 } \textbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \textbf { S } ^ { - 1 }$ based on a material consisting of P3HT and processed using cyclopentylmethyl ether (CPME) as a solvent.72,73 The Cao group reported the results of manufacturing polymer light-emitting diode and OFET devices using $d$ -limonene, which is a non-chlorinated nonaromatic solvent.74 In particular, the organic semiconductor FBT-Th4(1,4) (structure shown in Fig. 6) was found to be equally or even more soluble in $d$ -limonene than in $o$ -DCB, suggesting the possibility of $d$ -limonene serving as a new and effective ecofriendly solvent. Ding and co-workers succeeded in developing an OFET showing compliant hole mobility of $2 . 4 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ as a result of processing using the universal organic solvent acetone.75 Meanwhile, the Qiu group designed molecules with high solubility in diethyl ether and in $n$ -hexane by introducing side chain substituents, and achieved mobility levels of $1 . 5 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and even higher.66

# 3. Strategies for dissolving organic semiconductors in green solvents

# 3.1. Designs of organic semiconductors amenable to environmentally friendly processing

One of the major issues for the realization of green solventbased OFET manufacturing is the poor solubility of OSCs to green solvents. The solubility level of OSCs in green solvents can be directly indicated by the well-known principle of dissolving that ‘like dissolves like’ and analyzed by the HSP.25,93,94 According to this, three partial parameters (cohesive energy densities of dispersion, polarity, and hydrogen bonding) to the interaction for the solvents and materials (in this case OSCs), and structures showing similar HSP values are likely to dissolve each other. The solubility region of a material is roughly illustrated with a sphere in the 3D space of the solubility parameter. Thus, the solvents with combined parameter values within this region enable material dissolution. Common halogenated solvents dissolve OSCs very well, but green solvents like water and alcohols exhibit extremely high polarity/hydrogen bonding factor, and low dispersion parameter, which leads to HSP values and a solubility range far away from those of OSCs. Therefore, dissolving OSCs in eco-friendly solvents is essential to achieve green solvent-based OFET manufacturing.

3.1.1. Introduction of a solubilizing side chain. Most OSC materials investigated so far have been improved through several methods of optimizing their structures for the purpose of achieving superior device performance. Specifically, these molecules are generally designed to be highly planar and to have rigid backbones that enable strong $\pi - \pi$ stacking for smooth charge transport, but the resulting strong intermolecular interactions often yield a significantly reduced solubility.95 Therefore, many groups have reported carrying out side-chain engineering to maintain or improve the charge transfer ability with high alignment regularity while also improving solubility.23,96 Linear alkyl chains of various lengths and branched ones of various lengths and shapes have been introduced into OSCs, as shown in Fig. 9. In our work, charge mobility was greatly improved without affecting the solubility by using the branch points of the side chains located away from the backbone to make strong intermolecular interactions.40 In addition, Pei and co-workers directly compared the linear and branched side chains and reported that the introduction of a branched side chain into the OSC molecule yielded thin films whose devices showed improved performance. Introducing alkyl side chains induced the formation of a stackable alignment between semiconductor molecules resulting in a threedimensional (3D) structure favorable for charge transport. Similarly, including the siloxane side chain group (as shown in Fig. 9b) was shown to increase the solubility of semiconductor materials in organic solvents and at the same time impart high crystallinity, creating a 3D domain advantageous for charge transport during the formation of semiconductor thin films.61,66,79 On the other hand, Yang and co-workers deployed a high-polarity phosphonate group as a side chain to induce an edge-on orientation and form a molecule highly soluble in 2-MeTHF, a non-chlorinated solvent, resulting in more environmentally friendly solution processing.71 Meanwhile, the Gagne group used a nature-inspired branched-chain engineering approach to incorporate galactose-containing moieties into semiconducting polymers to improve their processability in environmentally friendly solvents. The solubility of OSCs in alcohol-based solvents was improved by synthesizing novel isoindigo-based polymers with different relative amounts of galactose-containing side chains.88 Meanwhile, Reynolds and co-workers introduced a novel method allowing for aqueous solution processing without compromising the performance of the OSC devices by introducing side chains that can be cleaved at each stage of device fabrication.86 Regarding methods for increasing the solubility of OSC materials in ecofriendly solvents such as water or alcohol, there have been many examples of incorporating ionic hydrophilic groups such as sulfonate, phosphate, or ammonium into the hydrophobic portion of the semiconductor molecule, but in the construction of any one semiconductor molecule, only positively charged ionic groups or only negatively charged ones should be incorporated in order to prevent molecular aggregation, and hence an ability to control the acidity of the system is required. On the other hand, incorporating ethylene glycol as a side chain (as shown in Fig. 9d), which is non-ionic neutral but strongly polar, into an OSC molecule can be an excellent strategy to increase the solubility of the semiconductor molecule in eco-friendly polar solvents. Xiao and co-workers added a hydrophilic tri-ethylene glycol side chain into polythiophene, a representative p-type material and hydrophobic polymer, and found that the resulting product can be dissolved in water, a polar solvent.82 Kim and co-workers reported that, interestingly, the solubility of oligoethylene glycol (OEG)-containing semiconductor materials in an ethanolic solvent increased as water, a typical antisolvent, was added to the ethanol.84

3.1.2. Asymmetric structure: backbone irregularity. Another method for increasing the solubility of an organic semiconducting material is to asymmetrically deform the structure of the OSC molecule. Many polymer materials are poorly soluble in organic solvents due to the high degree of regularity and symmetry of the polymer backbones. Therefore, disrupting the high symmetry of polymer backbones has been attempted for the purpose of increasing the polymer solubility.54,63,64,90 However, it should be noted that charge carrier mobility can be adversely affected when the regularity of the polymer is broken. So, a cautious design strategy is needed in which the charge transfer capability and solubility of the polymer material are simultaneously considered. Our group reported a new design involving replacing PDPP-TVT/SVS, which shows high carrier mobility and has a symmetric structure, with PDPP-TVS, which is asymmetric, displays excellent semiconducting properties and is highly soluble in relatively low-toxicity non-chlorinated solvents.54 The TVS group binds to each thiophene end of the DPP counterpart, and at the same time, the thiophene (T) or selenophene (S) of the TVS moiety is irregularly connected to the thiophene of the DPP end, breaking the complete regularity of the polymer chain. Interestingly, the crystalline and morphological properties of the PDPP-TVS thin film were found to be not significantly different from those of the symmetrical copolymer combined with TVT or SVS. Similarly, Li and co-workers designed two asymmetric polymer materials with the DPP moiety as the acceptor unit and two different aromatic substituents, namely thiophene/thienothiophene $( \mathrm { T } / \mathrm { T T } )$ and thiophene/2-methylthiophene $\mathbf { ( T / M T ) }$ , as the donor units, as shown in Fig. 10.63 As a result, both polymers with high molecular weights of over $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { k g } \ \mathrm { m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ showed excellent solubility in toluene. Meanwhile, Bao and coworkers inserted flexible alkyl linkers each containing a tertiary carbon branch point into DPP-based copolymers to relieve aggregation and crystallinity between the polymers in solution, thereby increasing the free volume of the polymer chains. Likewise, increasing the free volume enabled the developed copolymers to have a 70-fold higher solubility in organic solvents than the fully conjugated counterpart, i.e., that without a non-conjugated flexible linker. In particular, OFETs produced using solvents less toxic than chlorinated solvents showed a passable performance, of up to $2 . 7 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , raising the expectation for future development of environmentally benign mass production of such OFETs.58 Also, Geng and co-workers prepared nine kinds of analogous chalcogenophene-containing polymers and analyzed the effects of using different types of chalcogenophenes, namely furan, thiophene, and selenophene.90 In addition, solubility tests about all prepared polymers were conducted for the solvents $o$ -DCB and $^ o$ -xylene, which are frequently used in OSC processing, and anisole, which is considered a green solvent. These tests showed that the solubility tended to increase as the structure of the polymer molecule became less regular.90

3.1.3. Introduction of substituents into the backbone. The effects of side chain groups on the solubility of OSCs were described above, but this section reviews cases in which improvement in solubility was sought by introducing substituents into the backbone. Various cases for improving solubility or maximizing the performance of semiconductor materials by introducing diverse substituents into the semiconductor molecules have been reported, but many of these semiconductor materials were produced by being processed using a chlorine-based solvent.97–101 Therefore, in describing the strategy of introducing substituents into the backbone, only cases using non-chlorinated solvents are reviewed. Ye and co-workers deployed an oxetane group as a substituent of polythiophenes (PTs), a representative class of p-type OSCs (PTHOT), to improve the solubility of the PTs in environmentally friendly solvents.77 Conventional PTbased polymers are dissolved in solvents such as chloroform, chlorobenzene, toluene, and THF. PTHOT, a polymeric material containing a unique type of substituent with a square ring composed of one oxygen atom and three carbon atoms at the end, is soluble in ethanol as a green solvent. The OFETs made by these investigators using ethanol and THF as co-solvents showed a relatively low mobility of $0 . 0 0 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ .77 However, by introducing polar substituents into the backbone, the solubility of PTHOT in alcohol solvents was significantly increased; this procedure constituted a novel method for designing polymer materials. McNeill and co-workers reported the results of using hexane as a semiconductor-processing solvent for the first time based on an effective design strategy using DPP.81 PDPPF-DFT was designed using DPP substituted with furan at the 3,6-positions as the acceptor unit (DBF) and thiophene substituted with fluorine at the 3,4-positions as the comonomer (DFT), as shown in Fig. 6. Using a small-sized highly soluble thiophene as a comonomer along with two octyldodecyl chains substituted into the DPP core made fabrication of PDPPF-DFT possible with hexane, a non-chlorinated solvent. In addition, the two fluorine atoms introduced into the DFT moiety imparted rigidity to the polymer chain through hydrogen bonding with furan linked to DPP, enabling strong $\pi -$ $\pi$ stacking, which not only favored charge transport but also made the HOMO energy level of the polymer shallow. Lowering the HOMO level of a molecule increases its oxidation stability.102 OFETs prepared using PDPPF-DFT and hexane solvent exhibited ambipolar characteristics and yielded maximum electron and hole mobilities of $0 . 2 6 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 1 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively.81

Zhu and co-workers developed FBT-Th4(1,4), a polymeric material containing benzothiadiazole (BT) and a thiophenebased monomer substituted with decyltetradecyl in thiophene to make excellently performing OFETs using neither nonchlorinated nor non-aromatic solvents.74 They also fabricated p-type OFETs from FBT-Th4(1,4) using $d$ -limonene as a solvent. Note that $d$ -limonene is classified as a green solvent because it can be extracted from natural substances such as tangerine peels. These OFETs showed electron mobility levels as high as $1 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . 74 Meanwhile, Wang and co-workers closely analyzed the changes in the properties of semiconducting materials according to the change in the terminal structure of the heteropentacene moiety.103 In particular, DTPT-PYCN (structure shown in Fig. 11), which contains pyrimidine with a cyano group substituted as the terminal group, showed excellent solubility in THF, unlike other materials that dissolve in solvents harmful to the human body such as carbon disulfide, 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene, and 1-chloronaphthalene.103 In addition, including the PYCN end group with DTPT was found to contribute to the charge mobility by lowering the crystallinity of the material. Hence, a design concept involving the inclusion of a small-molecule substituent on the terminus of an OSC was established.

3.1.4. Random copolymerization: backbone irregularity. A novel synthetic strategy for designing semiconducting polymers that are sufficiently soluble in eco-friendly benign solvents while maintaining prominent electrical properties is introduced. This strategy involves random copolymerization, in which three or more different monomers are used in a polymerization in order to disrupt the regularity of the polymer and hence increase its solubility and improve its processing. A few examples of random copolymerization are reviewed. Yang and co-workers introduced a series of copolymers with different TVT-to-selenophene composition ratios in a DPP backbone by combining a DPP unit as the acceptor and TVT with selenophene as the donor unit.70 These polymers were treated with 2-MeTHF, a non-chlorinated solvent, and showed a good mobility of $4 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Our group also reported using random copolymerization to improve the solubility.55,56 As in the above-mentioned reports, materials incorporating DPP as the backbone and thienothiophene (TT) and selenophene-vinyleneselenophene (STS) in various ratios as the donor unit were designed to preserve the high charge carrier mobility of the polymer material. These copolymers were found to show significantly enhanced solubility compared to the essentially insoluble corresponding homopolymers, and results with satisfactory mobility characteristics were achieved by processing the copolymer OSC with tetralin.56 In a similar design concept, different ratios of SVS to bithienothiophene (BTT) as donor units were tested, and the resulting copolymers were soluble in non-chlorinated solvents such as THF, toluene, xylene, and tetralin, in contrast to the insolubility of the corresponding homopolymers. Notably, a newly fabricated OSC device prepared with xylene solvent showed a high mobility of up to 6.51 cm2 V-1 s-1 .55

Hole and electron mobilities exceeding $5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ have, to date, mostly been reported for OFET devices made using toxic solvents such as chloroform, toluene, and other aromatic organic solvents.104 As previously mentioned in section 3-1,

OSCs have been made to be highly soluble in green solvents by modifying the molecular structure of the semiconductor based on the structure of an existing high-mobility reference semiconductor from semiconductor thin films processed using toxic solvents. These approaches for green solvent processing are new ways to develop OSC structures. As the molecular structure is changed, the molecular stacking structure can also change, which would affect the charge transport behavior of OFETs. To solve these problems, eco-friendly OFET fabrication methods using OSC nanoparticles derived from previously developed OSCs with good electrical performances have been investigated, and reports of the investigations are reviewed here.

OSC nanoparticles are usually made by carrying out a miniemulsion procedure.105,106 OSCs are added into a green solvent. And then the mixture of the solvent and semiconductors is sonicated until OSC nanoparticles are well dispersed and with a uniform size. A small amount of a surfactant may be added to the mixture prior to sonification. To achieve efficient charge transport when fabricating OFET devices using the miniemulsion technology, a high ordering and crystalline structures of the polymer molecules within the particles should be produced.85 Also, a green solvent that does not damage the chemical structure of the polymer should be chosen.107

The Landfester group reported the first-ever aqueous semiconducting polymer colloid fabricated via the miniemulsion process.108 They used sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a surfactant to make nanoparticles of polymeric semiconductors in an aqueous medium. The OFETs from these nanoparticles, however, have shown low $( \sim 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ mobilities, attributed to residual surfactant preventing charge transport in the OFET channel layer. More recently, researchers have investigated using other surfactants besides SDS, ones that may be easier to eliminate and hence allow the use of a miniemulsion to synthesize OSCs displaying enhanced electrical properties.109 After fabricating thin films from colloidal solutions, conducting post-processing procedures such as heat treatment, washing, pyrolysis and dialysis is needed to eliminate residual surfactants.106,109–112

In this section, some cases of FET device fabrication methods using organic nanoparticles are described. Cheon and co-workers fabricated FET devices by simply dispersing conjugated random copolymer nanoparticles in alcohol.87 They used the random copolymer (P-DPP-TT(7)-SVS(3), with a DPP-SVS unit content of $\approx 3 0 \%$ (diketopyrrolopyrrole-selenophene-vinyleneselenophene (DPP-SVS) and DPP-thienothiophene (DPP-TT)). The chemical structure of the P-DPP-TT(7)-SVS(3) is shown in Fig. 12. They sonicated P-DPP-TT(7)-SVS(3) in ethanol and butanol solvents for 2 to $^ { 6 \mathrm { { h } } }$ without further heating to disperse P-DPP-TT(7)-SVS(3) molecules and make sub-micrometer-sized polymer colloids. They observed that physically dispersed polymer colloids in alcohols were not damaged by oxidation. They also used X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXD) techniques to show the ordered crystalline nature of the alcohol-processed thin films of the polymeric semiconductors. Compared to chloroformprocessed films, the alcohol-processed films showed less of an edge-on orientation of the crystalline domain. The OFET device from colloids in butanol achieved a hole mobility as high as $1 . 0 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and the OFET device from colloids in ethanol yielded a lower hole mobility, of only $0 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ .

Cho and co-workers fabricated FET devices with surfactant-free, emulsion-dispersed nanoparticles of the polymeric semiconductor DPP-TT, as shown in Fig. 8.92 Polymer colloids were prepared in butyl acetate (BA) or ethyl acetate (EA) by carrying out a simple emulsion dispersion. The colloids were fabricated by dispersing polymer powder in BA or EA, achieved using sonication at room temperature. OFET devices from colloids in BA achieved a hole mobility as high as $2 . 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and the OFET device from colloids in EA yielded a lower hole mobility, of only $0 . 6 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Cho and co-workers also suggested carrying out a heat-assisted elimination of surfactant by utilizing the liquid crystalline nature of the PBTTT semiconductor.109 SDS surfactant has a relatively low thermal degradation temperature $\left( T _ { \mathrm { d } } \right)$ of $< 2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . They fabricated an aqueous colloid using PBTTT, which undergoes a second phase transition at annealing temperatures above $2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and has a $T _ { \mathbf { d } }$ of more than $\sim 4 5 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } . ^ { 1 1 3 }$ So high-temperature $\left( > 2 0 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } \right)$ thermal annealing was predicted to be possibly effective at removing the SDS surfactants from the PBTTT films. They showed that heat treatment of PBTTT films at $2 7 0 \mathrm { ~ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ indeed eliminated SDS surfactants efficiently and led to successful sintering of the PBTTT nanoparticles; it also led to a high charge carrier mobility of $0 . 1 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , comparable to those resulting from the use of organic solvents.109

Although SDS is a good surfactant for the dispersion of OSC nanoparticles, it has disadvantages when applied to an OFET active layer from SDS-dispersed nanoparticles. To overcome this issue involving SDS, Cho and co-workers also introduced a series of non-ionic surfactants each consisting of an ethoxy moiety as a hydrophilic group and a carbon chain, i.e., $\mathbf { C } _ { m } \mathbf { E } _ { n }$ $\mathrm { \ C _ { 1 2 } E _ { 4 } }$ , $\mathrm { C _ { 1 6 } E _ { 1 0 } }$ , and $\mathbf { C _ { 1 8 } E _ { 2 0 } }$ ).114 The chemical structure of $\mathrm { C _ { 1 2 } E _ { 4 } }$ is shown in Fig. 13. The non-ionic ethoxy group is much less polar than is SDS—and, therefore, it would act as a much less efficient charge-trapping center. And these surfactants, being mostly composed volumetrically of hydrophilic components, can be easily removed using a washing procedure. Cho and coworkers used PDPP-SVS, whose chemical structure is shown in Fig. 8. Fourier-transform infrared analyses were conducted for films of PDPP-SVS nanoparticles. They found that washing was effective at removing non-ionic surfactants efficiently. However, in SDS-based films, the SDS remained even after the films were washed. OFET performances were evaluated by depositing polymer particles from aqueous colloids. Compared to a chloroform-processed PDPP-SVS OFET with a hole mobility of $5 . 3 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ , a $\mathrm { C _ { 1 6 } E _ { 1 0 } }$ -based OFET processed from aqueous colloids yielded a high hole mobility, of up to $2 . 5 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The $\mathrm { C } _ { 1 2 } \mathrm { E } _ { 4 }$ -based OFET processed from aqueous colloids showed a slightly lower mobility value, of $1 . 3 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ presumably due to the smaller-sized polymer particles. The SDS-based OFET showed the poorest electrical performances, with a mobility value of $0 . 0 0 4 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ .

Cho and co-workers also demonstrated a universal colloid technique, one that can successfully produce water-based semiconductor thin films regardless of the polarity of the semiconductor polymer, by carrying out a highly efficient removal of residual surfactant.85 They investigated the effects of ionic character (anionic, cationic, and non-ionic) and alkyl chain length of surfactants on the colloidization of polymer semiconductors. They suggested a more efficient way to remove residual surfactant, namely carrying out a post-washing process with ethanol after fabricating the thin films from colloidal solutions. The developed colloid technique was applied to various polymeric semiconductors, namely P3HT (p-type), PBTTT (p-type), PIID-SVS (p-type), PDPP-SVS (p-type), PDPPCN-TVT (ambipolar), and PNDI2OD-T2 (n-type), as shown in Fig. 8. OFETs processed with the universal colloid technique showed more than half the field-effect mobility of OFETs processed with organic solvents.

Yu and co-workers studied the molecular affinity between OSCs and surfactants.106 They designed a new polymer semiconductor, termed SiDPP-TVT, that would have enhanced molecular affinity for ionic surfactants. SiDPP-TVT was designed to be highly soluble and highly hydrophobic without losing the semiconductor properties of the reference polymer semiconductor, DPP-TVT; this design involved replacing the branch point C atom of the alkyl solubilizing group with an Si atom. They found a more efficient transfer of SiDPP-TVT than of DPP-TVT from an organic phase to a water phase during miniemulsion synthesis, attributed to the better molecular affinity of SiDPP-TVT for ionic surfactants. The charge mobility of the OFET processed from the aqueous colloids was found to be $0 . 9 1 ~ { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { \Lambda } } \mathrm { \mathbf { S } } ^ { - 1 }$ , similar to that for the case of organic solvents.

Despite the research by the Cheon group and the Yu and Cho group on using green solution processing to fabricate nanoparticle-based thin films in OFET active layers, the synthesis and purification of the tested OSCs, namely P-DPP-TT(7)-SVS(3), DPP-TT, SiDPP-TVT, P3HT and other polymers, required large amounts of halogenated solvents. # 4. Conclusions and outlook

An overview of eco-friendly processing of OSCs in OFET devices has been summarized in this review article with a focus on the solvent properties, molecular structure and device processing engineering strategies. Side chain engineering of OSCs and disrupting the high symmetry of their polymer backbones or the regularity of the polymers can be effective ways of improving the solubility of OSCs in environmentally benign solvents. Many kinds of OSCs amenable to environmentally benign OFET fabrication procedures have been synthesized using this molecular engineering concept and strategy. In addition, green OFET fabrication technologies based on miniemulsions of nanoparticles of OSCs are also being considered.

While this paper mainly focuses on the contents including the relationship between OSC and solvent properties, OSC molecular structure for dissolving, and device fabrication processing methods, there is a need to consider eco-friendly solution processing methods with material respects and processing respects. Though out of the scope of this report, there is much research relating to green solvent-based processing, which has not been limited only to the OSCs. Indeed, numerous reports have been conducted for the fabrication of OFETs in other components such as electrodes, interlayers, dielectrics, and passivation layers.19,115

PEDOT:PSS, the most commonly used conductive polymer, is a water-soluble material that has been widely used as an electrode for OFETs by improving conductivity using eco-friendly solvents such as ethylene glycol and dimethyl sulfoxide.116,117 Watersoluble and alcohol-based silver inks have also been widely utilized as electrode materials for OFETs.118 Besides, Tang et al. reported the MXene ink dissolved in ethanol, and applied the MXene source/drain electrodes for OFETs.119 In addition, there were many results with the fabrication of an ion gel for dielectric layers of organic electrochemical transistors through green solvents like acetone.120,121 Caironi and his research group used honey in the supermarket as a dielectric layer for OFETs and integrated devices.122 Hong and her colleges applied organic– inorganic nanohybrid materials dissolved in ethanol as the passivation layer for OFETs.123 All these findings hold the promise of ecofriendly solution processed device manufacturing.

The final objective of this review is to provide evidence that combinations of green solvents and materials are possible and to provide a rationale to guide the development of more sustainable strategies towards organic electronic devices with ecofriendly circumstances. Currently, there are only a few studies related to organic electronic devices with this concept. Further development of eco-friendly processing for organic electronic devices is essential, and the value of such processing has been recognized by many research groups, which can contribute to realizing safe industrial production of nextgeneration organic electronics.
