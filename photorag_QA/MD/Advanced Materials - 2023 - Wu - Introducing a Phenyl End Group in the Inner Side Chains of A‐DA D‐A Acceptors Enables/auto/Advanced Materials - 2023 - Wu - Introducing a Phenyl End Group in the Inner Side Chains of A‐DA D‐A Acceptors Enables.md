# Introducing a Phenyl End Group in the Inner Side Chains of A-DA'D-A Acceptors Enables High-Efficiency Organic Solar Cells Processed with Nonhalogenated Solvent

Xiangxi Wu, Xin Jiang, Xiaojun Li,\* Jinyuan Zhang, Kan Ding, Hongmei Zhuo, Jing Guo, Jing Li, Lei Meng, Harald Ade,\* and Yongfang Li\*

Power conversion efficiency (PCE) of organic solar cells (OsCs) processed by nonhalogenated solvents is unsatisfactory due to the unfavorable morphology. Herein, two new small molecule acceptors (SMAs) Y6-Ph and L8-Ph are synthesized by introducing a phenyl end group in the inner side chains of the SMAs of Y6 and L8-BO, respectively, for overcoming the excessive aggregation of SMAs in the long-time film forming processed by nonhalogenated solvents. First, the effect of the film forming time on the aggregation property and photovoltaic performance of Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph is studied by using the commonly used solvents: chloroform (CF) (rapid film forming process) and chlorobenzene (CB) (slow film forming process). It is found that Y6- and L8-BO-based OSCs exhibit a dramatic drop in PCE from CF- to CB-processed devices owing to the large phase separation, while the Y6-Ph and L8-Ph based OSCs show obviously increased PCEs Furthermore, L8-Ph-based OsCs processed by nonhalogenated solvent o-xylene (o-XY) achieved a high PCE of $1 8 . 4 0 \%$ with an FF of $8 0 . 1 1 \%$ . The results indicate that introducing a phenyl end group in the inner side chains is an effective strategy to modulate the morphology and improve the photovoltaic performance of the OSCs processed by nonhalogenated solvents.

# 1. Introduction

Organic solar cells (OsCs) have drawn great attention owing to their advantages of light-weight, low-cost solution processability, fexibility, portability, and potential as an energy source for wearable electronics.[1-4] In recent years, small molecule acceptors (SMAs), especially the A-DA'D-A type SMAs, have promoted the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the OSCs from $1 5 \%$ in 2019 to $1 8 { - } 1 9 \%$ recently,[5-10] which reached the threshold for application. While realizing application, we also need to increase the stability and reduce the cost of the photovoltaic materials and devices.[11-12] In addition, in order to avoid the air pollution of the halogenated solvent,[13] the widely used halogenated solvent in the fabrication of high-performance OSCs at present should be replaced by nonhalogenated solvent for future application. Actually, the present high-performance A-DA'D-A type SMAs possess strong intermolecular interaction and high crystallinity, which is usually beneficial for charge transport.[14,15] This property needs the fast film-forming process of the donor (D):acceptor (A) blend active layer for the formation of suitable D:A interpenetrating network morphology, which requires the low-boiling-point solvent such as chloroform (CF). And the high PCEs are mostly obtained from the small-area OsCs by spin-coating the blend solution of the photovoltaic materials with fast evaporation of CF solvent to form the optimized morphology of the active layers.[5,16] However, CF is volatile and easily decomposed into highly corrosive gases, which will seriously deteriorate the equip. ment lifetime for the OsCs fabrication and cause serious air pollution problems.7] On the contrary, the green solvents (or called nonhalogenated solvents), able to dissolve the conjugated organic photovoltaic materials, typically have higher boiling points and lower solubility for the high-performance SMAs, which causes difficulty in morphology controlling the crystalline A-DA'D-A type SMAs. During the film forming process, the evaporation of the higher-boiling-point solvent needs a relatively longer time, which results in over aggregation of the A-DA'D-A type SMAs and uncontrollable morphology of the device active layers.[18,19] Thus, we need to develop high-performance photovoltaic materials that can be processed by green solvent in fabricating the OSCs with a longer film forming process. In addition, the appropriate longer film forming process will be beneficial to the fabrication of large-area devices by printing technology, because it will be difficult in the morphology control of large-area active layers by the fast solvent evaporation for future application of the OsCs.[20,21]

Based on the above analyses, controlling the excessive aggregation of the SMAs would be the effective way to realize green-solvent processing and large-area preparation in industrial production. Side chain engineering is widely used to tune the intermolecular interaction and aggregation properties of the SMAs.122-26] And some work has been reported to tune the aggregation of SMAs and reduce their excessive aggregation in the longer-time film forming process by extending the alkyl side chains.[20,27] However, there exists detrimental effects as the reduced $\pi \cdot \pi$ stacking interactions will inevitably influence the carrier mobilities.231 Furthermore, increasing the processing temperature of nonhalogenated solvents can increase the filmforming rate, and reduce the excessive aggregation of SMAs, but this will cause serious energy loss and decrease device repeatability.[19]

Here, we consider balancing the molecular packing and aggregation of the A-DA'D-A type SMAs by introducing a phenyl end group in its branched short alkyl inner side chains (we call it "phenyl-alkyl side chains" in the following). The phenyl unit in the tail of linear alkyl side chains has been used to modulate the crystallinity of ID1C.[28] In this work, the phenyl end group in the phenyl-alkyl side chains will increase the molecular solubility while preventing the rapid aggregation of SMAs in the filmforming process. Furthermore, the phenyl end group in the side chains would further modulate the intermolecular interaction and facilitate the molecular packing during the relatively long film-forming process with high boiling-point solvents. Therefore, we designed and synthesized two new SMAs Y6-Ph and L8-Ph (their molecular structures are shown in Figure 1a) by introducing the phenyl-alkyl inner side chains instead of the branched alkyl inner side chains on the DA'D fused ring of the representative A-DA'D-A type SMAs Y6 and L8-BO.[5,24] The differential scanning calorimeter (DsC) measurement indicates that Y6-Ph and L8-Ph have relatively weaker crystallinity than that of Y6 and L8-BO, which is beneficial to reduce the rapid crystallization of molecules in the film-forming process of active layers of their corresponding OSCs. Then, we compared the photovoltaic performance of the four SMAs of Y6-Ph, L8-Ph, Y6, and L8-BO by using PM6 as polymer donor processed by the most commonly used low-boiling-point solvent CF and high-boiling-point solvent chlorobenzene (CB) to demonstrate the effect of the solvent boiling point on their photovoltaic performance. In the CF-processed films, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph represent blue-shifted absorption compared with corresponding Y6 and L8-BO, owing to the steric hindrance of the phenyl end group in the inner side chains to prevent molecular stacking. While for the CB-processed films, Y6- Ph and L8-Ph not only exhibit the similar absorption edge of Y6 and L8-BO, but also present more ordered molecular packing, achieving a balance between molecular aggregation and packing in the films processed by high-boiling-point solvents. Excitingly, the OSCs based on PM6:L8-Ph showed an impressive PCE of $1 8 . 8 3 \%$ . Then, L8-Ph was further used in the OSCs processed through green-solvent $o$ xylene $\left( 0 { - } \mathrm { X Y } \right)$ , and a higher PCE of $1 8 . 4 0 \%$ with a high FF of $8 0 . 1 1 \%$ was achieved. Moreover, the L8-Ph-based blend films also present great tolerance against large-area preparation, an outstanding efficiency of $1 6 . 6 6 \%$ was achieved for $1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ area OSC with $o$ XY-processing. These results verified that the acceptors bearing bulky phenyl-alkyl inner side chains possess favorable crystallinity and suitable aggregation in the films formed by slow film forming process under the nonhalogenated solvent processing, thus showing good potential for industrial production of the OSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Synthesis and Characterization

Figure 1a shows the molecular structures of the A-DA'D-A type SMAs of Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph, and the detailed synthesis processes of the SMAs of Y6-Ph and L8-Ph with the phenylalkyl branched side chain are described in Supporting Information. The molecular structures of the monomer, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and the corresponding $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR and $^ { 1 3 } \mathrm { C }$ NMR spectra are shown in Figures S1-s20 (Supporting Information). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to investigate the thermal stability of Y6-Ph and L8-Ph, and the results are shown in Figure S21 (Supporting Information). Y6-Ph and L8-Ph exhibit decomposition temperatures with $5 \%$ weight loss at 317.8 and $3 2 8 . 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ respectively, which are high enough for the application as photovoltaic materials in OSCs.

To investigate the crystallinity of the two new acceptors of Y6- Ph and L8-Ph in comparison with Y6 and L8-BO, DSC measurement was carried out with a heating rate of $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ under $\Nu _ { 2 }$ atmosphere for the four SMAs. As shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), all four SMAs display obvious melting peaks, revealing that there exist crystalline structures for the SMAs. The melting point $( T _ { \mathrm { m } } )$ of Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph are 299, 321, 248, $2 8 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and the corresponding melting enthalpy $( \Delta H _ { \mathrm { m } } )$ are $2 8 . 7 \mathrm { J } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } , 4 3 . 1 \mathrm { J } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } , 2 2 . 8 \mathrm { J } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } , 3 1 . 3 \mathrm { J } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Compared with Y6, the higher $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ and $\Delta H _ { \mathrm { m } }$ of L8-BO could be ascribed to its branched alkyl outer side chains. In addition, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph with the phenyl-alkyl inner side chains show lower $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ and $\Delta H _ { \mathrm { m } }$ than that of Y6 and L8-BO, respectively. These results indicate that the phenyl-alkyl inner side chains can decrease the molecular crystallinity, which will help to reduce the rapid crystallization of the SMAs in the film-forming process.

To investigate the influence of the phenyl end group of the alkyl inner side chains on the aggregation behavior of the SMAs under different film-forming conditions, the UV-vis absorption spectra of Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph were measured and compared for the four SMA films prepared from the low-boiling-point CF solutions and those prepared from the high-boiling-point CB solutions. Figure S23 (Supporting Information) and Figure 1b,c show the absorption spectra of the SMAs solution and films respectively, and the corresponding optical data of the SMAs solutions and films are listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information) and Table 1 respectively. As shown in Figure S23 (Supporting Information), the absorption spectra of Y6, Y6-Ph and L8-BO, L8-Ph in CF and CB solutions are almost identical, with a maximum absorption peak located at 730 and $7 2 9 \mathrm { n m }$ in the CF and CB solutions, respectively, which indicates that the absorption spectra of the SMAs are mainly determined by the molecular A-DA'DA backbone of the SMAs (the four SMAs possess the same ADA'D-A backbone). The absorption spectra of the SMAs films, as shown in Figure 1b,c, are red-shifted than those of their solutions, indicating stronger intermolecular interaction and molecular aggregation in the solid state than in solution. Interestingly, the degree of the absorption spectra red-shift of the four SMAs is different, which is also influenced by the processing solvents. For the films processed by CF, the maximum absorption peaks $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ are at 820, 810, 794, and $7 8 9 \mathrm { n m }$ with the absorption coefficient of $1 . 1 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ $1 . 1 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ $1 . 3 3 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 2 3 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ for Y6, Y6-Ph, L8-BO, and L8-Ph, respectively (Figure S24, Supporting Information). Obviously, the branched alkyl upper side chains in L8-BO weakened its intermolecular interaction and resulted in the significantly blue-shifted absorption (by $2 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ ) of its film in comparison with that of Y6 film, and the phenyl end group in the inner side chains of $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ and L8-Ph also influenced the intermolecular interaction and leads to $5 { - } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ blue shift of their film absorption than that of their corresponding Y6 and L8-BO films due to the steric hindrance of the phenyl end group in Y6-Ph and L8-Ph. Compared with the CF-processed films, the absorption spectra of the SMAs films processed by CB display further redshift, and the corresponding $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ moves to 848, 834, 799, and $7 9 7 { \mathrm { n m } }$ , respectively. The further red-shifted absorption is mainly attributed to the longer filmforming time of the CB-processed films, so that the molecules can be fully assembled to form a closer packing. In contrast, the branched alkyl upper side chains in L8-BO and L8-Ph will inhibit the molecular aggregation in the high-boiling-point solvent, as evidenced by that the absorption spectra of their films processed by CB exhibit a nearly identical absorption peak with that of the CF-processed films. For the CB-processed films, the difference of absorption peaks and the absorption edges between L8-BO and L8-Ph are significantly reduced (Table 1), indicating that the phenyl group does not prevent their molecular aggregation during the longer film-forming process, and even the phenyl groups could further promote the SMAs to form more ordered packing. This can be demonstrated by the changes in the shoulder peaks exhibited by L8-BO and L8-Ph at $\approx 7 1 0 - 7 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . From L8-BO to L8-Ph, the decreased intensity of the shoulders elucidates a gradually increased $J$ type aggregation of molecules,[29-31] which is mainly owing to that the phenyl-alkyl side chains may provide more abundant intermolecular interaction to modulate the molecules to form more ordered packing. And more ordered molecular packing will be beneficial to efficient charge transport.[32]

Frontier orbital energy levels of Y6, Y6-Ph, L8-BO, and L8- Ph were measured by cyclic voltammetry with Ag/AgCl as reference electrode and the ferrocene/ferrocenium $( \mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } } )$ (the redox potential of $\mathrm { F c } / \mathrm { F c ^ { + } } \left( { \varphi _ { \mathrm { F c / F c } } } ^ { + } \right)$ is $0 . 4 4 \mathrm { V }$ versus $\mathrm { \sf A g / A g C l ) }$ redox couple as an internal reference, and their cyclic voltammograms are shown in Figure S25 (Supporting Information). The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels (EHomo/LUMo) of the SMAs were calculated according to the equations: $E _ { \mathrm { H O M O / L U M O } } = \cdot \mathrm { e } ( \varphi _ { \mathrm { o x / r e d } } + 4 . 8 - \varphi _ { \mathrm { F c / F c } } { } ^ { + } )$ (eV), where onset oxidation and onset reduction potentials $( \varphi _ { \mathrm { o x } }$ and $\varphi _ { \mathrm { r e d } } )$ were obtained from their cyclic voltammograms (Figure S25, Supporting Information).[33] The calculated results are listed in Table 1 and shown in Figure 1d. The $E _ { \mathrm { H O M O } } / E _ { \mathrm { L U M O } }$ values of $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ and L8- Ph are $- 5 . 7 0 / - 4 . 0 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 6 7 / - 3 . 9 1$ eV respectively, which is exactly the same as those of their corresponding SMAs of Y6 and L8-BO. These results demonstrate that the phenyl end group in the alkyl inner side chains has no influence on the molecular frontier orbital energy levels.

# 2.2. Photovoltaic Properties

To investigate the influence of phenyl end group in the alkyl inner side chains on the photovoltaic performances of the SMAsbased OSCs at different film forming rates (processed from the solvents with different boiling points), the OsCs were fabricated by using low-boiling-point solvent CF and high-boilingpoint solvent CB respectively with the traditional device structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layers/PDINN/Ag. PM6 was selected as a polymer donor in the OsCs in considering its complementary absorption (Figure S26, Supporting Information) and energy level matching with the Y6 derivative acceptors, Y6, L8-BO, $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ , and L8-Ph. The device fabrication conditions were conducted following the reported article.5l The corresponding current density-voltage $\left( J \cdot W \right)$ curves of the optimal OSCs processed by CF and CB are shown in Figure 2a,b respectively, and the relevant photovoltaic parameter data are listed in Table 2. Under CFprocessed conditions, the OSCs based on PM6:Y6 and PM6:L8- BO display PCE of $1 6 . 2 2 \%$ and $1 7 . 7 4 \%$ respectively. The Y6-Phbased device shows a slightly lower PCE of $1 6 . 0 1 \%$ in comparison with the corresponding Y6-based devices due to slightly reduced FF ( $7 4 . 1 0 \%$ vs $7 5 . 5 5 \% )$ . The slightly reduced FF could be ascribed to the bulky steric hindrance of the phenyl-alkyl side chains of $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ , which impedes molecular packing in the fast film forming process. Interestingly, the OSC based on PM6:L8-Ph demonstrated a higher PCE of $1 8 . 0 9 \%$ than that of the corresponding L8- BO-based device, benefitted from its simultaneously increased $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF (Table 2). The increased $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF indicate that the introduction of phenyl end group in the alkyl inner side chains plays a positive role in regulating molecular stacking and phase separation of the L8-BO derivative SMAs.

While for the CB-processed OsCs, the phenyl end group plays a significant role in improving photovoltaic performance of the SMAs (Table 2), due to the long film-forming process of their active layers to evaporate the high-boiling-point CB solvent. The long film-forming process allows the SMAs to aggregate for a longer period. The OsCs based on PM6:Y6 and PM6:L8-BO showed poorer PCEs of $1 3 . 3 7 \%$ and $1 6 . 2 8 \%$ respectively, due to the decreased $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF values in comparison with the CF-processed devices (see Figure 2b and Table 2). Compared with the Y6-based device, the less reduction in PCE of the L8-BO-based device may be ascribed to that the branched outer side chains of L8-BO reduced its over aggregation to some extent. In contrast, the devices based on Y6-Ph and L8- Ph with phenyl-alkyl inner side chains demonstrated improved photovoltaic performance with CB as the processing solvent (Table 2). For instance, the Y6-Ph-based devices exhibit a higher PCE of $1 6 . 8 0 \%$ with increased $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ $( 2 6 . 9 2 \mathrm { \ m A } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ and FF $( 7 6 . 1 8 \% )$ than its CF-processed devices. This indicates that Y6- Ph not only effectively inhibits its over aggregation, but also promotes the formation of more suitable active layer morphology in the long-time film-forming process, which is beneficial for obtaining the higher PCEs. More importantly, the L8-Phbased device processed by CB promoted the PCE to an even higher value of $1 8 . 8 3 \%$ , with simultaneously improved $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ of 0.876 V, $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of $2 6 . 6 8 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } } ,$ and FF of $8 0 . 5 5 \%$ , which outperforms that of the L8-BO-based devices processed by CF and CB (Table 2). The results indicate that the phenyl end group in the inner side chains leads to the ideal aggregation of L8- BO derivative in the OsCs processed by the high-boiling-point solvent.

It is worth noting that the devices based on $\mathrm { P M } 6 { : } \mathrm { Y } 6 { \cdot } \mathrm { P h }$ and PM6:L8-Ph processed by the high-boiling-point solvent CB exhibit significantly improved $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ compared with that of the devices based on Y6 and L8-BO. To better understand this phenomenon, external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement was conducted to evaluate the photoelectric responses of the OSCs. The EQE curves of the devices processed by CB are shown in Figure 2c, and those of the devices processed by CF are shown in Figure S27 (Supporting Information). The integrated current densities from the EQE curves show good consistency with the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ values obtained from $J { \cdot } V$ curves within a $5 \%$ mismatch. As depicted in Figure 2c and Table S2 (Supporting Information), the $\lambda _ { \mathrm { o n s e t } }$ of EQE curves for the PM6:Y6-Ph and PM6:L8-Ph blend films processed by CB show slightly red-shifts compared with the absorption of the neat SMA films, while blue-shifts are observed for the PM6:Y6 and PM6:L8-BO blends, which indicates that PM6 polymer donor influences the intermolecular interaction of the SMAs in different ways: enhanced the intermolecular interaction for Y6-Ph and L8-Ph, and weakened the intermolecular interaction for Y6 and L8-BO. The results indicate that the phenyl end group in the alkyl inner side chains benefits more ordered molecular packing in the CB-processed blend films, which is significantly different from the SMAs with long alkyl side chains for suppressing over aggregation but at the expense of losing some molecular ordered packing.[23,34] Based on the above reasons, the $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ and L8-Ph-based devices show a more red-shifted and higher response of the EQE curves than that of the Y6 and L8-BO-based ones. The $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ value of Y6-Ph-based device integrated from the EQE spectrum is $2 6 . 6 3 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ (while it is $2 3 . 8 4 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ for the PM6:Y6 based device). As for the OSC based on PM6:L8-Ph, the highest EQE response exceeds $90 \%$ outperforming that of the PM6:L8-BO based device, and the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ value integrated from its EQE spectrum reached $2 5 . 9 6 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ (in comparison with $2 3 . 7 6 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ for the PM6:L8-BO based device).

# 2.3. Exciton Dissociation, Charge Transport and Recombination Properties

The exciton dissociation, charge transport, and recombination characteristics in the blend films were investigated to further explore the effect of the phenyl-alkyl side chains on the photovoltaic performance of the SMAs. The dependence of photocur. rent density $( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } )$ on the effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ of the devices processed by CB was investigated, and the results are shown in Figure 2d and Table S3 (Supporting Information). In the measurements, the exciton dissociation efficiency $( P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } } )$ could be calculated according to the formula of $P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } } = J _ { \mathrm { p h } } / J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ under shortcircuit conditions.135] Consequently, the $P _ { \mathrm { d i s s } }$ values were estimated to be $9 1 . 0 5 \%$ $9 6 . 3 0 \%$ $9 6 . 7 8 \%$ , and $9 7 . 2 2 \%$ for the devices based on PM6:Y6, PM6:L8-BO, PM6:Y6-Ph, and PM6:L8- Ph, respectively. These results indicate that the exciton dissociation efficiency of devices based on the SMAs with phenyl-alkyl in. ner side chains is significantly improved. The L8-Ph-based device possesses the most efficient exciton dissociation, which could explain the highest $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF of the L8-Ph-based devices.

The charge transport properties of these devices were investigated by using the space-charge limited current (SCLC) method. The hole and electron mobilities $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } )$ of the blends processed with CB were measured. As presented in Figure S28 and Table S4 (Supporting Information), the Y6-based films show relatively lower electron mobilities $( 1 . 4 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { ~ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ s } ^ { - 1 } )$ than that of the L8-BO, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph-based films. The relatively lower electron mobility and unbalanced $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ value of 2.46 suggest suppressed charge transport. As a contrast, the PM6:Y6-Ph blends present improved $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ of $2 . 2 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and more balanced $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ as 1.58. In addition, the charge carrier mobilities of the devices were evaluated by photon-induced charge-carrier extraction with linearly increasing voltage (photo-CELIV). As shown in Figure 2e, the charge carrier mobilities of the PM6:Y6-Ph and PM6:L8- Ph-based devices are $1 . 8 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 6 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , surpassing that of the PM6:Y6 and PM6:L8-BObased devices $( 1 . 4 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 3 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ $\mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ . These trends are in line well with that measured by the SCLC method. Here, the relatively lower carrier mobilities of the Y6 and L8-BO-based devices processed in CB would probably result from the excessive aggregation of SMAs, which will lead to a strong recombination loss, and thus decrease the $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF.

Furthermore, the dependence of $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ on the illumination light intensity $( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ was investigated to provide further insight into the charge carrier recombination mechanism. The relationship of $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ follows the equation $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } \propto P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } ^ { \alpha }$ [36] the closer to 1 the $\alpha$ value is, the less bimolecular recombination of the charge carriers will occur in the blend film. As shown in Figure 2f, the $\alpha$ values of the Y6-Ph and L8-Ph-based devices are 0.992 and 0.988, respectively, which are higher and closer to 1 than the other two devices (0.978 for PM6:Y6 and 0.983 for PM6:L8-BO), demonstrating the significantly suppressed bimolecular recombination of the charge carriers in the films based on Y6-Ph and L8-Ph. In addition, the recombination properties were also evaluated by transient photovoltage (TPV) measurement (Figure S29, Supporting Information). As shown in Table S5 (Supporting Information), the charge carrier lifetime of the devices based on Y6-Ph $( 2 . 1 7 ~ \mu \mathrm { s } )$ and L8-Ph $( 2 . 4 7 ~ \mu \mathrm { s } )$ was higher than that of the devices based on Y6 $( 1 . 7 9 ~ \mu \mathrm { s } )$ and L8-BO $( 1 . 9 5 ~ \mu \mathrm { s } )$ , which indicates that carrier recombination of the devices based on the SMAs with phenyl-alkyl inner side chains are decreased. This is in line with the results derived from the dependence of $\dot { J } _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ on the light intensity of the devices. Furthermore, the charge extraction characteristics were measured by transient photocurrent of the devices. As shown in Figure S30 (Supporting Information), the PM6:Y6-Ph and PM6:L8-Ph-based devices present faster turn-off dynamics than that of the PM6:Y6 and PM6:L8-BO-based ones, suggesting faster charge extraction and less trapped charges in blends.

To investigate the effect of the phenyl-alkyl side chains on the photophysical properties of the SMAs, broadband femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TA) (Figure 3 and Figure S31, Supporting Information) was carried out to study the charge transfer (CT) and recombination on the CB-processed blend films of PM6:Y6, PM6:Y6-Ph, PM6:L8-BO and PM6:L8-Ph (Figure S32, Supporting Information).[37,38] At first, we obtained the exciton spectra and excited state dynamics of the four acceptors by photoexciting their neat film. Figure S33 (Supporting Information) showed the fs-TA spectra of the four acceptors at selected time delays. We then investigated the hole transfer behavior of PM6:Y6-Ph films by selectively photoexciting the ac ceptor Y6-Ph at $8 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ wavelength. As shown in Figure 3a, immediately after excitation, the spectrum shows ground-state bleach (GSB) with peaks at 850, 733, and $6 3 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and an excited state absorption (ESA) peak at $9 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The GSB peaks at 850, $7 3 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and the ESA peak at $9 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ could be ascribed to the exciton of $\mathrm { Y 6 - P h }$ , and the $6 3 6 \ \mathrm { n m \ G S B }$ peak could be ascribed to the cation of donor PM6,[39] indicating ultrafast hole transfer. In the first 2 ps, the Y6-Ph exciton signal decays rapidly, while after 10 ps, the transient spectrum is basically dominated by the CT state that shows ESA peaks at 710, 780, and $9 8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , as well as GSB peaks at 580, 636, and $8 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The spectral features of the CT state slowly decay and recombine to the ground state over 5 ns. The transient absorption spectrum of PM6:Y6 blend film was collected under the same condition, as shown in Figure 3b, and the kinetic traces at 733 and $9 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ were compared with that of the PM6:Y6-Ph blend film to study the CT state dynamics of the two blends. Figure 3c shows that the $7 3 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ peak, which belongs to Y6-Ph exciton, decays faster than Y6 exciton in the blends at early time, suggesting a faster hole transfer channel in the Y6-Ph-based films. In addition, global fitting was performed on the TA kinetics of the Y6 and Y6-Ph-based systems (Figure S34, Supporting Information), and the corresponding lifetimes and hole transfer rates $( k _ { \mathrm { H T } } )$ were extracted and displayed in Table S6 (Supporting Information), which exhibits that the PM6: Y6-Ph blend possesses higher $k _ { \mathrm { H T } }$ than that of the PM6:Y6 blend. On the other hand, as shown in Figure 3d, the ESA of the CT state at $9 8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ decays slower in the Y6-Ph blend than that in the Y6 blends, which means PM6:Y6-Ph blends exhibit a slower nongeminate recombination process (Figure S35, Supporting Information). The faster hole transfer and slower recombination result in a higher $J _ { s c }$ and FF in the PM6:Y6-Ph-based devices. We also measured the transient absorption spectra of PM6:L8-BO and PM6:L8-Ph blend films (Figure 3e,f) with excitation wavelength at $8 1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . The two samples display similar spectral profiles, but the kinetic traces at $7 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ shows that L8-Ph exciton signal decays faster than L8-BO exciton due to its higher hole transfer rates of L8-Ph than that of L8-BO, and the non-geminate recombination is found to be much slower in the PM6:L8-Ph blends than the PM6:L8-BO blends when probing at $9 8 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the ESA of the CT state, as indicated in Figure 3g,h. These results agree well with the higher $J _ { s c }$ and FF in their device parameters.

# 2.4. Morphology Characterization

Grazing-incident wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXs) was used to investigate the impact of phenyl-alkyl side chains on the molecular packing and orientations of the SMAs in neat films.[40,41] We prepared the thin films of Y6, Y6-Ph, L8-BO, and L8-Ph on Si substrates by spin-coating from their CF and CB solutions. Figure S36 (Supporting Information) displays the 2D GIWAXS patterns of the corresponding SMA films and their line cuts in the in-plane and out-of-plane directions. All the neat materials show face-on orientation with $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the out-ofplane direction. As shown in Figure S36 and Table S7 (Supporting Information), Y6-Ph exhibits slightly increased $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distances than that of Y6 (from 3.55 to $3 . 5 8 \mathrm { \AA }$ while increased crystal coherence lengths (CCL) under the CF-processed condition. This may be due to the steric hindrance of phenyl-alkyl side chains leading to a larger molecular stacking distance, but the abundant molecular interactions of aryl promote molecular stacking. A similar phenomenon is observed in the L8-BO and L8-Ph-based systems, with increased $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distances and higher normalized integrated intensity of the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking from L8-BO to L8-Ph. From CF-processed films to CB-processed films, Y6 adopts several different packing modes with significantly decreased integrated intensity of the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking in the out-ofplane direction. And this change is not obvious in L8-BO-based film, which may owe to their branched outer side chains inhibiting the aggregation of L8-BO to some extent. For the Y6-Ph and L8-Ph, the change of processing solvent did not significantly affect the molecular $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking (Table S7, Supporting Information), but it is worth noting that the integrated intensity of the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking of Y6-Ph and L8-Ph in CB solution is higher than that of Y6 and L8-BO, which indicates that the SMAs with phenyl-alkyl inner side chains have more ordered molecular packing in the CB-processed films.[42,43]

To gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between the molecular structure of the acceptors and their related device performances, we conducted GIWAXS measurements on the blend films based on these acceptors. The different morphology characteristics of these films could be explained on the basis of the influence of thermodynamic and kinetic processes on the final microstructures. When using CF as the processing solvent, the wet films were solidified rapidly due to the low boiling point of the solvent, and reasonable phase separation can be fixed for Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph-based films. However, when changing the processing solvent from CF to CB, the drying process of wet films will be prolonged, giving the materials a relatively long time to aggregate. For the Y6 and L8-BO based blends in CB, during the film-forming process, these molecules tend to over-aggregate due to the prolonged drying process, which results in excessive phase separation (Figure 5i). Furthermore, different from the long side chains substituted molecules with suppressive aggregation coming at the expense of molecular packing (Figure 5j), the GIWAXS patterns exhibit that Y6-Ph and L8-Ph possess the highly ordered molecular packing in the CB-processed films, which mainly comes from the further selfassembly of SMAs under the abundant interaction provided by the phenyl end group. Finally, the suitable phase separation and ordered molecular packing are all beneficial for the red-shifted absorption spectra, efficient energy transfer, and reduced charge combination, and hence help achieve impressively high $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ , FF, and PCE of the devices processed by the high-boiling-point solvent CB.

# 2.5. Application of L8-Ph in Green-Solvent Processing and Large-Area Fabrication of OsCs

The low solubility for organic photovoltaic materials and high boiling point of the nonhalogenated solvents cause difficulties in active layer morphology tuning, and commonly lead to poorer photovoltaic performance of the OsCs.44,451 Considering that the SMAs of Y6-Ph and L8-Ph with the phenyl-alkyl inner side chains show increased solubility and solve the excessive aggregation problem in the high-boiling-point solvent, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph could be suitable for application as an acceptor in the OsCs processed by the high-boiling-point nonhalogenated solvents, such as the commonly used green-solvent $\boldsymbol { 0 } { \cdot } \mathrm { X Y } . ^ { [ 1 7 ] }$ To compare the device performance of the Y6, Y6-Ph, L8-BO, and L8-Ph SMAs in the OSCs processed with the high-boiling-point solvents of CB or $\partial { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { X Y } ,$ we fabricated the OSCs based on the SMAs with PM6 as polymer donor by using o-XY as processing solvent. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, the phenyl end group was introduced in the branched alkyl inner side chains for overcoming the uncontrollable aggregation of SMAs during slow film formation process in the OSCs processed by the high-boiling-point nonhalogenated solvent, and two new SMAs of Y6-Ph and L8-Ph were synthesized by replacing the alkyl inner side chains of Y6 and L8-BO with the phenyl-alkyl inner side chains. The effect of the phenyl-alkyl side chains on molecular crystallinity and aggregation behavior of the SMAs and on the active layer morphology of the OSCs processed by low-boiling-point solvent CF and high-boiling-point solvent CB were systematically investigated and compared for the four SMAs of Y6, L8-BO, Y6-Ph, and L8-Ph. The DSC measurement indicates that the phenyl-alkyl side chains substitution can effectively weaken the molecular crystallinity. In the CB-processed films, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph not only exhibit the similar absorption edge of Y6 and L8-BO, but also present more ordered molecular packing. Owing to the prolonged film-forming process, Y6 and L8-BO-based blends in CB tend to over-aggregate, which results in excessive phase separation. For the corresponding Y6-Ph and L8-Ph-based films, the steric hindrance effect of phenyl-alkyl side chains can suppress the molecular excessive aggregation and promote the active layer to form reasonable phase separation. Simultaneously, Y6-Ph and L8-Ph can form more ordered molecular packing in the CB-processed films. The more suitable phase separation and ordered molecular packing make the Y6-Ph and L8- Ph based films possess more effective exciton dissociation and less charge carrier recombination than that of the Y6 and L8- BO-based devices processed by CB. Finally, the Y6-Ph and L8-Phbased devices exhibit the PCEs of $1 6 . 8 0 \%$ and $1 8 . 8 3 \%$ receptively, which are significantly higher than that of the Y6 and L8-BObased ones. Moreover, the PM6:L8-Ph blends display great compatibility with green-solvent and large-area preparation, showing extraordinary PCE of $1 8 . 4 0 \%$ and $1 6 . 6 6 \%$ for $0 . 0 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ and 1 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ devices under $o { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { X Y }$ processing conditions, respectively. And $1 8 . 4 0 \%$ is the highest PCE in the green-solvent processed OSCs at present. In short, our results highlight the importance of introducing phenyl end group in the inner side chains on aggregation, crystallization, and morphology control of the SMAs for achieving higher performance large-area OsCs by green-solvent processing.
