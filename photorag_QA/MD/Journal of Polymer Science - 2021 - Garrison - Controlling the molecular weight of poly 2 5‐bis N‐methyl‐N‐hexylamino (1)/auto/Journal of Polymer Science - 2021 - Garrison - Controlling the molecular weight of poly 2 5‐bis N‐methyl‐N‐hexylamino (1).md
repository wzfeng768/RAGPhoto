# Abstract

Poly(2,5-bis[N-methyl-N-hexylamino] phenylene vinylene) (BAM-PPV) has been studied for several decades as an anti-corrosion coating. Although the polymer is readily synthesized via base-promoted radical chain polymerization, BAM-PPV exhibits poor solubility at high-molecular weights $\mathrm { ( M _ { w } > 1 0 0 ~ k D a ) }$ which limits its applications. In this work, the molecular weight of BAM-PPV was modulated with nitrobenzene. At nitrobenzene loading as low as $0 . 9 \ \mathrm { m o l }$ $\%$ , the polymerization reaction produced low-molecular weight BAM-PPV $( \mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { w } } = 5 { - } 4 6 ~ \mathrm { k D a } )$ . The polymerization of BAM-PPV was systematically studied via size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectroscopy, which revealed suppression of the dimer intermediate before chain polymerization. Collectively, this work could expand the practical applications of BAM-PPV for use in optoelectronic devices or corrosion inhibition coatings.

# KEYWORDS

conjugated polymers, Gilch polymerization, molecular weight control, nitrobenzene, radical inhibition

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Since their initial discovery, organic conjugated polymers have received significant attention due to their relatively high-electrical conductivity and unique optoelectronic properties, which has promoted their use in several commercial applications.1-4 Poly $\acute { p }$ phenylene vinylene) (PPV) polymers are a well-studied family of conjugated polymers and have been instrumental in the advancement of conjugated polymer technology through their incorporation into organic light emitting diodes (OLEDs),\* organic photovoltaics (Opvs),-11 sensors, and even bio-imaging probes.13,14 The most commonly studied PPVs contain alkoxy-substituents on the phenylene ring, but other substituent functionalities such as thio-alkyl and amino groups have also demonstrated unique properties with promising applications.15-17 Specifically, poly(2,5-bis[N-methyl-N-hexylamino]phenylene vinylene) (BAM-PPV, Scheme 1) has shown superior corrosion protection for aluminum alloys with performance matching hexavalent chromium primers in laboratory and field testing.18-21 Therefore, BAM-PPV has potential as a low toxicity alternative to conventional yet toxic metal coatings. In addition, the semi-conducting polymer has been studied as an OLED, and recently, as a polymer scaffold for methyl ammonium lead halide perovskite $\left( \mathrm { { M A P b I } } _ { 3 } \right)$ crystals.22 Unfortunately, like most PPVs, BAM-PPV has low solubility in common organic solvents (often below $1 \ \mathrm { w t } \%$ , which makes the polymer difficult to process and use in large-scale applications.19-21 Two typical approaches to improving PPV solubility are the introduction of solubilizing alkyl groups onto the PPV chain and controlling the molecular weight of the PPV chain during polymerization.3,6,23-28 BAM-PPV contains methyl- and hexyl-substituents on each amino-group that act as solubilizing groups, but molecular weight control of BAM-PPV has not yet been explored. This approach could be advantageous for use in commercial applications.

BAM-PPV is typically synthesized using the Gilch synthetic route as shown in Scheme 1, which involves formation of pPVs from 1,4-bis(halomethyl)-benzene compounds (typically chloro- or bromo-substituted) using potassium tert-butoxide. The mechanism of Gilch polymerization has been studied rigorously over the past two decades. Initially, an anionic mechanism had been proposed as a potential polymerization mechanism for Gilch synthesis.23,24,29-31 In contrast, more recent studies provide significant evidence for a radical polymerization mechanism.25-27,32-39 Cosemans et al. showed that the mechanism can be radical or anionic when using asymmetric precursors via the sulfinyl route, and the specific mechanism was dependent on the reaction conditions such as choices of solvent and base.4 Scheme 2 shows the generally accepted radical mechanism for the Gilch polymerization. In the first step, potassium tert-butoxide (KOtBu) is added to a 1,4-bis(halomethyl)-benzene to form a $p$ -quinodimethane via E2 elimination. The $p$ -quinodimethane is a reaction intermediate and is considered the active monomer during chain propagation.32,41 The polymerization initiator is a diradical formed from dimerization of the active monomer, and dimerization is considered to be the ratelimiting step of the mechanism.42 After the diradical dimer forms, chain propagation occurs rapidly between the diradical and $p$ -quinodimethane monomers to form the pre-polymer typically denoted as PPX. The growing polymer chain is a $\alpha$ -diradical, so recombination with other growing chains does not result in chain termination.39 In the final mechanistic step, the PPX pre-polymer is converted to the PPV polymer by elimination of HX with excess base. Typically, in the later stages of the Gilch reaction, dihalo-[2.2]paracyclophane and monohalo-[2.2]paracyclophene byproducts are observed because the $p$ -quinodimethane concentration is too low, which results in the intramolecular cyclization of the dimer initiator becoming favored over chain polymerization. 4243 However, the [2.2]paracyclophanes are typically minor byproducts that are easily removed by filtration and washing of the PPV polymer. Overall, the Gilch polymerization is an efficient method to provide PPV polymers in high yields.

Several studies have been performed to control PPV molecular weight and produce more processable polymers. Recently, advanced studies using continuous flow reactors have been implemented, and the method looks promising for obtaining uniform PPVs in high yields.44 46 In addition, another extensively studied approach to controlling PPV molecular weight involves the use of small molecule additives that can terminate the growing polymer chain. A few studies have focused on the use of mono-halogenated additives as terminal end-cap groups.23,30,36 The most recent work by Schwalm et al. suggests that the additives are not incorporated into the chain as endcaps, but the monohalogenated additives prevent gelation by solubilizing the polymer chain or inhibiting conversion of the prepolymer to the active polymer.36 Considering the radical nature of the Gilch polymerization, a similar approach is the addition of radical scavengers to the polymerization reaction for molecular weight control. For example, 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO) has been used as both an initiator and scavenger (depending on reaction conditions) in several radical polymerization processes.47-51 The stable nitroxide radical has also been studied for PPV molecular weight control and was able to substantially lower the PPV molecular weight at low inhibitor loadings (0.1 equiv. compared to the premonomer).26,52,53 When used in PPV polymerizations, TEMPO reacted with the diradical initiators irreversibly to form aldehyde-terminated compounds in the early stages of the reaction, which effectively lowered the monomer and dimer concentrations resulting in lower molecular weight PPVs as well as lower polymer yields.52,53 In addition, $\mathrm { C B r } _ { 4 }$ has demonstrated molecular weight control in PPV synthesis when using the sulfinyl and dithiocarbamate polymerization routes.54,55 $\mathrm { C B r } _ { 4 }$ functioned as a chain transfer agent during the polymerization by reacting with the growing polymer chain, and the resulting polymer contained brominated end-groups that were reacted further to form block copolymers.54,55 Like TEMPO and $\mathrm { C B r } _ { 4 }$ , nitrobenzene is also an effective radical inhibitor and has been used to control molecular weight in radical polymerizations.51,56-58 The inhibition mechanism of nitrobenzene differs from TEMPO because nitrobenzene can react through the aromatic ring or the nitro group.51 Also, TEMPO is a persistent radical that can terminate a free radical reaction by recombination whereas nitrobenzene is not a radical but forms a stable species upon reaction with free radicals. For example, in the radical polymerization of divinyl benzene with dimethyl $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ azobisisobutyrate, nitrobenzene lowered polymer molecular weight and was incorporated directly into the polymer chain, which showed that the molecule could potentially serve as a polymerization endcap.56 Considering the unique chemistry of nitrobenzene, the radical inhibitor could provide a new synthetic route to low-molecular weight $\left( \mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } } \right)$ PPVs.

In this work, BAM-PPV polymerization was performed in the presence of nitrobenzene to control polymer molecular weight. BAM-PPV was synthesized with nitrobenzene at different loading levels to determine the relationships between nitrobenzene loading and molecular weight $\left( M _ { w } \right)$ as well as polydispersity index (PDI). The uninhibited and nitrobenzene-inhibited reactions were also studied over time using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR. The results were compared to determine the potential mechanisms of inhibition by nitrobenzene. Lastly, high-molecular weight $\left( \mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } } \right)$ and low molecular weight $\left( \mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } } \right)$ BAM-PPV were isolated and characterized to determine if nitrobenzene was incorporated into the polymer as a repeat unit or endcap. Overall, this study shows the advantages and potential drawbacks of using nitrobenzene for BAM-PPV molecular weight control, which will allow polymer properties to be fine-tuned for desired applications.

# 2 EXPERIMENTAL

# 2.1 General

Anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) $( \geq 9 9 . 9 \%$ , inhibitorfree), anhydrous toluene $( 9 9 . 8 \% )$ , nitrobenzene $( \geq 9 9 \% )$ potassium tert-butoxide $( \geq 9 8 \% )$ and all nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) solvents $( \mathrm { C D C l } _ { 3 }$ , toluene$d _ { 6 } .$ THF- $d _ { 8 . }$ ) were purchased from Millapore-Sigma and used as received. Methanol (MeOH) was purchased from

Fisher Chemicals and used as received. The BAM-PPV monomer was synthesized as previously reported.8

# 2.2 |Synthesis of BAM-PPV

To a flamed-dried, round-bottomed flask, 2,5-bis (chloromethyl)- $. N 1 { , } N 4$ -dihexyl- $. N 1 { , } N 4$ -dimethylbenzene1,4-diaminium (1) $( 0 . 2 5 0 \mathrm { g } , 0 . 5 2 7 \mathrm { m m o l } )$ was added in a 1:1 THF/toluene mixture $( 8 . 3 4 ~ \mathrm { m l } ; 0 . 0 6 3 2 ~ \mathrm { M } )$ as a suspension. The solution was degassed and put under a nitrogen atmosphere then nitrobenzene was added at the desired loading $( 0 { - } 9 2 ~ \mathrm { m o l \% } )$ . The reaction vessel was cooled to $- 4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ using a dry ice-acetone bath, and the bath temperature was monitored with an external thermometer. Potassium tert-butoxide $\left( 0 . 4 8 0 \ { \mathrm { g } } , \ 4 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { m m o l } \right)$ was added as a solid to the reaction mixture. The reaction temperature was maintained between $- 4 0$ to $- 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ then the cooling bath was removed and the reaction proceeded overnight at room temperature $\left( \sim 2 0 \mathrm { h } \right)$ . The solution was precipitated in methanol to form an orange precipitate. The precipitated solid was isolated using vacuum filtration and then analyzed via SEC. The initial syntheses were performed to determine the general trend between nitrobenzene loading and molecular weight $\left( M _ { w } \right)$ , so further purification was not performed outside of the SEC sample preparation.

# 2.3 Size-exclusion chromatography

SEC was performed on a Viscotec TriSEC Model 302 GPC with a refractive index (RI) detector. Separation was accomplished using two in-series Varian PLgel $5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ Mixed-D 300 X $7 . 5 \mathrm { m m }$ GPC columns with a matching $5 0 \mathrm { ~ X ~ } 7 . 5$ guard column. A THF mobile phase flowing at $1 \mathrm { m l / m i n }$ was used. A nine-point calibration curve using polystyrene standards ranging from approximately 1000-480,000 molecular weight was used for analysis. Prior to injection, BAM-PPV was dissolved in THF with a small amount of toluene used as a flow rate marker and passed through a $0 . 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ PTFE filter. For the SEC studies performed over a $1 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ period, an aliquot $( 1 . 0 ~ \mathrm { m l } )$ of the reaction was quenched in MeOH $( 1 . 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m l } )$ , and the resulting solution was added to THF with standard and filtered through a $0 . 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ PTFE filter prior to analysis.

# 2.4| Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

LC-MS was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity highperformance LC system coupled to a 6120 Quadruple mass spectrometer with a sample volume of $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { l }$ . The instrument flow rate  was $0 . 2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m l / m i n }$ and a $1 5 \mathrm { c m } \mathrm { x } 2 . 0 \mathrm { m m } \mathrm { x } 4 \mu \mathrm { m }$ Phenomenex Synergy Fusion-RP 80A column was used for separation. The gradient mobile phase for the analysis was divided into stages as follows: first stage was $6 0 / 4 0 \ \mathrm { \ M e O H / }$ water (with $0 . 1 \%$ formic acid and $2 0 \%$ acetonitrile) for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , second stage was $5 0 / 5 0$ acetonitrile/MeOH (with $0 . 1 \%$ formic acid) for $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and third stage was $6 0 / 4 0 \ \mathrm { M e O H / }$ water (with $0 . 1 \%$ formic acid) for $1 5 \mathrm { m i n }$ . The analyzed samples were aliquots $( 1 . 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ m l } )$ from the polymerization reaction quenched in MeOH $( 1 . 0 \mathrm { m l } )$ at different times over a $1 0 \mathrm { { h } }$ period. The quenched solutions were analyzed without further purification.

# 2.5 Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

$^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectroscopy was performed on an AVANCE III ${ 5 0 0 } \mathrm { M H z }$ Bruker instrument. For the NMR kinetics studies, starting material 1 $( 2 7 { \mathrm { m g } } )$ was suspended in 1:1 THF- $d _ { g }$ :Toluene- $\cdot d _ { s }$ solution (volume: $0 . 9 \mathrm { \ m l }$ ) and submerged in liquid nitrogen. Upon reaching the freezing point of the solvent, potassium tert-butoxide $( 5 2 \mathrm { m g } )$ was added to the NMR-tube and subsequently heated to $- 4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ This procedure was repeated for the inhibited reaction except nitrobenzene $( 5 . 4 ~ \mu \mathrm { l } )$ was added to the suspension prior to freezing in liquid nitrogen. Spectra were recorded periodically over a $1 0 \mathrm { { h } }$ time period.

# 2.6| Spectroscopic analyses

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy samples were obtained using a Thermo Nicolet 6700 spectrometer equipped with the "SMART iTR" attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. The detector type was a liquid nitrogen cooled MCTA. The spectra are an average of 32 scans with $4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ resolution. Background subtraction (clean germanium crystal) and baseline corrections were used to decrease noise from the system.

# 3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

BAM-PPV was synthesized with nitrobenzene loadings between 0-0.92 equivalents $( 0 { - } 9 2 ~ \mathrm { m o l } ~ \% )$ compared to the premonomer (1), and the resulting polymers were isolated after precipitation in MeOH then filtration. The polymers were analyzed using SEC to determine polymer molecular weight $( M _ { \mathrm { w } } )$ as well as PDI. Figure 1 shows the relationships between nitrobenzene loading and $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ as well as PDI. The uninhibited reaction $0 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene) was performed multiple times, and $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ BAMPPV $( M _ { \mathrm { w } } > 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { k D a } )$ was isolated for each uninhibited reaction. However, the associated $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and PDI values in each uninhibited reaction were highly variable with the $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ values ranging between $1 3 0 - 2 0 5 \mathrm { k D a }$ and PDI values between $2 . 4 \ : - \ : 7 . 9$ The variability in the $M _ { w }$ and PDI values reflects the nature of radical chain polymerization, which offers little control over $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and PDI. At low nitrobenzene loadings $( < 0 . 9 0 \mathrm { m o l } \mathrm { \ } \% )$ , the radical inhibitor did not affect the polymerization reaction, and the $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and PDI values were similar to the uninhibited reaction. This result is highlighted in the SEC curves of Figure 1 considering the polymer peak positions are nearly the same at 0, 0.25, and $0 . 5 0 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene. In contrast, at nitrobenzene loadings $> 0 . 9 0 \ \mathrm { m o l } \%$ , significant decreases in $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ were observed with values $< 5 0 \mathrm { k D a }$ and typically lower than $1 0 \mathrm { \ k D a }$ at nitrobenzene loadings greater than $10 \mathrm { \ m o l } \%$ . The molecular weight distribution of the polymer also became more uniform with PDI values ranging from $1 . 3 \substack { - 2 . 6 }$ at nitrobenzene loadings ${ > } 1 2 \ \mathrm { m o l } \%$ . Polymerization even occurred at a nearly stoichiometric loading of nitrobenzene $( 9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \% )$ . In previous studies with TEMPO, 1.0 equivalent of inhibitor was required to prevent polymerization, and the proposed mechanism suggested that the inhibitor fully reacts with the dimeric, diradical initiator before chain growth can commence.26 In contrast, much higher loadings (8-12 equivalents) of $\mathrm { C B r } _ { 4 }$ were required to completely inhibit polymerization in previous studies, and chain growth was still observed with a degree of polymerization of ${ \sim } 2 5$ 54,55 In this study, the polymerization at $9 2 \ : \mathrm { m o l } \%$ loading demonstrates that nitrobenzene likely has a similar inhibition mechanism as TEMPO. Thus, nitrobenzene likely inhibits the reaction of the diradical initiator with the active monomer.

The SEC curves of the polymers formed at different nitrobenzene loadings are also shown in Figure 1. For the uninhibited reaction, three primary peaks are present: (1) a broad, $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ polymer peak, (2) a dimer peak $\left( M _ { \mathrm { w } } \sim 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { D a } \right)$ , and (3) a monomer peak $\mathrm { \Delta } ( M _ { \mathrm { w } } \sim 4 0 0 ~ \mathrm { D a } )$ PPV polymer peaks are typically broad due to the high PDI from radical chain growth polymerization. Dimer peaks are also common in PPV polymerizations due to the formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes from intramolecular cyclization of the dimer as the monomer concentration declines throughout the reaction.42,43 The monomer peak was also present in the SEC curve, which indicates that the monomer is not fully converted to polymer during the course of the uninhibited reaction. Gelation occurred during the uninhibited polymerization reaction, so the resulting increase in viscosity likely prevented full conversion of monomer to polymer. As mentioned above, the polymerizations performed with low nitrobenzene loadings $( < 0 . 9 \mathrm { m o l } \% )$ had similar SEC curves as the uninhibited reaction. Noticeable shifts in the polymer peak position were observed at higher nitrobenzene loadings $( > 0 . 9 \mathrm { ~ m o l ~ } \% )$ to lower $M _ { \mathrm { w } }$ In addition, the heights of the oligomer and monomer peaks began to increase relative to the polymer peak height with increased nitrobenzene loading. This result supports the hypothesis that radical inhibitors like nitrobenzene inhibit low molecular weight radical species before polymerization can commence.26 For the reaction performed with $9 2 \ : \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene loading, multiple low molecular weight peaks were observed as shown by the three low molecular weight peaks in Figure 1. This pattern was also observed in the TEMPO inhibition study on PPV polymerization by Wiesecke.26 In this study, the peaks likely show byproducts formed via reaction of nitrobenzene with the dimer radical as well as the formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes. Further experimentation was performed to determine the specific inhibition mechanism of nitrobenzene on BAM-PPV polymerization.

SEC was also performed on aliquots from the uninhibited reaction and inhibited reaction ( $9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene) at intervals over $^ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ to provide insight into the inhibition mechanism of nitrobenzene. The SEC results are shown in Figure 2. For both the uninhibited and inhibited reactions, three peaks were observed that represent polymer (P3a and P3b), dimer (P2a and P2b), and monomer (P1a and P1b). In both reactions, the polymer peaks were observed only after warming to room temperature. During the initial $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at low temperature, the monomer peaks (P1a and P1b) decrease, and the dimer peaks (P2a and P2b) have a subsequent increase, which is consistent with dimerization of the active monomer to form a diradical initiator. The pathways of the uninhibited and inhibited reactions diverge after the reaction warms to room temperature. In the uninhibited reaction, the monomer and dimer peak heights (P1a and P2a) drop significantly and a polymer peak (P3a) emerges at $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ reaction time. P3a continues to grow over the $1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ period indicating further polymerization whereas P1a begins to flatten completely over the course of the uninhibited reaction. A small amount of dimer remained throughout the uninhibited reaction, which likely represents the formation of [2.2] paracyclophanes from the dimer initiators as the monomer concentration decreases.42,43

In contrast, for the nitrobenzene-inhibited reaction, the monomer peak continues to decrease after the reaction warms to room temperature but at a slower rate than the uninhibited reaction. P1b was still relatively large after $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 3 h }$ and remained throughout the $1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ reaction period. As shown by Schonbein et al., activation of premonomer and dimerization of the active monomer are both relatively slow reaction steps compared to the chain growth polymerization step.42 Therefore, at minimum, addition of nitrobenzene can slow down the consumption of monomer during the initial reaction stages by diluting the monomer concentration relative to the uninhibited reaction. The dimer peak (P2b) increases throughout the $1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ reaction period in the presence of nitrobenzene. The increasing dimer content could be due to nitrobenzene reacting with the diradical initiator upon formation to prevent polymerization. Alternatively, nitrobenzene could be acting as a reaction diluent and thus promoting formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes after dimer formation instead of chain growth polymerization. In the TEMPO-mediated PPV study, polymer and [2.2]paracyclophane formation did not occur until TEMPO was completely consumed because the dimer initiator reacted rapidly with the inhibitor after formation26 Thus, TEMPO clearly acts as a radical scavenger and not a reaction diluent in the PPV polymerization. However, the role of nitrobenzene in the BAM-PPV polymerization is still unclear because the increasing dimer content may consist of terminated dimer species after reaction with nitrobenzene or [2.2]paracyclophanes. Although nitrobenzene clearly inhibits the polymerization reaction after formation of the dimer, polymers are still able to form as shown by the growth of P3b. Overall, the increase in the dimer peak implies that nitrobenzene inhibits the reaction before chain polymerization begins, but the exact mechanism of inhibition requires further analysis on the small molecules formed during the inhibited reaction.

LC-MS was performed to analyze the small molecules formed during the uninhibited and inhibited reactions $( 9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene). Electrospray ionization (ESI) was used as the ionization technique. Because proton and solvent adducts form in LC-ms,59,0 the starting monomer was analyzed first to determine potential adducts of the BAM-PPV premonomer. The chromatogram and corresponding mass spectra of the premonomer are shown in the Supporting Information (Figures S1-S2). The monomer adducts elute between $1 . 5 \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 3 . 0 \mathrm { ~ m i n }$ and show a broad LC peak. The corresponding MS shows peaks with $\mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 3 9 3 . 4$ and $\mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 1 9 7 . 0$ , which represent the mono-protonated $[ \mathbf { M } + \mathbf { H } ^ { + } ]$ and di-protonated $\mathrm { [ M + 2 H ^ { + } }$ or M/2] forms of the monomer after methoxysubstitution of the chlorides, respectively. Isotopic chlorine peaks ( $^ { \cdot 3 5 } \mathrm { C l }$ and $^ { 3 7 } \mathrm { C l }$ ) were also not observed, which provides further evidence that the chlorines were displaced during the analysis. Thus, the benzyl chlorides in the reactions are likely displaced by methoxy-groups after quenching in MeOH or during LC-MS analysis, and the observed adducts will likely be mono-protonated or diprotonated forms of the methoxy-substituted anilines.

LC-MS on the uninhibited and inhibited reactions showed the formation of multiple dimer-type compounds throughout the reactions, and the LC chromatograms for both reactions over a $1 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ period are shown in Figure 3. In the uninhibited reaction, two large peaks eluted at times ranging from 1.5 to $4 . 5 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and four small peaks eluted between $3 0  { \mathrm { ~ - ~ } } 6 0  { \mathrm { \ m i n } }$ . The large peak eluting at ${ \sim } 4$ min began to decrease substantially after the reaction was warmed to room temperature. The large peak eluting between $1 . 5 \textrm { - } 3 . 5 \ \operatorname* { m i n }$ did not change over the course of the reaction, which was surprising considering the rapid decrease of the monomer peak in the SEC studies. The disparity between the LC-MS and SEC monomer contents is likely due to differences in the sensitivity of small molecule detection in each method. The mass spectra (Figures S3-s4) for the two large peaks between $1 . 5 ~ \textrm { -- }$ $4 . 5 \ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ both showed methoxy-substituted monomeric compounds with $\mathrm { m / z } ~ = ~ 3 9 3 . 4$ . The corresponding diprotonated form $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 1 9 7 )$ of the premonomer was absent from the mass spectra likely due to the basic conditions of the reaction. The four small peaks were only observable during the low temperature incubation period and had elution times of 34.0, 37.0, 44.0, and $5 3 . 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ These peaks disappeared after the reaction warmed to room temperature. Based off the corresponding mass spectra (Figures S5-s8), the four small LC peaks correlated with dimeric compounds having $\mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z }$ ratios between 723 and 769 and typically showed the corresponding [M $+ 2 \mathrm { H } ^ { + } ]$ peaks at 361-384. The $[ \mathrm { M } + 2 \mathrm { H } ^ { + } ]$ peaks were likely observable for the dimeric compounds but not the monomeric compounds because the dimeric compounds contain four basic amino groups versus only two for the monomers. The LC peak eluting at $5 3 \mathrm { m i n }$ was significant because the peak grew within the first $0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of the reaction then disappeared rapidly after the reaction warmed to room temperature. The mass spectrum of this peak showed two primary peaks $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 7 2 2$ and 361), which is an excellent match for the proposed dimer initiator with benzyl methoxy-substituents instead of chlorides. Considering the four small peaks eluting between 30 and $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ all disappeared after the reaction warmed, these results suggest that all the corresponding dimer compounds are involved in the BAM-PPV polymerization reaction likely as polymerization initiators. Also of note for the uninhibited reaction, the concentrations of the dimers are fairly low considering the eluted peaks at 1.5-4.5 min saturated the detector. To prevent saturation of the detector, LC-MS was also performed on more dilute samples, but the four small peaks were no longer observable after dilution further highlighting their low concentration. The low concentrations of the dimers in the uninhibited reaction is consistent with past PPV studies considering the propagation step is fast.42

LC-MS on the nitrobenzene-inhibited reaction $9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene) showed some similarities to the uninhibited reaction but also significant differences. Similar to the uninhibited reaction, a persistent monomer peak eluting between $1 . 5 \textrm { - } 3 . 5 \ \operatorname* { m i n }$ was observed in the chromatogram. The corresponding mass spectrum (Figure S9) confirmed the presence of protonated monomer adducts. In the inhibited reaction, an additional peak eluting at ${ \sim } 4$ min was not observed, but the LC monomer peak shoulder centered at ${ \sim } 2 . 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ does begin to disappear after warming the reaction to room temperature. Therefore, the monomer reactivity appears to be similar between the two reactions based on LC-MS, and the observed differences in the chromatograms between 1.5 - $4 . 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ are likely due to differences in sample concentrations and saturation of the detector. During the $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ lowtemperature incubation period, a peak eluting at $5 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ began to emerge and spiked after $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The corresponding mass spectrum (Figure S10) showed two molecular ion peaks $( { \bf m } / { \bf z } = 7 2 2$ and 785) along with the doubly protonated adduct peaks $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 3 6 1 $ and 392) indicating dimer compounds. Because the LC peak eluting at $5 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ spikes at 3 h reaction time then disappears, the corresponding dimer compounds are likely reaction initiators, and the result shows that multiple reactive dimers may be involved in the inhibited reaction. One dimer compound $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 7 2 2 )$ was observed in the uninhibited reaction as well and thus likely represents the same dimer initiator in both reactions. However, the other dimer compound $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 7 8 5 )$ was only present in the inhibited reaction. A structure for the latter dimer could not be determined or proposed, but the compound's disappearance as the reaction proceeds suggests that the compound is a reaction intermediate.

Furthermore, LC-MS studies on the nitrobenzeneinhibited reaction showed several dimeric compounds that formed and persisted throughout the reaction period. First, an LC peak eluting at $5 2 \mathrm { m i n }$ was observed in the initial chromatogram and grew throughout the reaction period. The mass spectrum (Figure S12) of the LC peak had two molecular ion peaks $( [ \mathbf { M } + \mathbf { H } ^ { + } ]$ peaks at $\mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 6 9 2$ and 722 as well as the corresponding [M $+ 2 \mathrm { H } ^ { + } ]$ peaks), which correlate well with the molecular weights for a chloro-[2.2]paracycloph-ene structure and the methoxy-substituted form of the dimer initiator. However, no isotopic chlorine pattern was observed for the potential chloro-[2.2]paracycloph-ene compound in the mass spectrum. In addition, an LC peak eluting at $4 6 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ formed after $3 \ \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ reaction time and remained throughout the reaction period. The mass spectrum (Figure S11) showed two prominent peaks $( \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { z } = 6 5 9$ and 329), which correlate to the $[ \mathbf { M } + \mathbf { H } ^ { + } ]$ and [M $+ 2 \mathrm { H } ^ { + } ]$ adducts of a [2.2]paracyclophane after elimination of both chlorides. The formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes suggests that nitrobenzene inhibits the chain propagation reaction between the active monomer and diradical initiator but does not react directly with the initiator irreversibly like TEMPO. As mentioned above and in previous studies, [2.2]paracyclophane formation typically results from a low active monomer concentration because the intramolecular cyclization of the diradical initiator becomes favorable under these conditions (Scheme 2).42,43 No aldehyde or nitrobenzenecapped dimers formed based on the LC-MS results, which further shows nitrobenzene does not react directly with the diradical to irreversibly deactivate the initiator.

The formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes during the inhibited reaction warrants further discussion. In previous studies, the dimer initiator formed exclusively via tail-to-tail addition of the $p$ -quinodimethane methylene groups to form the chloro-stabilized benzyl diradical. 39,43 The diradical forms a [2.2]paracyclophane via an intramolecular cyclization reaction resulting in both halide substituents on the same ethylene bridge.43 The monohalogenated [2.2]paracycloph-ene was observed and formed after dehydrohalogenation of the di-halogenated [2.2]paracyclophane. 43 The doubly dehydrohalogenated [2.2]paracyclophane product was not observed in past work. However, the previous studies focused on alkoxysubstituted 1,4-bis(halomethylene)benzene monomers rather than amino-substituted monomers. 43 The aminosubstituent is a better electron donor than the alkoxysubstituent and thus may promote the second dehydrohalogenation by donating electron density into the $\pi$ system to eliminate the nearby halide, which is similar to mechanisms that have been proposed for elimination in $\gamma$ -amino alkyl halides.61 This mechanism is likely implausible for [2.2]paracyclophanes formed via tail-to-tail combinations of the active monomer because dehydrohalogenation of a vinyl chloride to form a strained alkyne is unlikely. In contrast, if the head-to-tail reaction could occur to form intermediate A (Scheme 3), the resulting di-halogenated [2.2]paracyclophane could form the cyclic diene product. The LC-MS results for the uninhibited reaction showed multiple dimer peaks form in the early stages of the reaction, which implies multiple dimer initiator isomers were present and formation of the head-to-tail diradical cannot be ruled out for aminosubstituted precursors. Thus, the formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes from amino-substituted monomers should be examined in more detail in future studies and would complement the existing studies on alkoxy-substituted monomers.43

$^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR was also performed on the uninhibited and nitrobenzene-inhibited reactions over a $1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ period to gain additional mechanistic insight, and the spectra are shown in Figure 4. For both reactions, the premonomer peaks were present at $7 . 3 ~ \mathrm { \ p p m }$ (aromatic peak) and $4 . 8 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ (benzyl chloride peak). During the initial $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at low temperature, the $p$ -quinodimethane active monomer was present in both reactions as shown by the five proton peaks between 6.6 and 5.2 ppm then disappeared after warming to room temperature. After $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , BAM-PPV formation was observed during the uninhibited reaction as evidenced by the small broad aromatic peaks at 7.6 and $7 . 8 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ . The premonomer disappeared completely in the uninhibited reaction after $^ { 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . At $^ { 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , a broad vinyl peak formed at ${ \sim } 5 . 6 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ in the uninhibited reaction, which is typically not observed in pure BAM-PPv. All peaks in the uninhibited reaction were broad after $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ because the reaction had gelled, which was observed visually upon removal from the NMR. The identification of the compound corresponding to the vinyl peak could not be determined due to the gel formation, but the vinyl peak did not correlate to BAM-PPV based on previous work.

The nitrobenzene-inhibited reaction was much slower than the uninhibited reaction. The premonomer as well as nitrobenzene remained throughout the reaction period, and the polymerization was difficult to observe based off formation of the aromatic peaks at 7.6 and 7.8 ppm likely because the polymer concentration is low compared to the uninhibited reaction. The highpremonomer concentration throughout the reaction suggests that nitrobenzene also inhibits formation of the active monomer from the premonomer. The relatively small size of the $p$ -quinodimethane peaks compared to the premonomer peaks in the early part of the inhibited reaction supports this hypothesis. Based on previous mechanistic studies, the dehydrohalogenation  of premonomer to form active monomer is about twice as fast as the rate-limiting dimerization step to form the diradical initiator.4 Considering these two steps have similar rate constants, the high-nitrobenzene concentration likely affects the rate of active monomer formation by diluting the reactant concentrations in the initial step. As a result, the high-nitrobenzene loading $( 9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ ) initially serves as a reaction diluent to prevent formation of active monomer.

In both reactions, vinyl peaks formed between 6.5 and $5 . 5 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ , which are likely indicative of [2.2]paracyclophane formation as the monomer concentration decreases. The positions of the vinyl peaks differ for the uninhibited and inhibited reactions. The vinyl peak in the uninhibited reaction was located at $5 . 6 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ and was broad due to gelation of the sample. For the nitrobenzene-inhibited reaction, the vinyl peaks were located at $6 . 1 \ \mathrm { p p m }$ and $5 . 9 \ \mathrm { p p m }$ . The differences in the vinyl peak locations could be attributed to the different types of [2.2]paracyclophanes formed. In the LC-MS results for the inhibited reaction, chloro[2.2]paracyclophene and the doubly dehydrohalogenated [2.2]paracyclophane product were observed, which showed that [2.2]paracyclophane formation is not only more prevalent in the inhibited reaction but also more likely to undergo elimination of both chloride groups. The formation of [2.2]paracyclophanes is consistent with previous Studies,3,43 but typically, both chlorides are not eliminated from the dimer. As discussed in the LC-MS results, the presence of the amino functionality may better promote elimination from the [2.2]paracyclophanes than the alkoxy functionality in previous studies.

As a radical inhibitor, nitrobenzene can react with active radicals through the aromatic ring or the nitro group.51 One proposed mechanism of nitrobenzene inhibition is reaction of a radical with the nitro group followed by elimination of nitrosobenzene, which can undergo further reaction to create a carbonyl terminus on the end of the growing chain.51 Thus, the NMR region between 9.5 and 12.0 ppm was compared for the uninhibited and inhibited reactions to check for the presence of aldehyde and carboxylic acid functional groups. For both reactions, a small aldehyde peak was observed at $1 0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ (Figure S13), but the peak was not significantly larger for the inhibited reaction over the uninhibited reaction. The aldehyde functional group also forms when oxygen is present in the reaction, and past studies show the aldehyde peak can form due to trace amounts of oxygen.62,63 As a result, the peak shown at $1 0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { p p m }$ in both reactions is likely due to residual oxygen present in the reaction vessel, and the nitrobenzene does not form terminal carbonyl endcaps.

After the preliminary mechanistic studies, $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ BAM-PPV polymers were isolated after precipitation, filtration, and purification via extraction. The $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra for $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ were nearly identical (Figure S14), and the spectra were also identical to the previously reported spectra. For the inhibited polymerization product, the absence of additional peaks in the NMR spectra further demonstrates that nitrobenzene does not chemically modify BAM-PPV during chain propagation but instead inhibits the reaction of the dimer initiator and active monomer. In addition, FTIR analyses were performed on $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ (Figure 5). $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ did not show nitro functional group peaks, and the spectra of the $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ were similar. Therefore, the FTIR results further indicate that nitrobenzene was not incorporated directly into the polymer chain through the aromatic ring, which eliminates another mechanistic possibility.

The mechanism of nitrobenzene inhibition is still not fully understood with the current experimental results, but a few mechanistic possibilities could be eliminated. The SEC and LC-MS results clearly show that nitrobenzene inhibits the reaction after formation of the diradical initiator, so a viable mechanism will likely involve the dimer species interacting with nitrobenzene (Scheme 4). Typically, nitrobenzene is expected to react with a propagating chain irreversibly through the aromatic ring to form an intermediate radical, which becomes a nonradical nitrobenzene endcap after hydrogen abstraction to restore aromaticity.51 This pathway was not observed based on the absence of nitrobenzene endcapped dimers in the LC-MS and nitro groups in the FTIR. Alternatively, nitrobenzene could react with a propagating radical polymer directly through the nitro group to form an intermediate radical, which can propagate further resulting in the nitrobenzene unit becoming incorporated directly into the polymer chain.51 This pathway would not irreversibly terminate the dimer species as was shown in the SEC results, and additional aromatic peaks would be expected in the $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR but were not observed. Therefore, this pathway is also not feasible. Instead of chain propagation, the radical intermediate formed through the nitro group can eliminate nitrosobenzene,1 which would leave an oxygen radical endcap that can react further through chain propagation, recombination, or form a carbonyl after chlorine extraction. However, the SEC results do not support further chain propagation or recombination after reaction of the dimer with nitrobenzene, and the $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR and FTIR spectra do not show formation of carbonyls in $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$

In contrast, the experimental results show that nitrobenzene likely plays a role as a reaction diluent and also interacts with the diradical initiator to prevent chain propagation. The SEC results show that nitrobenzene slows the formation of the active monomer from premonomer as well as formation of diradical initiator, which is likely due to a concentration effect from dilution of the premonomer with increased nitrobenzene loading. Previous work has shown that formation of the active monomer as well as the diradical intermediate are slow steps in PPV polymerization and thus would be sensitive to reactant concentration.42 In addition, the SEC and LC-MS results show increased formation of dimer species throughout the inhibited reaction, and the LC-MS results provide evidence of [2.2]paracyclophane formation, which typically form when the active monomer concentration is low relative to the initiator concentration.42,43 Nitrobenzene is able to inhibit the chain propagation step even at low loadings (between 1 and $1 0 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ , which suggests nitrobenzene also has a chemical role rather than acting solely as a reaction diluent. Therefore, nitrobenzene likely reacts with the diradical intermediate in competition with the active monomer to form a stabilized diradical species that cannot participate in the chain propagation reaction (Scheme 4). The resulting reaction would be reversible because no nitrobenzene-capped dimers were observed in LC-MS, so the diradical initiator would be in equilibrium with a nitrobenzene-modified dimer intermediate. As discussed previously, the dimer initiator can react with the nitro group or aromatic ring of nitrobenzene,51 but considering reaction with the aromatic ring would disrupt aromaticity, reaction with the nitro group is proposed in Scheme 4. However, if the diradical initiator did react with the aromatic ring of nitrobenzene, reformation of the aromatic system would provide a strong driving force for reversibility. Upon reformation of the diradical initiator and nitrobenzene, the dimer can react irreversibly with the active monomer via chain propagation, cyclize irreversibly to form [2.2]paracyclophanes, or react reversibly with nitrobenzene again. Based on the SEC and LCMS results for the reaction at $9 2 \mathrm { m o l } \%$ nitrobenzene loading, [2.2]paracyclophane formation becomes more favorable than chain propagation with high-nitrobenzene content. Thus, for the proposed inhibition mechanism, the product distribution would be dependent on the relative concentrations of the premonomer and nitrobenzene inhibitor, which will impact the chemical equilibria of the reversible reaction and ultimately the distribution of the irreversible products (PPV polymer or [2.2] paracyclophanes).

# 4 CONCLUSION

The polymerization of BAM-PPV can be modulated using nitrobenzene as a reaction additive. The inhibitor lowers the molecular weight and PDI of the conjugated polymer at nitrobenzene loadings as low as $0 . 9 \mathrm { \ m o l } \%$ . Mechanistically, nitrobenzene appears to inhibit the reaction after formation of the dimeric diradical initiator by preventing chain growth with the active monomer. Specifically, for the nitrobenzene-inhibited reaction, SEC studies showed an increase in dimer formation as the reaction progressed, and LC-MS confirmed that the dimer products were  [2.2]paracyclophanes  and  dehydrohalogenated derivatives of these compounds. The formation of [2.2] paracyclophanes shows that the intramolecular cyclization reaction becomes more favorable than chain growth in the presence of nitrobenzene (especially as the inhibitor loading is increased), which typically occurs in PPV polymerizations when the active monomer concentration is low. The exact mechanism of inhibition by nitrobenzene is not known because LC-MS, $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR, and FTIR did not show any chemical modifications of the polymers and dimers during the reaction or after isolation of products. During $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR analysis, formation of a small aldehyde peak was observed in both the uninhibited and inhibited reactions, which suggested the aldehyde formed due to reaction with residual oxygen and not via reaction with nitrobenzene. Also, FTIR spectra of the isolated $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ polymers did not show any significant differences, and specifically, the $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ did not show evidence of a nitro functional group, which shows nitrobenzene was not incorporated into the polymer backbone. Overall, nitrobenzene inhibits BAM-PPV polymerization after formation of the diradical initiator, which is expected for a radical inhibitor, but there is no evidence that the compound reacts irreversibly with the polymer or reaction intermediates. Future work will focus on the use of $\mathrm { H M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and $\mathrm { L M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ BAM-PPV as a corrosion barrier and other applications. These findings could be useful for controlling the molecular weight of BAM-PPV as well as other PPVs, and further studies on final polymer properties would be beneficial for future applications.
