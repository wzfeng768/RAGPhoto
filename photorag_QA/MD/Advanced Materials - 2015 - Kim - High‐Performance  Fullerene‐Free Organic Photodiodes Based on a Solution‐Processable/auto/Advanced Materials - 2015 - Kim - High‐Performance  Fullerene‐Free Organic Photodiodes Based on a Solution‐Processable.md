# High-Performance, Fullerene-Free Organic Photodiodes Based on a Solution-Processable Indigo

IIl Ku Kim, Xin Li, Mujeeb Ullah, Paul E. Shaw, Robert Wawrzinek, Ebinazar B. Namdas,\* and Shih-Chun Lo\*

Organic photodetectors (OPDs) are attracting attention for potential applications such as digital photography, medical sensing, artificial eyesight, and optical communication.1-9] Key advantages of using organic semiconductors as the photoactive medium are low embedded energy manufacturing, the ability to fabricate large-area detectors, and mechanical flexibility,[10,11] which are typically difficult to achieve with conventional inorganic semiconductors. Furthermore, OPDs can be designed to either have a broadband or narrowband response.l2,3,5,6,10]

The primary function of an OPD is light detection. Hence, to attain the required detectivity for real world applications, it is critical for OPDs to exhibit low dark current (i.e., the current measured in the device without light illumination) and high external quantum efficiency (EQE).[1,2,11,12] Given exciton binding energy, a necessary strategy in achieving a high EQE is to use an active layer consisting of a blend of electron donating (p-type) and accepting (n-type) organic semiconductors-a bulk heterojunction (BHJ) layer. At present, fullerenes have been the predominant acceptor utilized in the BHJ layer of the best performing OPDs.2,3,6,8,10,11] Fullerenes have also been found to enable reduction of OPD dark current. For example, introduction of a thin evaporated layer of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ as a hole-blocking material between the active BHJ layer and the cathode has been shown as an effective approach to decrease the dark current.3,8,10] However, solution processable fullerene derivatives commonly used in the BHJ layer have disadvantages, such as relatively high cost in synthesis and purification,[13,14] low molar extinction coefficient in the visible region for broadband photodiodes, low air-stability and a broad absorption wavelength range that makes it difficult to be used in narrowband OPDs.

Recently, one of the families of original natural dyes and pigments, indigo, was found to exhibit balanced electron and hole mobilities of $1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in transistors when films were prepared by vacuum deposition. Indigo films were also found to exhibit excellent stability against degradation in air.[15-20]

Solution processing of simple indigos is, however, not possible due to their poor solubility in common organic solvents, arising from the strong hydrogen bonding.[21,22] Only a few examples of solution processable indigo derivatives had been used for optoelectronics.[23] An elegant method for depositing indigo films from solution was recently reported by Glowacki et al., who developed di-N-protected indigos, which can be processed into neat thin films as well as blended films with a semiconducting polymer.[18] However, a required thermal deprotection step was used in their work and organic photovoltaic devices prepared with poly(3-n-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) as the donor only showed relatively poor performance.

Here, we report a strategy for fullerene-free OPDs using a solution processable indigo as the electron acceptor blended with P3HT donor. We show that attachment of a 2-ethylhexyloxyphenyl group to $6 { , } 6 ^ { \prime }$ -dibromoindigo provides excellent solubility and electron affinity to act as a solution processable acceptor. To avoid the use of a moisture sensitive/low work function cathode such as calcium or samarium that was generally employed in the best performing OPDs based on fullerene,3,9,24] we fabricated the indigo OPDs in an inverted device architecture. The OPDs showed an almost spectrally fat response between 350 to $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ with a high responsivity $( 0 . 4 \mathrm { ~ A ~ W ~ } ^ { - 1 } )$ and a high detectivity $( 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones). These values are comparable to those of the best fullerene based OPDs and silicon-based photodiodes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first high performance and fully solution processed fullerene-free broadband OPD.

Scheme 1 illustrates how the mono-N-arylated $6 , 6 ^ { \prime }$ -dibromoindigo 3 was synthesized from $6 , 6 ^ { \prime }$ dibromoindigo 1 under Ullmann-Goldberg coupling conditions. By simply heating 1 with 1-(2-ethylhexyloxy)-4-iodobenzene,[25] caesium carbonate, copper iodide and 1,1,1-tris(hydroxymethyl)ethane in dimethylsulfoxide for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ ,[26] 3 was formed as a dark blue solid in a $54 \%$ yield (see the Supporting Information for the details).

Unlike indigo or the dibromoindigo 1, 3 was found to be soluble in common organic solvents and good-quality thin films of 3 could be prepared by spin-coating. This can be attributed to the mono-arylation, which provides good solubility due to the nonsymmetrical structure motif and the branched long alkyl surface group. It also maintains the ability to form hydrogen bonding in 3, allowing for good intermolecular interaction. Thermal gravimetric analysis (Figure S3, Supporting Information) showed that 3 has good thermal stability with a decomposition temperature, corresponding to a $5 \%$ weight loss at $2 9 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ 3 was also found to have a glass transition temperature at $7 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and crystallization at $1 5 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ as well as two melting peaks at 200 and $2 2 9 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ likely arising from polymorphism (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

Given that 3 was to be used as an electron acceptor in an OPD device, we next investigated its electrochemical properties using cyclic voltammetry. However, 3 did not show fully reversible redox waves similar to its parent chromophore[16] (Figure S5, Supporting Information). We hence used photoelectron spectroscopy under air (PESA) to determine its ionization energy (IP). The IP of 3 measured as a neat film was $5 . 7 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and to estimate the electron affinity, we added the optical gap to the IP. The optical gap was calculated from the intersection of solid-state absorption and emission spectra (Figure S6, Supporting Information) and was determined to be $1 . 8 \mathrm { e V } .$ Hence, the electron affinity was estimated to be $- 3 . 9 \mathrm { e V } ,$ (Figure 1b), which is similar to that reported for $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 0 } \mathrm { B M }$ [30] indicating that 3 in principle is suitable to act as an electron acceptor with donor materials such as P3HT.

To confirm that indigo 3 could act as an electron acceptor, we measured film photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) of P3HT as a function of the acceptor concentrations of 3 (see Table S1, Supporting Information). It was found that at $1 \ \mathrm { w t } \%$ of 3, around $80 \%$ of the P3HT luminescence was quenched. A similar level of quenching was observed when $5 0 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ of 3 was used. Thus the electron affinity of 3 was suitable for being an electron acceptor in an OPD.

It was found that while neat films of 3 did not show transistor characteristics in the OFET mode, it had SCLC mobilities of $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ and $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for holes and electrons, respectively (Figure S7, Supporting Information). In the final part of the study, we fabricated OPDs in an inverted structure consisting of ITO/PEIE/active layer $\left. \mathrm { \Delta } \right. \mathrm { \Omega } ^ { \mathrm { \tiny { M o O } } _ { 3 } / }$ Ag (where PEIE is ethoxylated polyethylenimine, a work function modification layer;[31] $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ is molybdenum oxide, a hole extraction layer) as shown in Figure 1c. The inverted OPD structure offers advantages for integration with CMOs technology for pixel design[] as well as avoiding the usage of moisture sensitive cathodes such as calcium or samarium generally used for the best fullerene based OPDs.3,9,24] The spin-coated active layer consisted of a 1:1 (by weight) blend of P3HT:3 and it had a thickness of $5 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . Figure 1c shows the current density-voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ curve measured in the dark and it can be seen that the dark current density was $9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 9 }$ and $2 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 8 } \mathrm { A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ at $- 1 . 0$ and $- 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively. The dark current of the OPDs is at least as good as the state-of-the-art fullerene based OPDs (Table S6, Supporting Information). It should be noted that this remarkably low dark current was achieved without the use of hole or electron blocking layers.2,3,8,9,11]

The low dark current under reverse bias suggests that there is an intrinsic large barrier for electron and hole injection from the opposite electrode to the active layer. As illustrated in Figure 1b, the hole and electron injection barriers were significant: a $1 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ offset between the work function of ITO/PEIE and the IP of P3HT; and a $1 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for electron injection barrier between the $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { A g }$ electrode and the electron affinity of 3. We note that a thin PEIE layer has been reported to lower the work function of ITO by $\approx 1 ~ \mathrm { e V } . ^ { [ 3 1 ] }$ To gain further insight into the properties of the OPD, we measured the dark current as a function of temperature (Figure 1d and Figure S11 for $J { - } V$ curves in the Supporting Information). The experimental data show very week temperature dependence of the dark current. The activation energies $( E _ { \mathrm { a } } )$ of 0.07 and $0 . 0 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ were determined from Arrhenius plots of dark currents at $- 1 . 0$ and $- 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively. These values are much lower than the injection barrier for electrons and holes from the opposite electrodes. This means that the charge injection is temperature independent and happens via leakage through a tunneling process.30] After tunneling, the charge transport is governed by a thermally activated process. At a higher bias $( - 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } )$ , the activation energy reduces whereas the dark current increases. This can be explained by large band bending, which facilitates efficient electron and hole injection via field-assisted tunneling.

Figure 2a shows the EQE characteristics of the OPDs under negative bias. The photoresponse of the device was almost spectrally flat between 350 and $6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , which can be attributed to the thick $( 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ junction layer, where the optical density moves beyond the Beer-Lambert law limit.9] By increasing the reverse bias, the EQE systematically increased, reaching up to around $80 \%$ at $- 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } .$ Control devices (without indigo 3) using neat P3HT (i.e., $\mathrm { I T O / P E I E / P 3 H T / M o O _ { 3 } / A g ) }$ were also fabricated but only showed low photocurrents $( \approx 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \mathrm { ~ A ~ c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ and poor EQE $( < 1 . 5 \%$ at $- 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } ,$ see Figure S16 and Table S4, Supporting Information), indicating that 3 is responsible for the obtained high device performance.

The response speed of a photodiode is strongly related to charge transport and collection. The transient time of free carriers can be controlled by the thickness of the photoactive layer, applied bias and the choice of interfacial charge transport materials. In OPDs of this work the measured frequency response is suitable for conventional imaging applications such as mobile phone image sensors, which have frame rates of $3 0 \ \mathrm { H z }$ [32]

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a high performance fullerene-free OPD using a new solution processable indigo acceptor, which can be obtained via a straightforward synthetic route. An inverted device structure gave a high EQE, nearly spectrally flat response between 350 and $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , high responsivity (0.4 A w-1), and high detectivity $( 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ Jones), which are comparable to stateof-the-art fullerene based organic and silicon photodiodes. Such indigo-based OPDs can be further improved by changing the donor material or by manipulating the indigo structure. The approach presented shows the potential for new materials that are cost-effective and CMOS compatible for OPD applications.
