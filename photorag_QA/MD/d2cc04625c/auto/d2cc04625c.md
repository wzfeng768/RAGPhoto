# 2-Styrylthiophene-based donor-acceptor linear conjugated polymers with tunable cyano substituents are atom-economically obtained via direct $\mathtt { c - H }$ arylation for platinum-free photocatalytic hydrogen production, affording a HER of up to 9.79 mmol $\mathfrak { h } ^ { - 1 } \mathfrak { g } ^ { - 1 }$

Since Fujishima et al. discovered hydrogen evolution from illuminated semiconducting $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ in the presence of water,' photocatalytic hydrogen production (PHP) has been considered one of the most promising technologies to produce green energy in a cheap and inexhaustible way. In recent years, $\pi$ -conjugated polymers (CPs), as a kind of soft semiconducting material consisting of alternating single and double bonds with overlapping p orbitals and a delocalized $\pi$ -electron system, have attracted ever-growing attention for photocatalysis due to their broad tuneability in terms of structure, specific surface area, functionalisation, optoelectronic properties, etc.2-4 So far, many kinds of $\pi$ conjugated materials have been explored for PHP, such as graphitic carbon nitride $( \mathrm { g } { \cdot } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ) , ^ { 5 }$ conjugated porous polymers, covalent organic frameworks, linear conjugate polymers,1,11 and polymer heterojunctions.12

The photocatalytic activity of CPs can be affected by many factors, including frontier molecular orbital (FMO) levels, light absorptance, charge transport, building block conformations, morphology, particle sizes, etc. The introduction of electron donor-acceptor (D-A) architecture into CPs has been demonstrated as a promising strategy to develop high-performance photocatalysts owing to their widely tunable bandgaps, high charge carrier mobility, and enhanced intramolecular charge transfer (ICT). Cyano (-CN), as a simple yet strong electron-withdrawing group, has been widely incorporated into polymeric semiconductors for diverse applications, including organic photovoltaics (OPvs),13 organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs),14 and organic field-effect transistors (OFETs).15 The unique inter- and intramolecular interactions stemming from the cooperation of electrondeficient -CN and other functional groups also provides attractive properties of cyano-containing CPs and conjugated molecules for photocatalytic applications.16,17 The approaches to introduce CN into CPs typically require rigorous reaction conditions, such as the use of. lithium diisopropylamide,18 organotin,19 and high temperature.20 The establishment of an efficient and atom-economic strategy to integrate a -CN group into CPs and systematic structure-propertyperformance correlation studies will thus be highly desirable.

Herein, a series of 2-styrylthiophene (sTp)-based linear CPs, i.e., CP1, CP2, CP3 and CP4, were facilely synthesized, respectively, from (E)-2-(4-bromostyryl)thiophene (M1), (Z)-2-(4-bromophenyl)-3-(thiophen-2-yl)acrylonitrile (m2), (E)-3-(4-bromophenyl)-2-(thiophen-2-yl)acrylonitrile (M3), and 2-(4-bromophenyl)-3-(thiophen-2-yl)fumaronitrile (m4) via a one-component direct C-H arylation polymerization (DArP) (Scheme 1). Note that all the starting monomers M1-M4 with or without -the CN group were all atom-economically synthesized via direct C-H vinylation, Knoevenagel condensation, or direct C-H cyanation. The structure-property-performance correlation of these DArP-derived CPs (CP1-CP4) with -CN substituents at varied positions has been systematically studied by opto-electrochemical measurements and PHP tests.

As shown in Scheme 1, the STP derivatives possess the merits of structural simplicity, synthetic easiness, and efficient D-A architecture. Monomer M1 without the -CN group was obtained by C-H vinylation,21 i.e., Pd-catalysed $\mathrm { C - H / C { \mathrm { - } } H }$ coupling between bromo-styrene and thiophene in the presence of benzoquinone. Monomers M2 and M3 with the -CN group adjacent to phenyl or thienyl rings were obtained by Knoevenagel condensation, which is also a green reaction involving only the side product of $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ . A novel C-H cyanation protocol using nontoxic ${ \bf K } _ { 3 } \mathrm { F e } ( \mathrm { C N } ) _ { 6 }$ as the cyanide source recently developed by our team?2 was employed to re-cyanate M3 to afford M4 with double -CN substituents on both sides of the vinylene linker. The one-component DArP (i.e. $\mathbf { \nabla } _ { \mathbf { C } - \mathbf { H } / \mathbf { C } - \mathbf { B r } }$ coupling polymerization)23 of $\bf { M 1 - M 4 }$ in the last step accordingly yielded P1-P4. Note that the weighing of monomers M1-M4 is much easier compared to the conventional two-component co-polymerizations that need precise stoichiometric amounts of two monomers. The obtained P1-P4 contain similar conjugated backbones (i.e., alternating phenylvinylene-thienyl) yet different -CN substituents on the vinylene linkers (Scheme 1), which should be ideal models for the structure-property-performance correlation study.

Single crystals of M2 and M3 were successfully grown and subjected to X-ray diffraction analysis (Fig. 1) to reveal the effects of -CN substituents on the molecular geometries. Here, M2 is a newly reported crystal (CCDC 2190735t). Fig. 1 shows that the skeletons and intermolecular aggregation behaviours of M2 and M3 have distinctly been affected by the -CN groups located at varied positions of the vinylene linker. The dihedral angles between phenyl and thienyl rings are $1 2 . 1 5 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 3 . 5 1 ^ { \circ }$ for M2 and M3, respectively. A smaller dihedral angle endows M2 with better planarity, which facilitates the intermolecular face-to-face 2D $\pi - \pi$ stacking (Fig. 1), while for M3, the hand-tohand architecture is formed via interchain hydrogen bonds.24 These differences are expected to exert effects on the inter- and intramolecular charge transfer, and thus the photocatalysis of the corresponding CPs.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to predict the optimal geometries of M1-M4. The dihedral angles between phenyl and thienyl follow the order of $\mathbf { M 4 } \ >$ $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { 3 } \ > \ \mathbf { M } \mathbf { 2 } \ > \ \mathbf { M } \mathbf { 1 }$ (Fig. S1, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ J. The uptrend in the dihedral angles of M2 and M3 predicted by DFT matches well with the experimental results of the single crystals. The chemical structures of CP1-CP4 were verified by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra (Fig. S2, ESI+). All CPs display peaks at $\sim 1 6 4 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , which are assigned to the stretching vibration of $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { C }$ double bonds, demonstrating the presence of vinylene linkers. Except for CP1, CP2-CP4 all display the characteristic stretching mode of the $\mathrm { C } { \equiv } \mathrm { N }$ triple bond at $\sim 2 2 1 2 \ c m ^ { - 1 }$ implying the successful incorporation of $- \mathbf { C N }$ into CP2-CP4.

Scanning electron microscopy (sEm) was used to check the morphologies of powdery CP1-CP4, all of which display welldefined dimensions and aggregation (Fig. S3, EsIt). The average particle sizes of CP1-CP4 estimated from SEM are 0.87, 1.86, 1.54 and $0 . 9 8 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ respectively (Fig. S4, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ J. CP1 and CP2 exhibit a lamellalike morphology, while CP3 and CP4 have a more irregular structure. Overall, the SEM morphologies of CP1-CP4 have little differences between each other due to the same conjugate backbone being involved (i.e., alternating phenyl-vinylene-thienyl), implying that the variation of -CN substituents on the M1-M4 monomers has a limited influence on the morphologies of the CP1-CP4 polymers at the micrometer scale. To shed light on the effect of -CN substituents on the opto-electronic properties, CP1-CP4 were further characterized by various measurements (Fig. 2), including UV-vis and steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectra in solid state, cyclic voltammetry (CV), and transient photocurrent response (TPR).

The UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRs) of all CP1-CP4 exhibited a broad absorption in the visible region between 300 and $6 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. 2a). Owing to the enhanced intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) by the D-A interaction, the incorporation of the electron-withdrawing -CN group into CP2-CP4 enables a distinct redshift in light absorption compared to CP1, which benefits the photon capture and utilization. The absorption of CP4 can even extend to the near-infrared region by incorporation of double -CN groups. The UV-vis spectral trend of CP1-CP4 is reflected by the evolution of colours, i.e., stronger absorbance toward longer wavelengths corresponds to deeper colours (insets in Fig. 2a). The optical bandgaps $\left( E _ { \mathrm { g } } \right)$ estimated by plotting the curves of $\left( \alpha h \nu \right) ^ { 2 }$ vs. hv (Fig. S5, ESI+) are 2.18, 2.01, 1.92 and $1 . 8 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for $\mathbf { C P 1 - C P 4 }$ , respectively.

Photoluminescence, as a photon-to-photon process, is a facile and useful index to check the recombination behaviour of photoinduced electron-hole $( \mathrm { e } ^ { - } \cdot \mathrm { h } ^ { + } )$ pairs. Typically, a stronger PL intensity means a higher radiative recombination of $\mathrm { e ^ { - } \mathrm { - } h ^ { + } }$ pairs, which will hamper charge separation and thus be less favourable to photocatalysis. Fig. 2b shows the PL spectra of the CPs; among them, the -CN-free CP1 displays the strongest light emission. However, the other three CPs CP2-CP4 exhibit much lower PL intensities compared to CP1, implying that the -CN-based $\mathbf { D - A }$ architecture can effectively promote intramolecular charge separation and minimize radiative recombination, thus yielding weaker photo-induced emissions.

Thermodynamically, the appropriate frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) of CPs, i.e., the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels, are crucial to yield a sufficient driving force for the photocatalytic redox of ${ \bf H } _ { 2 } { \bf O }$ The LUMO and HOMO levels are responsible, respectively, for the photocatalytic reduction and oxidation reactions. Just like the optical properties, the FMOs of $\pi \cdot$ conjugated polymers can also be finely tuned by varying the functional groups incorporated. Here, the changes of FMOs with the structural evolution from CP1 to CP4 were investigated by CV measurements (Fig. S6, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). The LUMO levels were calculated as $E _ { \mathrm { L U M O } } = - 4 . 8 0 - \big ( E _ { \mathrm { r e d } } - E _ { \mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } } } \big ) ,$ where $E _ { \mathrm { r e d } }$ and $E _ { \mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } } }$ represent the reduction and ferrocene/ferrocenium couple potentials, respectively. The HOMO levels were calculated based on $E _ { \mathrm { g } }$ and LUMOs: $E _ { \mathrm { H O M O } } = E _ { \mathrm { L U M O } } - E _ { \mathrm { g } } .$ As shown in Fig. 2c, all CPs intrinsically have sufficient driving forces for the proton reduction (Fig. S7, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ The incorporation of the electron-withdrawing -CN substituent can simultaneously downshift the LUMO and HOMO levels. As a result, CP1 without -CN and CP4 with double -CN groups exhibit the highest and lowest FMO levels, respectively (Fig. 2c).

The TPR measurement was carried out to investigate the photo-tocurrent efficiencies of the CPs under visible light irradiation. As shown in Fig. 2d, CP2 exhibits the maximum photocurrent, followed by CP3. CP1 and CP4 showed the lowest photocurrents that are close to each other. The highest TPR of CP2 can be ascribed to the synergistic effect of the planar M2 precursor and its D-A architecture, which yields the highest intermolecular charge transport.

The above results of structure-property correlations and the suitable FMO levels of CP1-CP4 for PHP encouraged us to further investigate the structure-performance correlations. Here, the PHP activities of the CPs were tested under visible light irradiation $( \lambda > 4 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ using ascorbic acid $\left( \mathrm { A A } \right) ^ { 7 , 2 5 }$ as a sacrificial electron donor (sED) in the absence of a Pt cocatalyst. Note that all CPs can be well dispersed in ${ \bf H } _ { 2 } { \bf O }$ without using a water soluble organic co-solvent to assist wettability. The hydrogen evolution rates (HERs) of CP1, CP2, CP3 and CP4 were 0.28, 8.78, 7.60, and $0 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively (Fig. 3). Firstly, the HERs show a tremendous increase from CP1 to CP2 and CP3, meaning that the -CN-involved vinylene $\scriptstyle \left( - \mathbf { C } = \mathbf { C } - \right)$ linkage is an effective $\pi$ -bridge to boost electron migration and thus photocatalysis. Although both CP2 and CP3 involve a -CN substituent, CP2 with a -CN adjacent to the phenyl ring exhibits a more planar conformation compared to CP3 (Fig. 1). The D-A architecture of M2 combined with planar conformation will exert a synergistic effect on the promotion of $\pi$ electron delocalization, ICT, and charge separation, rendering a higher photocatalytic activity for CP2 than CP3. CP1 $( 0 . 2 8 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ and CP4 $( 0 \mathrm { \ m m o l \ h } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ showed the lowest PHP performance, which can be ascribed, respectively, to the poorest photoinduced charge separation efficiency of CP1 (Fig. 2b) and the deepest LUMO levels of CP4 with the lowest driving force for the proton reduction (Fig. 2c).

The stability of the best-performing CP2 was also checked by the recycling test. Only a slight decrease can be observed in the continuous PHP reaction. After four cycles of CP2 for $^ { 2 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ about $8 4 \%$ of initial $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ evolution $\left( 0 . 1 8 5 \mathrm { \ m m o l } \right)$ can still be maintained, showing good stability to sacrificial photocatalytic hydrogen production (Fig. S8, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$

Surface wettability plays a vital role in aqueous photocatalysis, especially for CP-based photocatalysts. $^ { 9 - 1 1 , 2 6 }$ To better understand the correlation between structures and performance, the water contact angle measurement was carried out to test the wettability of CP1-CP4. As shown in Fig. 4, the contact angles follow the order of CP1 $\left( 1 4 0 . 3 ^ { \circ } \right) \ > \ \mathbf { C P 3 } \ \left( 1 1 9 . 6 ^ { \circ } \right) \ > \ \mathbf { C P 2 } \ \left( 7 4 . 6 ^ { \circ } \right) \ > \ \mathbf { C P 4 }$ $\left( 5 0 . 6 ^ { \circ } \right)$ revealing that the inclusion of the electron-withdrawing -CN group can improve the polarity and hydrophilicity of the CPs. Correspondingly, CP1 without -CN and CP4 with double -CN substituents showed the largest and smallest contact angles, respectively. Although CP4 has the best wettability, its deep-lying FMO levels decrease the driving force for proton reduction. This result implies that, besides the wettability, the FMOs of CPs should also be fully taken into account for the design of CP photocatalysts.25

We also became interested in the co-polymerization among M1-M4, which can be co-polymerized at any feed ratios (Scheme 2) owing to the inherently equimolar ${ \sf { q } } \mathrm { - } { \sf { C } } \mathrm { - } { \bf { H } }$ and $\mathrm { C - B r }$ bonds involved in each monomer. Here, M2 and M3, as the precursors of the best-performing CP2 and CP3, were chosen to direct arylation co-polymerization at varied feed ratios $\scriptstyle ( x : y )$ of $3 : 1 , 1 : 1 , 1 : 2 , 1 : 3 , 1 : 4$ , and 1: 5, respectively (Scheme 2). Impressively, the co-polymerized product from $\mathbf { M } 2 : \mathbf { M } 3$ at a 1:3 ratio afforded a HER of $9 . 7 9 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } ~ \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (Fig. s9, Esit), even higher than the best-performing CP2 $( 8 . 7 8 \mathrm { \ m m o l \ h ^ { - 1 } \ g ^ { - 1 } } )$ , implying that a $_ { 1 : 3 }$ ratio reaches a turning point that has optimal bandgap, a suitable FMO level and minimal charge recombination. To the best of our knowledge, gradually varying the feed ratio of two DArP monomers for co-polymerization represents a novel strategy for regulating the structures and properties of D-A type CP-based photocatalysts.

In conclusion, a library of STP-based CP photocatalysts without -CN (CP1), with the -CN substituent at varied positions (CP2 and CP3), and with double -CN substituents at both sides (CP4) of vinylene linkages were designed and synthesized by an atom- and step-economic one-component direct arylation polymerization from the starting monomers (M1-M4) that are facilely obtained by $\mathrm { C - H }$ vinylation, Knoevenagel condensation or $\mathrm { C - H }$ cyanation reactions. The properties and performances correlated with the structural evolution from CP1 to CP4 are systematically  studied by single-crystal  analysis,  DFT calculations, SEM, UV-vis, PL, CV, TPR, and PHP tests, revealing that the D-A CPs involving the -CN substituent can broaden the light absorption, lower the FMO levels, and improve the charge mobility. Among them, CP2 with a -CN substituent adjacent to the phenyl ring shows the most suitable opto-electrochemical properties and the best photocatalytic performance owing to the synergistic effect of the planar M2 precursor and its D-A architecture with minimum exciton recombination and maximum transient photocurrent response. The DArP copolymerization of M2 and M3 at a 1:3 feed ratio further boosted the PHP performance up to $9 . 7 9 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } ~ \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , outperforming most of the reported linear CPs (Table S1, ESI). The simple yet efficient protocol of incorporating -CN into the conjugated chain and finely modulating the structures (Schemes 1 and 2) opens a new avenue for the design of high-performance D-A type CP-based photocatalysts.
