# Highly Stretchable, Compliant, Polymeric Microelectrode Arrays for In Vivo Electrophysiological Interfacing

Dianpeng Qi, Zhiyuan Liu, Yan Liu, Ying Jiang, Wan Ru Leow, Mayank Pal, Shaowu Pan, Hui Yang, Yu Wang, Xiaoqian Zhang, Jiancan Yu, Bin Li, Zhe Yu,\* Wei Wang,\* and Xiaodong Chen\*

Polymeric microelectrode arrays (MEAs) are emerging as a new generation of biointegrated microelectrodes to transduce original electrochemical signals in living tissues to external electrical circuits, and vice versa. So far, the challenge of stretchable polymeric MEAs lies in the competition between high stretchability and good electrode-substrate adhesion. The larger the stretchability, the easier the delamination of electrodes from the substrate due to the mismatch in their Young's modulus. In this work, polypyrrole (PPy) electrode materials are designed, with PPy nanowires integrated on the high conduc. tive PPy electrode arrays. By utilizing this electrode material, for the first time, stretchable polymeric MEAs are fabricated with both high stretchability $1 \approx 1 0 0 \% )$ and good electrode-substrate adhesion (1.9 MPa). In addition, low Young's modulus $( 4 5 0 \ k \mathsf { P a } )$ , excellent recycling stability (10 000 cycles of stretch), and high conductivity of the MEAs are also achieved. As a proof of concept, the as-prepared polymeric MEAs are successfully used for conformally recording the electrocorticograph signals from rats in normal and epileptic states, respectively. Further, these polymeric MEAs are also successful in stimulating the ischiadic nerve of the rat. This strategy provides a new perspective to the highly stretchable and mechanically stable polymeric MEAs, which are vital for compliant neural electrodes.

Microelectrode arrays (MEAs), which interface living tissues with electrical hardware, constitute powerful tools for the transduction of original electrochemical signals in living tissues to electron-based currents in external electrical circuits, and vice versa.[1-5] Through these recording and stimulating processes, MEAs play a vital role in promoting the understanding of underlying physiological mechanisms, as well as improving diagnostics and therapy of neurological disorders and disabilities.[6-12] In recent years, conducting polymers have witnessed great progress in the design and fabrication of in vitro and in vivo MEAs for biointegrated electronics.[13] The conductivity of conducting polymers results from the delocalization of electrons in the overlapping carbon orbitals along the polymer backbone and the existence of mobile charge carrier (dopant) moving along the $\pi$ bonded carbon atoms.14-16] Therefore, the conducting polymers can transfer charge both by electronic and ionic mechanisms which greatly reduced impedance.17] Furthermore, comparing to the inorganic counterparts, conducting polymers hold many advantages for serving as MEAs materials, such as facile solution-based fabrication, diminished foreign-body response, low costs, greatly reduced impedance, as well as good stimulation capacities.118-22] Typically, conducting polymers serve as coating layers for the modification of metallic electrodes,[23-25] thus efficiently lowering the electrode-tissue impedance and synchronously propelling electron transportation owing to the high conductivity of the metals.18,26] However in these conducting polymer-metal composite MEAs, drawbacks associated with their noble metal parts should be addressed, such as high rigidity, possible invasion to tissues, relatively high costs, and poor stretchability. Furthermore, the possible delamination of the conducting polymer coating constitutes an important factor that needs to be considered.27] These critical issues result in the major challenges that lie ahead in this field.

Polymeric MEAs, which employ conducting polymers as the sole conductors, play an important role in the growth of next-generation biointegrated electronics, as they provide a more promising route for achieving cost-effective and intimate biotic/abiotic interfaces.28-30] A key performance parameter of the polymeric MEAs is their compliant feature which enhances their compatibility with the soft biological tissues. Here, the compliant feature refers to two factors; the first is the conformal match of the electrode-tissue interface, as shown in Figure 1a. Many works that focus on conformal electrode-tissue interface have been reported.[31-35] However, two more crucial features are still indispensable for conformal electrode-tissue interface. 1) High stretchability of MEAs is demanded to satisfy the strain induced by the living tissues $1 0 \%$ strain for brain, $2 0 { - } 3 5 \%$ strain for heart, and ${ \approx } 1 0 0 \%$ strain for skin),[36] and 2) low Young's modulus is required to enable easy stretching of the MEAs in the biological system.37-42] The second factor is the good adhesion of the electrode-substrate interface (Figure 1a), in order to avoid the detachment of electrodes from the substrate during numerous cycles of stretching induced by the tissues. Recently, the first stretchable polymeric neural MEAs were demonstrated using polypyrrole (PPy)/polycaprolactone-block-polytetrahydrofuranblock-polycaprolactone composite films as the sole conductors.

These MEAs showed a Young's modulus of $1 5 3 \pm 2 5 \mathrm { { \ : M P a } }$ and could withstand 7.5 stretching/relaxing cycles at ${ \approx } 2 2 \%$ strain without obvious increase in resistance. Furthermore, it showed a reduced impedance that is far better than that of the metalbased MEAs.[43] However, the research on stretchable polymeric MEAs is still at the embryonic stage, highly stretchable compliant polymeric MEAs are still urgently desired for the biointegrated electronics. The crucial challenges to this goal lie in the competition between the electrode-tissue interface and the electrode-substrate interface. High stretchability and low Young's modulus would contribute to a conformal electrodetissue interface. However, they would also easily result in the detachment of electrode materials from the substrate due to the large strain induced and the mismatch of Young's modulus. As a result, a poor electrode-substrate interface is inevitable. Therefore, the main challenge for highly stretchable compliant polymeric MEAs is the achievement of both high stretchability and good electrode-substrate adhesion.

In this work, we fabricated nanowires modified PPy electrode materials through simple electropolymerization processes. The nanowires could introduce a transition layer between the electrode and substrate to enhance their adhesion, while the PPy film provides good conductivity and low impedance. By utilizing this electrode material, for the first time, we have successfully fabricated highly stretchable compliant polymeric MEAs, where only PPy serves as the conductive electrode. The as-prepared MEAs can provide both good electrode-- tissue interface and high adhesive electrode-substrate interface, because the devices not only possess a high stretchability $100 \%$ strains) and low Young's modulus of ${ \approx } 4 5 0 \ \mathrm { k P a }$ , but also show enhanced adhesion between the electrode arrays and substrate $( { \approx } 1 . 9 \ \mathrm { M P a } )$ . In addition, it could withstand $1 0 ~ 0 0 0$ cycles of mechanical loading and exhibit a conductivity of ${ \approx } 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { S } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ which is much larger than the previously reported polymeric MEAs.[25,43] Not only that, good electrochemical stability of the MEAs was also achieved. As a proof of concept, we demonstrate the successful in vivo pathological application of the polymeric MEAs by conformally recording the electrocorticograph (ECoG) signals from rats in normal and epileptic states, respectively. Furthermore, these polymeric MEAs are also successful in stimulating the ischiadic nerve of the rat. Our strategy provides a new perspective to the highly stretchable and mechanically stable polymeric MEAs, which are vital for compliant neural electrodes.

One effective way to make stretchable electrodes is to induce wavy structure to the electrode materials by a prestretching strategy.44,45] In this work, we also employ this strategy to fabricate the stretchable polymeric electrodes, as shown in Figure 1b. Here, the wavy structure not only enables the electrode to be highly stretchable, but also enables the whole MEAs device with a lower Young's modulus compared with that of the MEAs with flat electrode structures. Therefore, it is much easier to stretch the MEAs based on wavy-structured electrodes. Additionally, the wavy structure induces less strain to the electrode during the stretching/relaxing process (Figure 1c), thus these electrodes are expected to maintain good recycling stability.36,46] However, a severe problem of the wavy-structured electrode is the delamination of the electrode materials from the substrate, because the adhesive force normally comes from the weak Van der Waals' interaction. Furthermore, the decreased electrode-substrate contacting area resulting from wavy structures makes the adhesive force much weaker. This problem is even more critical when a high stretchability is required. Here, inspired by the buildings which stand firmly on the ground by a transition layer (taking foundation in the soil), we can build a transition layer (nanowires buried in the substrate materials) at the electrode-substrate interface to enhance the adhesion between the electrode arrays and substrate (Figure 1b). Hence, the delamination of the electrode arrays from the substrate can be dramatically suppressed. As depicted by the simulation results in Figure 1d, compared with the electrodes without nanowire modification (Figure 1d-2), the adhesive strength of nanowire modified electrodes can be dramatically enhanced (Figure 1d-1,d-3) (more details of the calculation can be found in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). Furthermore, because the transition layer enhances the electrode-substrate adhesion, it may also facilitate the transfer of electrode arrays to the substrate materials during the fabricating process.

In a typical experimental process, to achieve the proposed wavy-structured electrodes with transition layer at the electrode-substrate interface, we designed a reliable and facile "stamping transfer method" for the fabrication. As schematically shown in Scheme 1, the PPy electrode arrays were first deposited on the prepatterned indium tin oxide (ITO) slide by electropolymerization (more details can be found in the Supporting Information and the setup is schematically shown in Figure S2 in the Supporting Information). PPy has alternating single bonds and double bonds ( $\pi$ bonds) in the polymer backbone, as the molecule structure shown in Scheme 1. It exhibits good electrical conductivity, good biocompatibility, and has been widely used in the tissue engineering.47,48] To avoid the delamination of PPy electrode from the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate due to their mismatch in Young's modulus, we fabricated a layer of PPy nanowires on the PPy electrode to enhance their adhesion. The enhanced adhesion not only improves the mechanical stability of the MEAs but also facilitates the transfer of PPy electrodes to the PDMS substrate. PDMS is one kind of silicon-based organic polymer with a structure containing carbon and silicon (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information). It is widely used in medical devices due to its inert, nontoxic, and biocompatible properties. After removing the photoresist, a "stamping transfer method" was conducted to transfer the PPy electrode arrays. In this process, we stamped the PPy electrode arrays onto prestretched PDMS substrate. It should be pointed out that a thin layer of semicured PDMS was coated on the substrate's surface to serve as glue (the total thickness is about $1 6 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ Figure S4 in the Supporting Information). Due to the transition layer where PPy nanowires take root into the thin PDMS layer, the electrode-substrate adhesion is greatly enhanced. Thus, after peeling off the ITO slide, the PPy microbelts were uniformly transferred to the prestretched PDMS substrate. By releasing the substrate, a highly wavy-structured PPy electrode arrays grasping tightly on PDMS substrate were obtained. Till date, several strategies have been reported for the fabrication of stretchable electrodes.(46,49] However, these fabrication processes are complicated and unreliable for the fabrication of polymeric MEAs, whereby the electrodes are often subjected to harsh solvents or high-energy fields, resulting in unstable electrical properties.[24] The decrease in conductivity of the as-obtained material is primarily due to its tendency to dedope again in biological environment (Figure S5 in the Supporting Information). For our design, it was ensured that during the entire fabrication process, PPy conductors are free from exposure to harsh solvents and high-energy fields, resulting in enhanced electrical stability (Figure S5 in the Supporting Information).[43]

The inherent mechanical properties have a great effect on the performance of the compliant polymeric MEAs. The stretchability of the polymeric MEAs was first checked through simple visual clues. Specifically, we fabricated a multielectrode array with tens of PPy microbelts on a prestretched $( 1 0 0 \% )$ PDMS substrate (Figure 2a). Subsequently, we designed a simple circuit to power a light-mitting diode (LED) lamp, in which the PPy microbelts were employed as a stretchable conductor. The LED retained its luminescence when the PPy microbelts were stretched to a strain of ${ \approx } 1 0 0 \%$ , indicating that the MEAs had stable electrical conductivity under stretching. To further investigate the mechanism of the observed electromechanical behavior, the surface morphology of the PPy microbelts was monitored in situ during a stretching/releasing cycle via optical microscopy (inset of Figure 2b,c). It was observed that the PPy microbelts remained tightly adhered to the PDMS substrates during the whole stretching process. In released state, wavy PPy microbelt structures were induced due to buckling in the stretching direction. By increasing the strains on samples, the amplitudes of these wavy structures reduce with a corresponding increase in their lengths. Even when the strain increased to the original prestretched length of ${ \approx } 1 0 0 \%$ no crack was observed on the PPy microbelts, which accounts for the excellent electromechanical stability. By contrast, when metal is employed as the conductive electrode, such mechanical performances cannot be obtained. A control experiment was conducted by fabricating gold microbelts on a prestretched PDMS surface via an etching-assisted transfer method $6 0 \%$ prestretched length).[36] As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), after the prestretch is released, many cracked regions appear within the gold belts. This is because, during stretching/releasing process, the stresses along various directions produced by the PDMs damage the fragile metal electrodes easily. While the PPy conductors possess a $1 5 4 . 5 \mathrm { M P a }$ tensile strength and can inherently withstand tensile strains of ${ \approx } 9 \%$ (Figure S7 in the Supporting Information), indicating their remarkable mechanical strength.(43,50,51] Therefore, such high stretchability of the as-prepared polymeric MEAs should be ascribed simultaneously to the excellent inherent mechanical properties of the PPy conductors and the wavy microstructures. Concurrently, the electrical properties of the PPy microbelts were further in situ characterized during stretching. Figure 2d shows the I-V curves subjected to varying strains, indicating that these highly stretchable polymeric MEAs could be easily stretched up to a strain of $100 \%$ $100 \%$ is the prestretched length) without any increase in resistance. Moreover, the stability of the polymeric MEAs was tested by recording its resistance during 10 o00 cycles of the stretching/relaxing processes and no obvious increase in resistance was observed (Figure 2e). One particular problem of the stretchable compliant polymeric MEAs comes from the electrode-substrate interface, which is the detachment of the electrode materials from the substrate during the stretching/relaxing process due to the mismatch of their Young's modulus. To enhance the electrodesubstrate adhesion, we synthesized the nanowires on the surface of PPy electrode via an electropolymerization process to construct the transition layer. As shown in Figure 2h, the PPy nanowires uniformly cover the surface of PPy electrodes. The formation of the PPy nanowires most possibly attributes to the production of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ nanobubbles at the PPy film surface. During electropolymerization, the electrooxidation of water happened, overoxidation of PPy would occur in some sites due to the hydroxyl radicals, meanwhile $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ nanobubbles formed. These $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ nanobubbles would prevent the polymer from further overoxidation, and work as soft templates to induce the formation of PPy nanowires at the sites where $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ nanobubbles evolved.52,53] The nanowires can be buried in the semicured PDMS layer on the substrate. After complete curing, the nanowires were immobilized firmly in the PDMS substrate (Figure S9 in the Supporting Information). Therefore, the electrode-substrate adhesion is greatly enhanced. As shown in Figure 2i, the adhesive strength increases to about $1 . 9 \mathrm { M P a }$ after nanowires (aspect ratio is ${ \approx } 1 0$ modification $( { \approx } 0 . 2 5 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ without nanowires modification) (the details about the setup of the adhesive strength measurement are shown in Figure S10 in the Supporting Information). Furthermore, the adhesive strength of the PPy film with different aspect ratio was compared (Figure S11 in the Supporting Information), the adhesive strength increased with the increase of aspect ratio of the nanowires, which is in consistence with the simulating results. It is notable that most of the nanowires with the aspect ratio of 10:1 fractured and buried in the PDMS substrate after peeling of the PPy electrode (Figure S9 in the Supporting Information). Thus, it is not necessary to further increase the aspect ratio of nanowires. To demonstrate the enhanced adhesion in a visual way, we pasted the high temperature tape (acrylic adhesive, product of 3M Company) on the top of PPy film, where nanowires were modified on its bottom side, and buried in the PDMS substrate (Figure S12a,b in the Supporting Information). The PPy film adhered to the PDMS substrate too tightly to be removed by peeling of the tape (Video S3 in the Supporting Information). By contrast, without the modification of nanowires, it can be easily removed by the tape, as shown in Figure S12c,d and Video S4 in the Supporting Information. Additionally, it has been well confirmed that the smaller the material thickness, the better it matches with the substrates.24,31] In our work, the thickness of PPy electrodes has been decreased by one to two orders compared with their previous counterparts used in MEAs.30,43] The thin structure enables the PPy electrode to exhibit excellent flexibility, as shown in Figure S13 in the Supporting Information. It can be easily bent with a radius of curvature typically less than $1 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ . To highlight the benefits of reducing the electrodes' thickness, we conducted experiments figuratively by putting the PPy films with different thicknesses on the back of the hand, as shown in Figure S14 in the Supporting Information. The crease details of the hand can be clearly observed permeating through the $1 . 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ thick PPy conductors, indicating that a conformal cover on a curvilinear surface can be achieved. On the contrary, for the control sample with a thickness of ${ \approx } 1 5 ~  { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ the PPy film is too stiff to provide a fiting contact. Therefore, the reduced thickness of the electrodes enables them to match the surface of substrate, and nanowires based transition layer greatly enhances the adhesion of electrode-substrate interface.

It has been well established that the improvement in electrical conductivity of conducting polymers can dramatically enhance the charge transfer of polymeric MEAs for neural interfacing.[18] One way to improve the conductance of the polymeric electrodes is to increase their thickness.[43] However, this high thickness tends to passively increase their stiffness, which influences the match of electrode-substrate interface.31,54] Otherwise, these stiff electrodes act as microknives in the soft biological system, which results in the injury of brain and neural fibers.55] In this work, we employed aqueous p-toluenesulfonic acid (PTSA) as the electrolyte instead of the conventionally used sodium $\mathrm { p }$ -toluenesulfonate (PTS-Na) to improve the conductivity of the PPy electrode (more details can be found in the experimental part). The impact of the synthesis conditions on conductivity of PPy was investigated and characterized by the standard four-point probe method. It is found that the current density exhibits insignificant effects on the conductivity of the PPy electrode. As shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information), when a current density of $0 . 5 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ is applied, PPy exhibits the highest conductivity. $\mathrm { S o ~ } 0 . 5 \mathrm { \ m A ~ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ is chosen as the standard electroplating current density for all the experiments in this work. The samples polymerized in PTSA solution exhibit remarkably higher conductivity than that of PPy prepared in PTs-Na electrolyte (Figure 3a). In particular, when the mole ratio of pyrrole (py):PTSA is 1:2, the PPy film shows the highest conductivity of $8 2 0 ~ \mathrm { { S } ~ c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , which is much larger than the previous reports.[25,43] To reasonably explain this result, we take both interchain and intrachain conductivities of the conducting polymers into consideration. In many cases, the interchain conductivity of p-conjugated polymers plays a vital role in the macroscopic conductivity due to the significant barrier between the polymer chains.22,6,57] The vivid metallic luster of the PPy films represents a high electron density per unit volume (Figure S15a in the Supporting Information).[56] In addition, the PPy films show smooth surfaces composed of nanosized particles (Figure S15b in the Supporting Information), implying a low electrical resistance in the films obtained.156] This suggests that the PPy films may have a high interchain conductivity. We also characterized the molecular structure of the as-prepared PPy by Raman spectra (Figure S16 in the Supporting Information) for quantitatively estimating the conjugation length, which determines the intrachain conductivity. The intensity ratio of Raman peaks corresponding to the symmetric $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { C }$ stretching mode $( 1 5 8 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ and the skeletal band $( 1 5 0 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ of the PPy film synthesized in PTSA solution is larger than that of the PPy film fabricated in PTS-Na solution, which strongly indicates the lengthening of the $\pi$ conjugation of the PPy chains.58,59]

Most of the information processed and transmitted in the biological nervous system is of electrochemical nature, and the transferring efficacy of the electrical signals between ions and electrons at the electrode-tissue interface plays a vital role in the performance of MEAs.[4,11] Accordingly, the electrochemical properties of our polymeric MEAs were characterized in phosphate buffer saline solution $\mathrm { ( p H = 6 . 8 ) }$ . A gold electrode with the same geometric size $( \approx 1 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thickness) was employed for a fair comparison. As the electrical impedance spectroscopy shown in Figure 3b, polymeric MEAs show a much better response than those of the gold electrodes at lower frequencies (below $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { H z }$ . In addition, the phase responses (Figure 3c) indicate that the PPy electrodes are more resistive at frequencies below $1 \ \mathrm { k H z } . ^ { [ 7 , 8 ] }$ The as-prepared polymeric MEAs are expected to be more suitable for recording local field potentials $( 1 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { H z } )$ . The function of electrode-tissue interface is to realize effective communication between biosystem and external electrical systems. Aside from recording of electrophysiological signals from the biosystem, the transfer of external electrical signals into the biosystem is equally important.[60] The external input electrical signals not only can stimulate the biosystem for treatment of diseases, but also serve as a continuous feedback for the neural prosthesis.[61] To characterize the stimulation capability of our polymeric MEAs, we employ cyclic voltammetry (CV) sweeps to demonstrate its charge storage capability at low scan rate $( 5 0 \mathrm { m V } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , Figure 3d) and high scan rate $[ 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ s ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ , Figure 3e), respectively. Our MEAs show a capacitance of $\approx 6 7 . 1 ~ \mathrm { m C } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ at a scan rate of $5 0 \mathrm { m V s ^ { - 1 } }$ (Figure 3f), which is one of the best figures achieved so far in the polymerbased electrodes.23,62] This value is an order magnitude higher than that of smooth gold electrode $( 1 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m C } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ . At high scan rate $( 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ s ~ } ^ { - 1 } )$ , the polymeric MEAs still possess a capacitance of $5 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m C } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , while that for the gold electrode is $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { m C } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ (Figure 3f). As discussed above, the inherent mechanical properties and excellent electrical properties suggest that our highly stretchable polymeric MEAs are outstanding candidates for bioelectronic applications.

Eventually, facilitated by the mechanical and electrochemical properties of the polymeric MEAs, we demonstrate them as practical implants for the in vivo identification of normal and epileptic rat through the recording of ECoG signals. The tests here involved an anaesthetized rat with its head (with $4 \times 5$ mm exposed region of cortex) fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. The as-prepared MEAs (Figure 4a) were merely used to cover the visual cortex as shown in Figure 4b. Figure 4c,d shows the four channel ECoG signals when the rat was in the normal (Figure 4c) and epileptic (Figure 4d) states, respectively. The sudden intensified brain signals in the epileptic rat could be clearly and obviously observed using our polymeric MEAs. Cloud maps of the alpha wave band $( 8 \mathrm { - } 1 4 ~ \mathrm { H z } )$ , extracted from four-layer decomposition of the first channel using functioned waves and db10 wavelet, were also used to analyze the ECoG. A sharp increase in energy appears in $1 0 . 5 { - } 1 1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { H z }$ which is caused by the brain synchronicity discharge induced by epilepsy. Furthermore, encouraged by the high charge storage capability $( 6 7 ~ \mathrm { m C } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ of the polymeric MEAs, we demonstrate the stimulation efficiency of the MEAs by in vivo stimulating the ischiadic nerve of the rat. As shown in Figure 4e, the MEAs wrap the neural fber well and form a conformal contact with it. The polymeric MEAs were used to deliver pulsed electrical input to the neural fiber such that the rat leg moved periodically (Video S5 in the Supporting Information), and the corresponding electromyography was recorded by the needle electrodes (Figure 4e,f). It is noteworthy that even after 200 electrical pulse stimulations $( 0 . 6 \ \mathrm { H z }$ current pulse, ms pulse width), no obvious fatigue in the rat leg was observed (Figure S17 in the Supporting Information). This accounts for the enhanced electrochemical stability and long term functionality of the polymeric MEAs. Otherwise, the good biocompatibility and biosafety of the PPy electrode can be evaluated by the high viability of the cells on the surface of PPy electrode (Figure S18 in the Supporting Information) and the normal status (no obvious inflammation was observed) of the tissue around the implanted electrode after one week implantation (Figure S19 in the Supporting Information). The as-prepared PPy electrode also exhibited good long-term stability both in mechanical and electrical properties (Figure s20 in the Supporting Information). Due to its facile fabrication, low cost, as well as its efficiency in neural interface electronics, the highly stretchable PPy-based polymeric MEAs constitute to be promising candidate for in vivo neural interfacing applications. It is believed that the present work can promote the development of polymeric bioelectronics for practical applications.

In conclusion, by designing the polymeric electrode materials, where PPy nanowires integrated on the high conductive PPy electrode arrays, we introduce transition layer to the electrode-substrate interface to enhance their adhesion, and successfully fabricate highly stretchable compliant polymeric MEAs for electrophysiological interfacing. First, the compliant polymeric MEAs provide a conformal match of electrode-tissue interface. This is contributed by the merits of high stretchability (no electrical conductivity changed under $100 \%$ uniaxial tensile strain) and low Young's modulus $( \approx 1 2 5 \mathrm { k P a } _ { \mathit { i } }$ , of the polymeric MEAs. Second, the compliant polymeric MEAs possess enhanced electrode-substrate adhesion $( { \approx } 1 . 9 \ \mathrm { \ M P a } )$ , which is contributed by the transition layer. Additionally, excellent recycling stability (10 o00 times repeated mechanical loading), high conductivity (exceeding $8 0 0 \ \mathrm { S \ c m ^ { - 1 } } )$ , and stable electrochemical properties were also achieved. As a proof of concept, we demonstrated a successful implementation of using the as-prepared polymeric MEAs for in vivo recording the ECoG signals from the rat in normal and epileptic states, respectively. Further, these compliant polymeric MEAs were also successful in stimulating the ischiadic nerve of the rat. Our strategy provides a new perspective to the highly stretchable and mechanically stable polymeric MEAs, which are vital for compliant neural electrodes.
