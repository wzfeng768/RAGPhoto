# Intensification of Vertical Phase Separation for Efficient Polymer Solar Cell via Piecewise Spray Assisted by a Solvent Driving Force

Jiang Cheng, Shenghao Wang, Yang Tang, Rong Hu, Xingwu Yan, Zhen Zhang, Lu Li,\* and Qibing Pei

Piecewise spray has the advantage of controlling the component distribution, i.e., constructing vertical phase-separated active layers, to improve the charge generation efficiency of large-scale polymer solar cells. However, it brings a connection problem because of the drastic difference in the donor/acceptor ratio of continuous coatings using the conventional solvent. Assisted by 1,3,5. trimethylbenzene-based solvent engineering, a solvent driving force is formed, resulting in a continuous interface for piecewise spray-coated active layers. The blend film forms a bicontinuous interpenetrating network in the active layer and provides efficient percolation pathways for charge carrier transport, resulting in a considerable improvement in the photovoltaic performance. When the donor/ acceptor ratio is 1:2 in the first coating and 2:1 in the second coating, and the thickness of each coating is $\mathfrak { s o n m }$ , the performance of the PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F photovoltaic device shows a notable conversion efficiency of $1 2 . 2 9 \%$ with a high short-circuit current density of $2 1 . 5 5 \mathsf { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . Importantly, this piecewise spray technique can be used for large-scale module fabrication.

# 1. Introduction

Polymer solar cell (PSC) has ushered in a significant leap due to the rational material synthesis accompanied by heterojunction interface optimization. Recently, the nonfullerene bulk heterojunction (BHJ) device achieved an encouraging power conversion efficiency (PCE) in the range $1 4 - 1 6 \%$ for lab-based devices.1-6] In addition, this great progress is also inseparable from sufficient effort on the optimization of the morphology and microstructure of the BHJ layer.!7-91 To realize efficient exciton dissociation and provide good percolation pathways for charge carrier transport, donor and acceptor are mixed together to form bicontinuous interpenetrating networks with large interfacial areas.

Practically, an adequate vertical phase separation for donor and acceptor is desirable.11,11 Vertical phase separation in spincoated films is usually dependent on the properties of both substrate and solution.1] For example, by utilizing the surface energy difference of the components and the interaction between the active layer and the substrates, spontaneous phase separation could be realized!13] In addition, adding high boiling point additives such as 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) to the solution has been proved to be an efficient way to realize the vertical phase separation.14] Post-treatments such as thermal annealing[15] and solvent vapor separation[16] are also beneficial for the vertical domain orientation and gradient component variation. However, in the aforementioned methods, the phase separation degree is largely dependent on the surface state, substrate temperature, vapor pressure, and other environmental conditions, which could pose big challenges for the future commercial production of large-scale PSCs. The morphology control of large-scale BHJ film is much more complicated. Thus, although significant effort has been made in large-scale device or module fabrication using doctor-blading,17,18] spray coating,19,20] screen printing[21,22] and roll-to-roll printingl23,24] techniques, their commercial production is still a dream. For the conventional spray-coating technology, the performance of large-scaled device is sensitive to the Marangoni-effect-related micro defects such as coffee-rings and pin holes.25] In this study, an intensified vertical phase-separated BHJ layer was built via combining piecewise spray method and solvent engineering (i.e., adding 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene [TMB] in PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F precursor). The solvent driving force is formed, creating a continuous connection interface. This technique of realization of phase separation for BHJ films is much more efficient and controllable than the aforementioned methods.!13-16] As a result, the short-circuit current of spray-coated PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F solar cell exhibits a significant improvement, resulting in a high efficiency of $1 2 . 2 9 \%$

# 2. Results and Discussion

The molecular structures of conjugated polymer PBDB-T-2Cl and nonfullerene acceptor IT4F are shown in Figure 1a. In a typical coating process for PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F blend system, the donor is prone to form network first and the acceptor is restricted by PBDB-T-2Cl network, resulting in a poor crystalline for acceptor and also a poor phase separation.26] To realize vertical phase separation, a piecewise spray process was divided into two continuous coatings. The thickness of each coating is precisely $9 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and the total thickness of the active layer is $1 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . To achieve uniform spraying, each coating contains three spray cycles. In each cycle, the relative movement of ultrasonic nozzle and thermostatic plate follows a special circular route as shown in Figure 1b. The device structures along with energy levels are shown in Figure 1c,d, respectively.

To study the effect of the composition distribution, the donor and acceptor (D/A) ratio was varied in the two coatings, and the total D/A ratio was kept as a constant $( \mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A } = 1 { : } 1$ ). Thus, the D/A ratio of the two coatings was complementary, i.e., if the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio is 1:2 in the first coating, it should be 2:1 in the second coating. Mixture solvent consisting of chlorobenzene (CB), TMB, and $0 . 5 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ DIO was chosen to dissolve PBDB-T-2Cl and IT4F. When the solvent is CB and DIO, the blend film exhibited a relatively uniform mixture as shown in Figure S1a, Supporting Information. When the solution consists of $2 0 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ TMB, PBDB-T-2Cl always formed a network structure in the blend surface, and IT4F was located in the deeper position (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). In the case of exceeding $4 0 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ , a large amount of pigments appeared on the top of the network structure (Figure S1c, Supporting Information). The element fuorine signal was detected in these aggregations by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Figure S2, Supporting Information), suggesting that the IT4F was driven to the surface and aggregated to large species as observed. This nonuniform morphology will obviously deteriorate the device performance. Therefore, $20 \%$ TMB was chosen as the optimized concentration.

The current density-voltage $( J - V )$ characteristics and external quantum efficiency (EQE) curves of the devices prepared by piecewise spray using $2 0 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ TMB are shown in Figure 2a,b. The photovoltaic parameters of the devices are summarized in Table 1. Figure 2c shows the relationship between the device performance and $\mathsf { D } / \mathsf { A }$ ratios. The controlled device prepared by only one whole coating process, i.e., $\mathsf { D } / \mathsf { A }$ ratio of 1:1 (Device 4) exhibits a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 6 \mathrm { V }$ a $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 9 . 9 3 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and a fill factor (FF) of $6 2 . 9 3 \%$ , resulting in a PCE of $1 0 . 7 8 \%$ . Overall, the devices with higher concentration of acceptor in the first coating show better device performance than that of the controlled device. In other words, for the same $\mathsf { D } / \mathsf { A }$ ratio (e.g., Devices 3 and 5), the higher concentration of acceptor in the first coating (corresponding to the lower concentration of acceptor in the second coating because of the same total amount of the donor and acceptor) shows better device performance. In addition, the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio in the first coating also affects the device performance. When the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio is 2:3 in the first coating, the device performance shows $1 1 . 4 0 \%$ PCE. Further decreasing the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio to 1:2 in the first coating improved the performance of device PCE with the maximum PCE of $1 2 . 2 9 \%$ , which is very close to that of the spin-coated device.[4,27]

To illustrate the effect of TMB on the morphology of the blend film as well as device performance, the conventional solvent (CB with $0 . 5 \%$ D1O)28,29] used for piecewise spraying was also investigated. The corresponding $J { - } V$ characteristic plots and EQE of the devices are shown in Figure S3 and S4, Supporting Information. The performances of the devices are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. Clearly, all the devices without TMB show deteriorated performance as compared to that with TMB. The films rapidly dried and solidified during the spray process when spray solvent mainly comprised CB. As a result, the blend film prepared without TMB formed a mismatched connection in the interface during the discontinuous two-stage coatings (as shown in Figure S5, Supporting Information). Although the high boiling point DIO might have some effect on the component distribution, the effect of DIO on vertical separation is very limited. To further study the mechanism of solvent driving force on the phase separation, the effects of TMB in the drying process on the film morphology and the film composition were systematically investigated. A group of blend films with the D/A ratio of 1:1 using solvent with and without TMB was prepared. Figure 3a,b show the differential interference contrast images of the films. As shown, the blend film surface without TMB shows heavy "coffee-ring," and the edges of which are distinct. In contrast, by using CB and TMB as the mixed solvent, the edge of the "coffee-ring" disappeared. Some components are aggregated and enriched in the surface. Near-infrared reflection was utilized to investigate the component in the outermost surface of the blend films. The relative reflection spectra of PBDB-T-2Cl and IT4F films are shown in Figure 3c, and the reflection spectra of the blend films prepared using different solvents are shown in Figure 3d. For PBDB-T-2Cl film, the reflection range can be divided two parts: a visible reflection approximately in the range $6 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ with a reflection peak at $6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and a broad reflection range in the near-infrared range with a reflection peak at $1 6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ The IT4F film has a mono reflection peak at $7 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and a near homogeneous reflection with an average $1 7 \%$ relative reflection in the near-infrared range. The blend film shows the characteristics of both the components. The reflection value of the blend film prepared using mixed solvent at $1 6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ is higher than that prepared without TMB, indicating the film contains more PBDB-T-2Cl on the surface. The surface composition of the blend surfaces was verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The $\textsf { S } 2 \mathsf { p }$ peaks (containing S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2) and C 1s peaks blend prepared by using solvent with and without TMB are shown in Figure S8, Supporting Information. Quantitative elemental analysis via relative sensitivity factors (RSF) was performed. For the flm prepared using CB and DIO solvents, the relative atomic ratio value of $\mathrm { S } / \mathrm { C }$ is 0.186, whereas in the case of $2 0 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ TMB additive, the relative atomic ratio of $\mathrm { S / C }$ is increased to 0.195. The results provide additional evidence that the surface of the blend films prepared with TMB has more PBDB-T-2Cl component, because PBDB-T-2Cl has a higher concentration of S according their chemical structures (Figure 1). Because of the lower evaporation rate, there is enough time for the self-assembly process, and PBDB-T-2Cl also tends to form an orientated structure. Component distribution was further characterized by grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS). Figure 3e,f show the 2D GIWAXS patterns of the PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F blend films prepared using solvent with and without $2 0 \%$ TMB. Figure $3 \mathrm { g }$ shows the corresponding intensity profile for the in-plane orientations, the diffraction peak of which represents the number of face-on molecules. The blend film prepared using mixed solvent shows a more obvious face-on preferred orientation. The increasing FF for the device prepared using mixed solvent comprising TMB is also likely to be contributed by the increasing orientation of the polymers because the faceon orientation is favorable for the transport of free carriers toward contact electrodes.[8,9]

The elemental distribution of the blend films prepared using TMB was investigated by XPS. The sample was etched roughly by $3 0 \mathrm { n m }$ per step, and XPS spectra of Cl or F elements at the depths of 30, 60, 90, 120, and $1 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ were aquired, as shown in Figure 4. The intensity of Cl2p peak decreases as the depth increases from 30 to $1 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , whereas the intensity of the F1s S peak increases with increasing depth from 30 to $1 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . In addition, the variation trend of S2p also agrees to $\mathrm { C l } 2 \mathrm { p }$ well. Thus, the blend films with TMB shows a clear vertical phase separation; i.e., the PBDB-T-2Cl is located in more top direction and the IT4F is located in much deeper position. This result is fully consistent with the results of differential interference microscopy, relative reflection spectra, and the GIWAXS as shown in Figure 3.

The formation of varied morphologies could arise from the different physical properties of CB and TMB. For the device prepared without TMB, polymer is easy to crystallize because CB is an easy volatile solvent, and the polymers are prone to form network first, then IT4F is restricted by the crystallized network as shown in Figure S6a, Supporting Information. This structure will seriously deteriorate the carrier transport efficiency because of bimolecular recombination.130,31] With $2 0 \mathrm { v o l } \%$ low-volatile TMB, the crystallization rate decreased. Because TMB is a good solvent for IT4F and a bad solvent for PBDB-T-2Cl, a part of the polymer (i.e., PBDB-T-2Cl) first precipitated and wrapped on the surface. With further volatilization, PBDB-T-2Cl crystallized from inside and formed a semi-open network attributing to self-assembly. Finally, IT4F filled in the semi-open network and the blend formed a continuous interpenetrating network as shown in Figure S6b, Supporting Information. This crystallization manner leads to a dominant gradient component distribution; i.e., PBDB-T-2Cl was driven to the upper surface, whereas IT4F was driven to the bottom as shown in Figure 5a. For the device having a low D/A ratio in the first coating and high $\mathrm { D } /$ A ratio in the second coating (e.g., Device 2), PBDB-T-2Cl increased in the upper surface of the first coating and decreased in the bottom of the second coating, which equals a component homogenization near the interface between the two coatings as shown in Figure 5b. Thus, PBDB-T-2Cl tends to form a bicontinuous interpenetrating network in the whole range of the blend film, significantly improving the $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF and helps boosting the device performance to the maximum of $1 2 . 2 9 \%$

PBDB-T-2Cl first formed a crystallized network, and IT4F penetrates toward the deeper layer in the drying process. # 3. Conclusion

The intensification of vertical phase separation for PBDB-T-2Cl: IT4F blend film was realized for preparing high efficiency PSCs via piecewise spray technique, assisted by TMB-based solvent engineering. The low volatile TMB plays an important role in constructing a gradient component distribution. The controlled device with a constant $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio of 1:1 exhibits a relatively low PCE of $1 0 . 7 8 \%$ . An appropriate decreasing $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio in the first coating and increasing $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio in the second coating are beneficial for the vertical phase separation. The best condition is when the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ ratio is 1:2 in the first coating and 2:1 in the second coating, and the thickness of each coating is $9 0 \mathrm { n m }$ .The improved PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F photovoltaic device shows a notably improved PCE of $1 2 . 2 9 \%$ . The module realization with $6 . 6 8 \%$ conversion efficiency demonstrates the potential of the piecewise spray technique with TMB assistance for industrial production of efficient nonfullerene PSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

Device Fabrication: Conjugated polymer PBDB-T-2Cl and nonfullerene acceptor IT4F were purchased from Solarmer Materials Inc. Patterned

ITO glass with $1 0 \Omega / \mathsf { s q }$ sheet resistance was used as the substrate. Spray pyrolysis deposited ZnO $( 5 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ was used as the cathode buffer layer (CBL). During the active layer coating process, the flow rate of the carrier gas $( \mathsf { N } _ { 2 } )$ and solution-spraying rate were held constant at $2 0 \mathsf { L } \mathsf { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and $\bar { 0 } . 0 7 \dot { 5 } \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. The $\mathsf { D } / \mathsf { A }$ ratio were variable in each coating stage, and the concentration of the total solute in the solution was $6 \tt { m g } \tt { m } \tt { L } ^ { - 1 }$ ${ \mathsf { M o O } } _ { 3 }$ films $( 1 2 \mathsf { n m } )$ and $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ flms $( 7 5 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ were deposited on the top of the PBDB-T-Cl:IT4F flms by thermal evaporation technique under high vacuum $( < ] 0 ^ { - 4 } { \mathsf p a } )$ . To avoid irregularities, four specimens were prepared under each technique, and each specimen had four cells with $\dot { 4 } \mathsf { m } \dot { \mathsf { m } } ^ { 2 }$ active area.

Blend Film Characterization: The morphology of PBDB-T-Cl:IT4F blend surface were characterized by scanning electron microscopy. (SEM, Zeiss Merlin, Germany). The micro-scale surface morphology of the PBDB-T-2Cl:IT4F flms was determined using a 3D digital optical microscope (Olympus Dsx5oo, Japan). Reflection spectra of the blend flms prepared using different solvent were acquired using an integrating sphere using UV-Vis-IR spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 5000, USA). The chemical composition distribution of the fabricated flms was analyzed by XPS (Thermo ESCALAB, USA), and the thickness of the films was measured using a stylus profile meter (Alpha-Step D-100, USA). GIWAXS measurements were performed using a Bruker Nanostar system (AXS GmbH, Germany). Frequency-dependent impedance was measured using an electrochemical workstation (Chen Hua CHl660E, China) in the frequency range of $\rceil \mathsf { H z }$ to ${ \mathsf { 1 } } { \mathsf { M H z } }$

Device Characterization: The $J { - } V$ characteristics of the devices were measured using a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter, under the illumination of a xenon lamp (94043A, Newport) with a power of $\mathsf { l o o } \mathsf { m w c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . The EQE of the devices was obtained using an integrated system (Beijing SOFN 7-SCSpeclll) with a lock-in amplifier under short-circuit condition.
