# Electronic Structure Characterization of Soft Semiconductors

Xianjie Liu and Mats FahIman\*

Soft semiconductors are a class of materials that have seen increased interest in terms of both basic research and development of technology, in particular optoelectronic devices. These materials, organic semiconductors and metal halide perovskites, are defined by being more mechanically malleable than the traditional crystalline inorganic semiconductors and with thin flm fabrication done at lower temperatures and often from solution. In this short perspective article, basic properties of the materials are introduced, as well as their typical applications and a number of advanced characterization techniques that offer distinct advantages for studying soft semiconductor thin films.

# 1. Introduction

The use of inorganic semiconductor materials has revolutionized society in a wide variety of ways, improving quality of life all over the world. The now dominant semiconductor technology is based on covalently-bonded crystalline structures of inorganic elements, silicon being the prototypical example, often modified (doped) by controlled introduction of hetero-atoms/ions into the crystalline films. The strength of the covalent bonds makes the crystalline inorganic semiconductors relatively hard/brittle, however, they require high temperature processing (film growth from melts and/or chemical vapor deposition). In contrast, new types of semiconductor materials, such as organic semiconductorsll and metal halide perovskites,[2] have emerged where the film structure is maintained by van der Waals forces or ionic bonds, see Figure 1. This results in more mechanically malleable materials and film fabrication is done at low temperatures and often from solution. Though these semiconductors can be organic, hybrid organic, or even wholly inorganic, their "softness" is a unifying theme, providing advantages in terms of, e.g., low temperature solution-based processing that offers excellent energy payback time and the possibility for conformational design of films and devices.

Organic semiconductors (OsC) have been studied for at least ${ \approx } 7 0 $ years[3,4] and are now the prototypical soft semiconductor material. Organic semiconductors are $\pi$ conjugated organic molecules or polymers mainly consisting of light elements (H, C, N, O, F, and S). The (opto)electronic properties of these semiconductors are controlled by the $\pi$ electrons that form the occupied and unoccupied frontier electronic levels, i.e., the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), respectively. Taking/adding electrons from/to the HOMO/LUMO, which occurs during, e.g, optical absorption, charge injection, and charge transport, cause significant reorganization of the $\pi$ -electron density and hence result in bond alteration. The ionization potential (IP) and electron affinity (EA) of an OsC are hence not equal to the ground state energy of the HOMO and LUMO, respectively. The overall structural integrity of the molecules is maintained by the $\sigma$ bonds, which helps prevent bond scission during excitation and ionization processes. The HOMO and LUMO energies effectively can be modified by chemical synthesis, so there is a great freedom to tune the absorption and emission wavelengths of light, useful for applications such as photovoltaic cells[] and light emitting diodes.[] Solid films of OsC are formed mainly through intermolecular van der Waals interactions and are thus comparatively mechanically soft and hence of interest in wearable and stretchable electronic applications (especially given the light weight of OsC materials). The ability to use the OsC as inks for printed electronics manufacturingl7l is a driver for the development of many technologies, but OSC molecules can also be processed into thin films through low temperature vacuum deposition.8] The solution processability of OSCs enables "alloying' by blending different OSCs in a layer, a key concept in organic bulk heterojunction solar cells, where donor- and acceptor-type OsCs are mixed to interpenetrating networks that promote efficient charge generation and transport.tl Organic light emitting diodes for displays (cell phones, tablets, and TVs) were the first to see large-scale commercialization, but many other devices such as (printed) organic field effect transistors,[9] organic photovoltaic (OPV) cells, and organic thermoelectric generators[10] are all in various stages of development for commercial application. Also of great interest and utility is the ability to mix the OSC with (poly)electrolytes, thus combining electron- with ion-based charge transport. This facilitates realization of (opto)electrical devices with novel functionalities such as organic light emitting electrochemical cells, electrolyte-gated organic field effect transistors (EGOFET),[11] and organic electrochemical transistors.[12] The marriage of ionic and electronic charge transport can be utilized for charge storage applications, e.g., super capacitors[13] and supercapatteries[14] (a cross between a supercapacitor and a regular battery), as well as in electroactuators[15] (e.g., artificial muscles). The perhaps most exciting use of the combined electron/ion functionality of OsC is to be found in the field of organic bioelectronics.16] OSCs can capture, transport, and release ionic species, including charged (bio)molecules,17] while at the same time provide the "standard" (opto)electronic features of traditional semiconductor materials. Additionally, proteins and other bioactive substances can be attached to the surface of OsC films via bond formation (covalent or ionic), which aids signal exchange between, e.g., an (external/internal) electronic device and a biological entity.[17] OSC materials even can be introduced into living biological organisms, e.g., to act as organic electronic ion pumps,[18] delivering neurotransmitters in mammals[19] and plant hormones to control growth in plants.[20]

The toxicity of lead and the perovskite structural instability, mainly due to comparatively weak ionic bonds and resulting ionic motion, are key issues limiting large-scale application of this exciting class of soft semiconductor materials. Replacing lead with nontoxic bivalent cations while retaining the same remarkable optoelectronic properties have so far proven largely unsuccessful, however. This approach has been employed in perovskite solar cells, but even films featuring high crystalline quality,[28] suffer from nonoptimal (too large) bandgaps and comparatively high nonradiative recombination rates, the latter also a negative feature for LED applications. The exploration of suitable solution-processable lead-free semiconducting perovskites thus remains an important research direction.

The rapid development of the soft semiconductor materials and their optoelectronic devices has greatly been aided by the deployment of various characterization techniques that helped elucidate the interdependent structural, electrical, and optical properties of both films and interfaces. Precise measurement of time-dependent (opto)electronic processes are very important in, e.g., solar cell applications, which necessitates the use of "pump-probe" type experiments that are well developed and commonly used for probe energies in the optical range. Access to larger probe energies with sufficient temporal resolution that would enable a broader and deeper exploration of the soft semiconductors' dynamic material properties has until recent times been more limited. The structural softness that provides many advantages to the organic and perovskite semiconductors furthermore presents obstacles in terms of sample damage during materials characterization based on X-ray/ultraviolet photon and electron sources. The softness also greatly hinders the use of ion sputtering to obtain film-depth-dependent information from, e.g., photoelectron spectroscopy, also complicating the study of buried interfaces involving these class of materials. Another challenge common to the soft (intrinsic) semiconductor materials is sample charging due to often low conductivity of the films. Fortunately, several advances in experimental techniques are now available to help address these issues and further drive the development of these exciting materials, which is the topic of this perspective article.

# 1.1. High Energy-Resolution Inverse Photoemission Spectroscopy of Soft Semiconductors

As noted, soft organic and hybrid-organic semiconductor materials compatible with low temperature processing and even rollto-roll printing are already established in display applications and are being researched and developed for a wide variety of other uses such as energy conversion (solar, thermoelectric), bioelectronics, electronic circuits, and lighting. The field of soft semiconductor materials, however, is significantly more immature than that of the traditional inorganic semiconductors, not just in terms of technology but also basic scientific understanding. One issue is that exploring the unoccupied and occupied frontier electronic structure of semiconductor materials represents a key task for determining the material properties and understanding/predicting their functionality in optoelectronic devices. The energy of the charge transporting levels in the soft semiconductor materials are of obvious importance for design of, e.g., charge injecting contacts, electron/hole/ exciton blocking layers and exciplex-forming heterojunctions in LEDs and solar cells. The bulk heterojunction organic solar cells (BHJOSC) provide an excellent example of the importance of precise energy level alignment. The PCE of such devices has now reached over $1 6 \%$ and $1 7 \%$ for single[29] and tandem cells,[30] respectively, still less than that of the crystalline Si-based solar cells, however. The lagging PCE is mainly due to larger radiative and nonradiative voltage losses that can be traced to the structural softness of the OSC.31] To bring BHJOsCs closer to the inorganic counterparts in terms of PCE, these voltage losses must be reduced. A variety of strategies to enhance PCE by decreasing open circuit voltage loss have been demonstrated, and the perhaps most promising is the recently demonstrated approach where donor-acceptor bulk heterojunctions (BHJs) are tuned for a near-zero electron- or hole-transfer driving energy (see Figure 2) and substantially decrease the radiative voltage loss while retaining a high absorbed photon to free charge generation efficiency.132,33] The demonstrated effects are highly surprising and to better understand the physics involved and to further develop both materials and device design, precise characterization of the donor-acceptor interfaces involved is needed. Both the photon absorption energy and the donor-acceptor charge transfer exciton energy are accessible with a high degree of energetic resolution using optical and optoelectronic measurement techniques. Accurate determination of the IP and EA energies of the donors and acceptors, both in the bulk film and at the various heterojunctions, also is needed to capture and understand the whole process going from absorbed photon-- heterojunction charge transfer exciton-(collected) free charge carriers, and here suitable methods are partly lacking at present.

The research on the occupied electronic structure of semiconductor materials has undergone a revolution because of technique such as synchrotron-based photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) coupled with advances in theoretical and computational methods. Precise measurement of the unoccupied electronic structure, however, is comparatively lacking. Techniques such as optical absorption, electron energy-loss (EELS) and X-ray absorption (XAs) are used to probe unoccupied states (see Figure 3), but they measure the energy of forming an excited state, not the energy of adding an electron to unoccupied states, and hence contain an exciton binding energy component. For excitations in the optical range, the exciton binding energy of, e.g., single crystalline silicon and lead-halide perovskites is negligible, and the EA (here equal to the energy involved in adding an electron to the conduction band edge) can be accurately estimated by subtracting the optical bandgap from the IP (here equal to the energy involved in removing an electron from the valence band edge). For OsCs, the exciton binding energy, however, is large $( > 0 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ and EA estimates based on the IP and the optical bandgap hence yield substantial errors. Using cyclic voltammetry, the EA (IP) of OSC can be estimated from the reduction (oxidation) potential, but such measurements are carried out in solution and in the presence of counter ions, so the obtained EA (IP) is generally largely different from the EA (IP) of a solid OsC film as it would appear in a device, sometimes by as much as $0 . 4 { - } 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V } . ^ { [ 3 4 - 3 6 ] }$ Twophoton photoemission spectroscopy (2PPES) directly can probe the unoccupied frontier states in a solid Osc films, but only of molecules adjacent to a metal contact, and it is furthermore a time consuming, expensive, and complicated measurement technique.

In contrast, inverse photoemission (IPES) is a direct probe of the unoccupied states. Here an electron beam with a defined energy $( E _ { \mathrm { K } } )$ acts as the excitation source and the emitted photons are detected (i.e., the opposite of PES). Electrons couple to high-lying unoccupied states (an initial state with energy $E _ { \mathrm { K } } )$ undergo radiative transitions to a low-lying unoccupied state (a final state energy $E _ { \mathrm { B _ { \ell } } }$ thereby releasing photons with energy $h \nu ,$ i.e., $h \nu = E _ { \mathrm { K } } - E _ { \mathrm { B } }$ . The photons emitted in the decay process are analyzed as photon counts versus incident electron energy. The generated energy distribution of this radiation gives direct information on the energy distribution of the unoccupied states. The today typical IPES systems use electron beams that produce emitted photons in the vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) range. Detection of the VUV photons are generally carried out in Bremsstrahlung isochromatic (BIS) mode where the yield of emitted photons of constant energy $( h \nu )$ is measured as a function of incident electron energy $( E _ { \mathrm { K } } )$ using a basic setup that features an iodine-gas-filled Geiger-Muller (GM) tube with a $\mathrm { C a F } _ { 2 }$ plate installed in ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) to avoid the emitted VUV photons being absorbed by oxygen. This detection setup works as a bandpass filter centered at $9 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ with a bandpass width of $0 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ A bandpass width of $0 . 4 \mathrm { e V }$ can be achieved by replacing $\mathrm { C a F } _ { 2 }$ with $\mathrm { S r F } _ { 2 } [ 3 7 ]$ and there are even reports claiming a bandpass width below $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ by combining acetone gas and a $\mathrm { S r } _ { 0 . 7 } \mathrm { C a } _ { 0 . 3 } \mathrm { F } _ { 2 }$ plate.[38] Nevertheless, due to several fundamental drawbacks conventional IPES has, however, largely failed to make a significant impact in field of OsC. Most OsCs are very sensitive to damage from higher-energy electrons $( > 5 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ , and photon detection in the VUV range is thus problematic as this dictates an electron beam energy of $5 { - } 1 5 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ given the typical EA values of $2 { - } 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ of OSC films. This problem is amplified by another fundamental drawback of IPES, the low count rate. The cross-section ratio between IPES $( \sigma _ { \mathrm { I P E S } } )$ and PES $( \sigma _ { \mathrm { P E S } } )$ is the square of the ratio of the electron $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { e l } } )$ and photon $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { p h } } )$ wavelength which yields a five orders of magnitude lower crosssection for IPES than PES, dramatically increasing the risk of sample damage during measurement! This has restricted the use of IPES to just a few OSC molecular systems known for their stability. The VUV photon range has additional problems as the detection must be integrated into the vacuum system and the energy resolution of the commonly used bandpass filters is poor. High-resolution grating spectrometers combined with high-gain photocurrent detectors also have been applied as photon detectors in IPEs,[39-41] but have suffered from problems such as small collection angle of the emitted photons, low reflectivity of the gratings and high cost compared to the GMbased bandpass detector approach. Hence, GM-based bandpass detection is still the most widely used detector system in IPES today. Finally, the overall energy resolution of IPES is not only determined by the energy resolution of the detector $( 0 . 4 \mathrm { - } 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the GM approach), but also by the energy spread of the electrons source, the latter being equal to $2 . 5 ~ k T$ as all currently used electron sources in IPES use thermal emission from low work function materials. The normally used electron beams in IPES generated from tungsten filaments have an energy spread of about $0 . 5 \mathrm { e V } ,$ i.e., on order of the GM detector resolution.

Fortunately, pioneering developments of the IPES technique has significantly reduced the problems of sample damage while greatly improving the energy resolution, the latter having realistic hopes of becoming on par with ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy in the future. This new technique, called lowenergy inverse photoemission spectroscopy (LEIPs), features an unprecedented total energy resolution $( < 0 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ and has been demonstrated to obtain precise EA values $( < \pm 0 . 1$ eV) with negligible sample damage effects for a series of thin films of OSC molecules and even polymers.42-44] The LEIPS technique achieves its enhanced functionality through several different improvements upon the common IPES approach. The electron beam is generated using a BaO coated disk cathode that can operate at a lower temperature than the standard tungsten version, decreasing the kinetic energy spread by a factor of two. The kinetic energy is also reduced to $0 { - } 4 ~ \mathrm { e V } ,$ thereby bringing it below the typical threshold energy for electron beam induced sample damage. The reduced energy of the incident electron beam mandates that the photodetection is shifted to the near-ultraviolet visible (NUv-vis) range $1 8 0 { - } 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ n m } )$ which has several advantages. NUV-vis light can be transferred and detected in air, enabling cheaper and more flexible design choices. Furthermore, there is a wide selection of optical bandpass filters in the NUV-vis range with high resolution $( \approx 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ yielding about ${ \approx } 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ bandpass width for detected photons centered at $4 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V } _ { i }$ and transmittance in the bandpass can be more than $65 \%$ in the NUV range and up to $80 \%$ in the visible range, thus dramatically improving energy resolution while improving detection efficiency and hence reducing sample damage. If energy resolution is the key factor, the NUV-vis enables easy deployment of high-resolution spectrometers instead of optical bandpass filters, though this reduces the detection efficiency. In tunable photon energy mode, electrons of fixed energy impinge on the sample and the resulting emitted photon energy spectrum is measured using a spectrograph, which are readily available for photon energies in the NUV-vis range.

The introduction of LEIPs has brought the possibility of precise EA measurement for "normal' OsC molecules and polymers that previously could not even be studied using regular IPES due to sample damage. By providing the missing piece to the energy level diagram puzzle, i.e., the energy of the electron accepting/transporting state in OSC materials, LEIPS is set to pave the way for a hugely improved understanding and design of charge injection, generation and transport processes in present and future (opto)electronic devices. The continued improvement of the LEIPS technique and increased use thereof in the field of soft semiconductor materials (also metal halide perovskites suffer from low-energy electron sample damage) is thus one of the more exciting developments in the study of soft semiconductor materials.

# 1.2. Hard X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (HAXPES) of Soft Semiconductors

A great number of novel and useful material characterization techniques have been made available through large infrastructure facilities featuring, e.g., third and fourth generation synchrotron radiation rings and X-ray free electron lasers, of vast benefit to research and development in material science. The advancement made in the quality of X-ray light produced, combined with substantial development of the vacuum and photoelectron detection systems, is also paying dividends for the established techniques such as PES. Here, hard X-rays, especially in the so-called "tender" range $( 2 { - } 7 \ \mathrm { k e V } )$ [45] has proven highly useful for use in study of soft semiconductor materials.[46,47]

The use of hard X-ray PEs,[48] often called HAXPES, for study of soft semiconductor materials may seem counterintuitive, given that the recoil energy induced shifts in the measured binding energy is inversely proportional to the mass of the element while proportional to the kinetic energy of the photoemitted electron, and both OsC and metal halide perovskites typically feature light elements such as carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. By using X-rays in the tender range, however, the size of the shifts is limited to ${ \approx } 0 . 1 { - } 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ even for light elements such as carbon and nitrogen,49] while the kinetic energies of electrons emitted from frontier orbitals and the shallow core levels typical of light elements are sufficiently high to yield inelastic mean free paths large enough to probe bulk-like film properties and buried layers/interfaces or operate at (near) ambient pressure (see Figure 4). The extended probing depth afforded by HAXPES is highly useful for soft semiconductors, as sputterbased depth profiling generally is unsuitable. This is particularly the case for metal halide perovskites, as films of these materials typically are fabricated from solution and cannot be formed layer-by-layer in situ or ex situ, which is the approach commonly used to study buried interfaces and depth-dependence of electronic structure in OsC films. An illustrative example is a recent study where lead halide perovskite thin films of the type $\mathrm { C s _ { 0 . 0 6 } F A _ { 0 . 7 9 } M A _ { 0 . 1 5 } P b ( I _ { 0 . 8 5 } B r _ { 0 . 1 5 } ) _ { 3 } }$ (MA: methyl-ammonium, FA: formamidinium) fabricated from precursor solutions diluted with potassium iodide to provide excess concentration of iodine for reducing halide vacancy defects.[50] Here, HAXPES in the tender range was used to obtain a (nondestructive) depth profile of the elemental composition showing the formation of a potassium halide rich layer on the film surface and grain boundaries where they help passivate defects and inhibit halide migration. Other recent examples of the utility of HAXPES include nondestructive depth-dependent studies of doping concentration and electronic structure in OSC hole-conducting layers such as spiroOMeTAD,[51] and of stoichiometry of lead-free perovskite films for solar cell applications,[52] where again layer-by-layer growth of model systems where not an option. Finally, PES yields a partial density of occupied states where the cross-section dependence on incident photon energy differs dramatically between different orbitals and between different elements. Hence, the ability to vary the incident photon energy in the PES experiment all the way from the UV range into the tender X-ray range enables a much clearer analysis of the occupied frontier electronic structure that can be of complex mixed organometallic origin in many of the typically used metal halide perovskites.

A very exciting recent study demonstrated the ability of AP-XPS to probe the in situ formation of an electrochemical double layer (EDL) in an electrolyte liquid film deposited on a gold electrode.56] The interdependent electrical potential and structural reorganization of the electrolyte film that result in an EDL formation upon external polarization is extremely important not only for general electrochemistry but also is a key component in OSC technologies such as EGOFETs, super capacitors, and supercapatteries. Using $4 . 0 \mathrm { k e V }$ X-rays yielding an inelastic mean free path of ${ \approx } 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for their AP-XPS studies, the authors managed to directly observe and track the induced potential drop in a series of thin $( 1 0 { - } 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ water-based electrolyte films containing different electrolyte concentration. The potential of zero charge as well as the EDL potential profile were obtained by measuring the evolution of the core level features (binding energy shifts and peak broadening) upon applied bias, which enabled determination of how the EDL layer depends on both external bias and electrolyte concentration.[57] AP-XPS is also a useful tool for probing catalytic reaction under realistic conditions, be it at solid-gas or solid-liquid interfaces. For instance, the change in chemical state of the elemental species on the catalytic surface can be probed during exposure to the relevant gas and at elevated temperatures as, e.g., recently demonstrated for inorganic catalytic nanoparticles.[58] For OsCs, many interesting topics exist where similar experiments could help shed light on the processes involved. For instance, (tender range) AP-XPS of photo- and electro-stimulated oxygen reduction reactions at OsC-water interfaces could profitably be studied in order to in detail elucidate the catalytic reactions that typically either follow two-electron processes producing peroxidel9] or four-electron processes producing water.[60] HAXPES and especially the AP-XPS version offer many advantages for the study of soft semiconductor films and interfaces and is now available to a wider user community due to recent and ongoing developments at synchrotron radiation facilities worldwide. The increased and broader use of these techniques would greatly benefit both the basic research efforts and the more applied areas of research involving soft semiconductors.

High-Order Harmonic Generation of Soft X-Rays: In highorder harmonic generation (HHG) process, see Figure 5, typically (but not exclusively(61,62) noble gas atoms are tunnel ionized by an intense laser pulse, i.e., bound electrons tunnel into the continuum through atomic potential barriers that are suppressed by the laser field.163,64] These electrons are then accelerated by the electric field of the laser thereby gaining energy that is subsequently released as a higher-frequency photon when the electrons are recaptured by their respective ions. If the laser pulse (typically in the femtosecond range) and the higher-frequency photons propagate through the gas in phase (called phase matching), the high-frequency photons from many individual atoms in the gas add up coherently, generating the emission of an intense HHG pulse of substantially higher photon energies than that of the driving laser pulse. The HHG generates a distribution of photon energies creating a spectrum containing different harmonic orders where the highest obtained photon energy, $h \nu _ { \mathrm { m a x } } ,$ is equal to $I _ { \mathrm { p } } + 3 . 1 7 \ U _ { \mathrm { p } }$ where $I _ { \mathrm { p } }$ is the ionization potential of the gas used in the HHG process and $U _ { \mathfrak { p } }$ is the ponderomotive energy of the electron involved in the tunnel ionization process, i.e., its kinetic energy from oscillating in the laser field, averaged over a period. $U _ { \mathrm { p } }$ scales with laser intensity and with the square of the laser wavelength, so going towards driving lasers with higher pulse intensities and/or longer wavelengths will extend the range of available photon energies.[64] The use of HHG driven by intense coherent femtosecond light pulses to realize desktop synchrotrons[65-67] ideal for time-resolved PES and XAS experiments thus has long been seen as the future, and for a while seemed destined to remain so. The recent decade, however, has seen tremendous developments in both the HHG process and the laser technology that it relies on, where in the latter case the introduction of optical parametric chirpedpulse amplifiers (OPCPA) has been instrumental in increasing average pulse power (pulse energy and repetition rate) as well as tunability of wavelengths as compared to the traditional HHG source (Ti:sapphire lasers). The demonstration of intense coherent HHG femtosecond light pulses spanning the energy range of ${ \approx } 2 0 0 { \ - } 6 0 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and even $3 0 0 { - } 1 6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ for driving laser wavelengths of 2 and $3 . 9 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ respectively was a particularly important achievement as it extended the available photon energies into the core level $/$ absorption edge regions for many important elements.[64]

Significant efforts in recent years have been invested in building HHG desktop synchrotrons and to further develop the HHG technique to provide circular polarized light[68] useful in, e.g., X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) experiments and to push the available photon energies deeper into the X-ray region at stable "everyday" lab-use conditions. With photon energies extending just up to $6 0 0 \ \mathrm { e V } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ however, all relevant core levels / absorption edges of OsC can be accessed and for C 1s $/ \mathrm { C }$ K-edge PES/XAS the photon energy range must only extend slightly passed $3 0 0 \mathrm { e V } .$ For many of the metal halide perovskites, photon energies of up to $7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ are enough. Hence OPCPA driven HHG desktop synchrotrons are highly suitable photon sources for the soft semiconductor materials as the requirements for photon energy range are not so demanding. The pulsed nature of the HHG light sources are also useful for study of the soft semiconductors, in particular the OSC, as the in general poor conductivity of the films (in particular the thicker single crystalline films) often yields sample charging problems during PES experiments. The cycling of the light pulses provides time for the sample surface to be neutralized before the next light pulse and photoelectron emission event. The short pulse duration, typically in the femtosecond range though attosecond pulses are possible, enables pump-probe type time-resolved PES and XAS experiments to study dynamic processes in the soft semiconductor materials, e.g., following the evolution of the new occupied states created during optical excitation, charge transfer exciton formation and eventual conversion into free charges at donor-acceptor OSC interfaces in organic solar cells. A recent study on a typical donor polymer used in organic solar cells provided a first step in this direction, where the population of previously unoccupied orbitals caused by optical excitation was studied in terms of both electron binding energy and time evolution, demonstrating a 500 fs relaxation of the lowest excited state a much faster relaxation of 50 fs for the higher exited state.[69] The ultrashort pulses combined with a fairly high repetition rate, typically $\mathrm { k H z }$ to $\mathrm { M H z }$ , make the HHG light source ideal for combining with socalled time-of-flight (TOF)70,71] and angle-resolved time-of-flight (ARTOF)[72] detectors, as the energy resolution (and angular resolution for ARTOF) is enhanced at lower kinetic energies and lower pulse lengths. The TOF and ARTOF analyzers also feature excellent transmission of the photoemitted electrons to the detector, which together with the pulsed operation helps limit the sample damage typical of soft semiconductor films.

In conclusion, we have discussed soft semiconductors, an intriguing class of materials that feature a wide variety of functionalities useful in opto- and bioelectronic applications including low-temperature solution processing of thin films, charge conduction by electrons and ions and tunable bandgaps in the optical and near-infrared regions.
