# Ordering of conjugated polymer molecules: recent advances and perspectives

Yifan Yao,ab Huanli Dong\*a and Wenping Hu\*a

Received 25th January 2013   
Accepted 15th February 2013

DOI: 10.1039/c3py00131h

Molecular order and orientation significantly influence the physical properties of organic materials and their corresponding device properties. Therefore ordering organic molecules especially conjugated polymer molecules in the active layer has been a hot topic in organic electronics for high performance organic electronics and remarkable progress has been demonstrated recently. The purpose of this minireview is to highlight the novel strategies for controlling conjugated polymer molecule orders in the solid state, including rational chemical design, optimizing solution-processing techniques and growth of high quality polymer crystalline micro/nanostructures. Moreover, the charge transport mechanisms in conjugated polymers and the relationship of oriented polymer molecules with their field-effect properties are also discussed. Finally, a concise conclusion and perspective of highly ordered conjugated polymer molecules in organic electronics are also addressed.

# 1 Introduction

Compared with the traditional inorganic semiconductors, which need complicated processing methods with expensive equipment and harsh conditions, organic semiconductors as a new kind of semiconducting materials are generally obtained through chemical methods with a variety of easy-cutting structures and good solution-processability, demonstrating their potential applications for the next generation of exible electronic systems.1–6 Since the rst report of organic semiconductors in the late 1940s,7 over the past decades remarkable progress has been achieved in their applications in various elds, such as organic light emitting diodes (OLEDs),8,9 organic eld-effect transistors (OFETs)10–12 and organic solar cells (OSCs),13,14 etc. Considerable research has demonstrated that, as the core component for all organic optoelectronic devices, the properties of the organic active layer including molecular ordering and orientation in the crystalline domains and lm morphology signicantly inuence the charge transport properties and the overall device performances.15–17 Thus, controlling mesoscale and nanoscale ordering of organic semiconductors with desired molecular alignment in the active layer is essential for efficient carrier transport to afford high performance devices.

Organic semiconductors include organic small molecules and conjugated polymers. Low weight and low cost are their common characteristics. Specically, conjugated polymers possess many unique features: (i) high molecular weight; (ii) better exibility owing to the long molecular chain, which is different from the fragile small molecules, (iii) mechanical exibility and lm forming ability facilitating for large-area industrial processing. On the other hand, exible chain segments of conjugated polymers are easily tangled and large free energy barriers are needed to be overcome when the polymer chains tend to spontaneously form ordered states.

Additionally, the high molecular weight and amount of intermolecular interactions for conjugated polymers make the molecular ordering process more complex and unpredictable. So, it is a scientic challenge to control polymer molecular ordering with desired alignment in the solid state and intensive research has been carried out in this eld.18–20 In this minireview, we will focus on the recent achievements in controlling the ordering of conjugated polymer molecules and lm morphologies for high performance OFETs to guide the subsequent research in this eld of organic electronics. For better optimizing device performances from the point of ordering molecular orientation, rstly the charge transport mechanism in conjugated polymers is discussed in Section 2. In Section 3, various techniques usually used to orient conjugated polymer molecules in OFETs are further illustrated in detail with their advantages and limitations. Finally, a concise conclusion and perspective is also included.

# 2 Charge transport mechanisms in conjugated polymers

In order to better optimize device performance, it is necessary to fully understand the charge transport mechanisms in conjugated polymers. Generally speaking, charge carriers always transport between the neighboring delocalized $\pi$ -orbitals, so that in this case if there are not many obstacles existing between them ultrahigh charge carrier mobilities can be achieved logically, just like that in organic single crystals for the formation of energy bands owing to good $\pi - \pi$ orbital overlap.21,22 With conjugated polymers, the weak van der Waals force interactions between local $\pi \cdot$ -orbitals, means the transport of charge carriers is mainly realized by a slow phonon assisted hopping mechanism.23–26

To better illustrate the exchange of charge carriers between interchain $\pi$ -orbitals, it is assumed that neighboring polymer molecules adopt a lamellae face-to-face packing model (as shown in Fig. 1a). Under the inuence of an external electric eld, charge carriers can transport along in three different ways. (1) The rst way is along the conjugated polymer chain direction, which has the quickest transport properties due to the delocalized conjugated $\boldsymbol \pi$ -orbitals extending along the whole polymer backbone. Unfortunately, the existence of physical or chemical defects generally damages the continuity of the delocalized $\pi$ -orbital along the polymer chain and then severely hinders the efficient intrachain carrier transfer. On the other hand, if we could obtain high-planarity, high-purity and zero defect polymer chains, with the increase in conjugated length rather high charge carrier mobilities along the polymer chains and distinguished anisotropy would be achieved, which are also a signicant difference of conjugated polymers from organic small molecules. (2) Another transport way is along the $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction, which is theoretically lower than that along the conjugation direction. However, in fact most of the charge carriers in conjugated polymers transport through hopping to neighboring $\boldsymbol \pi$ -orbitals, which is mainly dependent on two factors: conjugated length and $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance.27 (3) The third charge transport direction is along the chain-to-chain direction (Fig. 1a, alkyl stacking direction), which is also the most difficult way, because the long alkyl chains actually act as an insulator layer as well as the increased chain-to-chain distance induced by the effect of side chain steric hindrance which signicantly hinder the charge carriers transport hopping to neighboring molecules.28 From the above discussions it is clear that in conjugated polymers the charge carrier transport is mainly determined by two-dimensional transport channels, i.e. intrachain and interchain directions. Thus, reduced charge traps along the polymer chain and formation of highly ordered as well as intact $\pi - \pi$ stacking in the solid state are crucial for efficient charge carrier transport to afford high performance polymer optoelectronic devices.29–35

# 3 Ordering conjugated polymer molecules in the solid state

The most attractive advantage of conjugated polymers is their good solution lm-forming properties, demonstrating promising use in a wide variety of next generation technologies including displays, solid-state lighting, transistors and organic photovoltaics etc.1,14 However, on the other hand, it is also a challenging task to well control the resulting lm morphologies as well as their molecular aggregation structures, which are largely affected by many factors, such as the organic solvents, solution-processes, substrate properties as well as the environmental conditions. Although amorphous lms are less sensitive to solution-processing methods and do not need complicated aer treatment, low charge transport properties are generally present due to the random molecular orientation and existence of many traps.36–39 Compared with amorphous polymer lms, semicrystalline polymer lms usually exhibit superior eldeffect transistor performance because of better macroscopic molecular ordering and effective $\pi - \pi$ interactions between neighbouring molecules.40–43 Research has demonstrated that there are mainly two kinds of stacking models: face-on and edge-on in the crystalline region (as shown in Fig. 1b and c), which are applicable to different kinds of device congurations depending on the charge carrier transport direction. For example, in eld-effect transistors, the molecules are better organized with the edge-on orientation model for high device performance due to the efficient two-dimensional charge transport along the conjugation direction and $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction. However, grain boundaries existing between adjacent crystalline regions and acting as charge trapping sites signi- cantly disturb the charge transport and lead to low eld-effect mobilities.44 Therefore, good control of molecular orders in polymer lms with large crystalline regions as well as reduced boundary traps is essential for achieving high charge carrier transport properties. In the following sections, there will be more discussions on the various strategies for ordering conjugated polymer molecules with the desired alignment in the solid state, including their advantages, limitations and applications in organic electronics.

# 3.1 Rational chemical design and synthesis strategy

Rational chemical design and synthesis is a direct way to control molecular orders by adjusting their self-assembly properties with suitable chemical structures. When polymer is deposited on substrates the best situation is forming a close face-to-face stacking and ordering arrangement, which is very difficult to achieve because of many inevitable factors inuencing molecule self-assembly.45 For example, a planar conjugated monomer has the largest delocalized $\boldsymbol \pi$ -orbital and intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ interactions. When they are polymerized in solution, long alkyl side chains need to be introduced for good solubility. However, the introduced long alkyl chains may weaken intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking and result in at molecular plane torsion, which is unfavorable for carrier transport. Thus, selection of solubilizing side chains on the conjugated polymer structure is an art of balance. Recently, Bao et al.46 departed from the conventional approach and focused on introducing a novel hybrid 1,1,1,3,5,5,5-heptamethyltrisiloxane terminal unit as solubilizing chains in the polymer. By incorporating this siloxane-terminated solubilizing side chain in isoindigo-based conjugated polymer, hole mobility was boosted to $2 . 0 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ compared with a traditional branched alkyl side chain where only $0 . 5 7 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved, because of the closer $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance. More recently, with isoindigobased conjugated polymers as an example, Pei et al.47,48 carried out systematic research on the inuence of building block symmetry and alkyl side chains on the molecular packing model and the resulting device performance. It was found that polymers with centrosymmetric units and the branching position at the fourth carbon in the alkyl chain exhibited better crystallinity and lamellar packing with good interchain $\pi - \pi$ stacking in thin lms, leading to higher eld-effect mobilities. An exceptionally high mobility of $3 . 6 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for isoindigo-based conjugated polymers. This molecular design strategy provides us a novel concept for further developing high performance eld effect polymer semiconductors.

Poly(3-alkylthiophenes) (P3ATs) as excellent conjugated polymers have attracted much scientic interest. The long alkyl plays an important role in balancing the intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking and thiophene backbone planarity. The position of the alkyl side chain on the thiophene backbone is important. If the alkyl chains do not follow any particular order along the polymer chain in a regiorandom case, the backbones are twisted and impaired molecular $\boldsymbol \pi$ -orbital overlap results in an amorphous lm.49 When alkyl chains are introduced into the thiophene units in a regioregular fashion, the interdigitated long alkyl chains bring in closer $\pi - \pi$ stacking and more crystalline regions in polymer thin lms.50,51

For the purpose of forming ordered conjugated polymer structures, chemical design and changing different side chains is not enough, molecular weight is another factor needed to be taken into consideration. Different to small conjugated molecules, the molecular weight of a polymer can adjust the molecular stacking ordering and thus the eld-effect performance. Taking P3HT as an example, the molecular weight of regioregular P3HT has a signicant impact on lm morphology and hole mobilities. Low molecular weight of regioregular P3HT is prone to form more ordered crystalline bers with polymer chains arranged in the face-to-face model. Low eld-effect mobility is demonstrated because of the pronounced grain boundary sites existing between the highly crystalline bers. While for high molecular weight regioregular P3HT, nodular structures were demonstrated but with three orders of magnitude greater mobility and enhanced OPV cell efficiency due to a well-connected network in lms (as shown in Fig. 2).52,53 So from the above-mentioned aspects, it is clear that rational chemical design and synthesis of conjugated polymers with suitable side chains, high regioregularity and high molecular weight is crucial for the formation of well-ordered molecular orientations for high device performance.

# 3.2 Techniques for alignment of conjugated polymers

Besides rational chemical design of polymer molecules, appropriate lm processing techniques are also necessary for enforcing polymer chains to orient in long range orders following molecule self-assembly processes. The aim of these various approaches is mainly to solve three aspects of problems: (1) enlarging crystalline regions; (2) reducing grain boundaries between neighboring crystalline domains; (3) optimizing the molecule packing model with desired orientation alignment and effective intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ overlap.

3.2.1 Solution-processing methods. Drop-casting, spincoating, dip-coating, and solution-shearing processing are the usually used solution-processing methods for polymer lm formation. Amongst them, drop-casting is the easiest device fabricating process, but the coffee stain effect makes polymer solutions form non-uniform thin lms, since the solvent rst evaporates from the edges and shrinks to the centre nally forming concentric rings (as shown in Fig. 3a). In contrast, spincoating can fabricate relatively uniform thin lms, but usually fails in forming large ordering of molecules, because during this process the solvent generally evaporates too fast to allow enough time for polymer chain self-assembly of large crystalline domains. For this point, the boiling point of the solvent has a great inuence on the resulting lm morphologies and device performances. Sirringhaus et al.54 found that low boiling point chloroform had rapid evaporation with limited time for P3HT crystallization during the spin-coating process, while a solvent with a high boiling point like 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene could dramatically improve the eld-effect mobilities by an order of magnitude by better molecular ordering and alignment in crystalline needle-like microstructures in thin lms due to the sufficient time for the self-assembly process. However, later on, Bao et al.55 found contrary result with the same regioregular P3HT as the active semiconducting layer formed by the dropcasting method with different solvents. They found that using the drop-casting process P3HT molecules adopted a lamellar $\pi -$ $\pi$ stacking model in the lms, and the eld-effect mobility was $\mathrm { C H C l } _ { 3 } > \mathrm { T H F } \gg$ toluene $> \mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ . Although in high boiling point solvents (e.g., toluene) P3HT lms formed better nanobrillar crystalline domains, lower eld-effect mobilities were exhibited, probably due to distinct spherulitic grain boundaries (as shown in Fig. 3b). While with $\mathrm { C H C l } _ { 3 }$ as the solvent, lms with node-like bril structures exhibited the highest eld-effect mobility due to the highly interconnected nanobrillar networks. From the above discussion it is clear that high boiling point solvents generally favour the formation of highly ordered nanostructures in the solid state but it does not mean that high carrier transport properties could be achieved because of the existence of grain boundary. So that according to different solution-processes, we should consider all the inuential aspects for efficient carrier transport and high device performance.

The limitations of the drop-casting and spin-coating processes are that they cannot exactly control lm thickness, smoothness and uniformity, and more importantly they fail in enforcing polymer chain orientation and alignment in one direction. Dip-coating and solution-shearing processes could overcome the above-mentioned weaknesses by controlling the solvent evaporation rate and incorporating shear force, so further enhanced lm macroscopic ordering could be obtained. In the dip-coating process, the substrate is immersed into the solution and at the solution's surface is formed a meniscus with the substrate. By pulling the substrate out of the solution at a certain rate, the solvent evaporation and material crystallization can be controlled, resulting in alignment of the molecules in the dip-coating direction (as shown in Fig. 3c). Thickness of dipcoated lms, dip-coating speed and solution concentration of the polymer all inuence the lms morphology, molecule ordering and eld-effect transistor performances. For example, each regioregular P3HT solution concentration is corresponding to an optimal dip coating rate.56 Moreover, the surface energy of the substrate also inuences the molecule deposition during the dip-coating process. Sandberg et al.57 found that when a high surface energy substrate like $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ was dipped into low concentration regioregular P3HT solutions, a stable monolayer ultrathin lm with thickness of approximately $2 0 \textup { \AA }$ was obtained with well-ordered molecular packing structures, which adopted a Stranski–Krastanov-type growth mechanism in the initial monolayer.57,58 Additionally, the microstructure of dip-coated lms is also very sensitive to the self-organization processing of the macromolecules when dipped from the liquid phase to the solid phase. Grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was conducted on dip-coated CDT–BTZ– C16 lms. Experimental results indicated that repeated polymer chain backbones presented in the aligned bers direction, and the lm morphology was totally different from that obtained by spin-casting with better lm ordering and higher mobilities.59 More recently, Mullen ¨ et.al.60 fabricated a conjugated polymer poly(2,5-bis(3-alkylthiophen-2-yl)thieno[3,2-b]thiophene) (PBTTT) lm through this dip-coating method. Long and thick bers were formed on top of a layer of PBTTT (as shown in Fig. 3d). Results demonstrated that the rst polymer monolayer played an important role in the microstructure evolution into the bulk lm during solution processing and the charge carriers transport properties in the transistors. Signicant improvement of mobilities occurs only at a critical multilayer network density. With a low dip-coating rate, the polymer chains are uniaxially oriented, yielding pronounced structural anisotropy and high charge carrier mobilities of $1 . 3 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ along the alignment direction. The dip-coating method can also be used for a SWCNTs and poly(9,9-dioctyluorene-co-bithiophene) (F8T2) composite. Compared to spin-coated lms it not only eliminated hysteresis but also showed higher eld-effect mobilities, high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } }$ and large $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ .61

The solution-shearing deposition technique is another promising method to fabricate highly ordered solution-processed thin lms with excellent device performance (as shown in Fig. 3e). Solution-shearing originated from doctor-blading, which was further used for the fabrication of lms of polymers, inorganic materials and organic semiconductors.62,63 Bao et al.64 rst proposed this technique for organic semiconductors and successfully improved the mobility of TIPS–pentacene thin lm transistors from $0 . 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to an extremely high value of $4 . 6 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ by incorporating crystal strains,65 and later this technique has also been applied in some n-type small conjugated molecules.66–68

Recently, Lee et al.69 utilized this technique to fabricate ambipolar polydiketopyrrolopyrrole thin lms. The polymer lms were rst prepared by solution deposition and then with a thermal annealing process to remove tert-butoxycarbonyl groups, and the deprotected lms could further form a hydrogen-bonded network. The solution-shearing technique in this study was used to help the formation of a preferential intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network, and an array of unidirectional valleys were observed aer thermal treatment at $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (as shown in Fig. 3f). Ambipolar charge transport properties with hole and electron mobilities of $1 . 3 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $2 . 6 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively were achieved, which was about one order of magnitude higher than that of drop-cast lms.

3.2.2 Solvent–vapor annealing. Solvent–vapor annealing is another effective way to improve the crystallization of solutionprocessed organic molecules in the solid state by providing a second self-assembly process under a certain vapour atmosphere of solvent.70,71 During this process selecting a suitable solvent is crucial, because the properties of the solvent including boiling point, polarity and solubility parameter strongly inuence molecular self-organization and molecular ordering.72 Di et al., 73 introduced this solvent–vapor annealing into conjugated polymer systems followed by a heating process and a 2–4 fold improvement in eld-effect mobilities was achieved compared to that without such a process, demonstrating efficient charge transport properties due to the ordered molecular packing in the treated polymer active thin lms. Recently, Liu et al.74 further carried out a systematic investigation of the effect of solvent–vapor annealing on polymer eld-effect transistor performance by adjusting the solvent polarity, solubility parameter and treatment time, etc. It was found that only when the solubility parameter and polarity of the solvent matched that of the polymer semiconductors, good lm morphologies and ordered molecular packing could be obtained for excellent charge transport properties. Moreover, for different polymer systems and lm thicknesses, the solvent annealing time should also be optimized accordingly.

3.2.3 Uniaxial orientation techniques. As mentioned before, conjugated polymers exhibit distinguished intrinsic anisotropic charge transport properties. Along the conjugated polymer chain (Fig. 1a conjugation direction) the fastest photoresponse and carrier movement are demonstrated. Thus, uniaxial orientation of polymer chain backbones enables maximizing of charge carrier mobility with better orbital overlaps and chain alignment along the direction of current ow. Mechanical stretching and rubbing are usually used uniaxial orientation techniques for alignment of materials including insulator polymers, conjugated polymers and organic semiconductors. Gustafsson et al.,75 found that uniaxially oriented poly(3-octylthiophene) (P3OT) lms could be fabricated by spincoating P3OT solution on polyethylene foil and then stretching the lm together with the foil. Anisotropic mobility was observed, but with very low mobility values. Sirringhaus et al.76 further reported the alignment of liquid crystal (LC) molecules poly(9,9-dioctyluorene-co-bithiophene) (F8T2) on rubbed polyimide (PI) substrate. Aer the LC phase annealed in a nitrogen atmosphere, F8T2 polymer chains in lms aligned to the rubbing direction of the underlying layer were obtained. The anisotropy of the eld-effect mobility was 5–8 fold varying between devices parallel or perpendicular to the alignment direction, demonstrating faster carrier transport along the conjugated polymer chain than that of interchain hopping. Recently, Dong et al.77 further extended this facile method for the fabrication of oriented rigid rod conjugated polymer lms. By drop-casting a poly $\dot { p }$ -phenylene ethynylene) with thioacetyl end (TA-PPE) solution on a friction-transferred PTFE substrate, highly ordered TA-PPE lms with conjugated backbones parallel to the PTFE sliding direction were formed. Compared with amorphous thin lms, the photoresponsive properties of the ordered thin lms were largely enhanced with the polymer chain orientated along the direction of current ow.

The shortcomings of mechanical stretching or rubbing methods are that it is difficult to precisely control the strength and sliding rate on the manual rubbing substrate, which usually resulted in large groove width and non-uniform depth. Accuracy and repeatability is not good enough, and even worse, oen leads to poor thin lm continuity and secondary thin lm degree of orientation. To solve these problems, Brinkmann and Wittmann78 used molecular aromatic crystals for nucleating and oriented surfaces were further used for alignment of polymer molecules. For example, by using a P3HT–1,3,5-trichlorobenzene (TCB) mixture solution, the semi-crystalline structures of P3HT that were oriented by directional solidication on TCB were formed with a typical edge-on lamellar morphology (as shown in Fig. 4). Recently, Muller ¨ et al.79 reported a one-step preparation of thin lms with pronounced in-plane alignment. By using TCB and a second carrier solvent chlorobenzene, P3HT rst epitaxially crystallized on TCB crystals. Subsequently through sublimation of the TCB solvent, highly ordered thin lms with square-centimeter-sized domains were obtained. This study offers a feasible method for uniaxial orientation of semi-crystalline conjugated polymer systems.

3.2.4 Langmuir–Blodgett technique. The Langmuir–Blodgett (LB) technique provides a unique approach to prepare ordered ultrathin lms with a high degree of thickness control, low number of defects, and self-organization at the molecular scale. Paloheimo et al.80 rst fabricated regioirregular P3HT LB lms with thicknesses ranging from a monolayer to some ten monolayers, but carrier mobilities of only ${ 1 0 } ^ {  { - } 7 }$ to $1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were obtained due to the irregular side chain arrangement causing steric hindrance and preventing polymer molecules forming effective conjugation and ordered $\pi - \pi$ stacking. Xu et al.81 improved this method and regioregular P3HT was used to fabricate LB lms. Because regioregular P3HT molecules were used, an edge-on conformation of polymer molecules formed with an enhanced eld-effect mobility of $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ . Generally, it is very difficult to produce neat P3HT LB lms because the polymers cannot form stable Langmuir lms at the air–water interface that could be successfully transferred to solid substrates with a good quality. Recently, Olivati et al.82 fabricated neat P3HT LB lms by using appropriate deposition conditions. In these LB lms, polymer chains appeared to be aligned in the dipping direction, and exhibited excellent optoelectronic anisotropy. Different from the LB lms of P3HT, an ultrathin lm of poly[2- methoxy-5-(20-ethylhexyloxy)-1,4-phenylenevinylene] (MEH-PPV) prepared by a modied LB method adopted a face-on texture on the substrate.83 This modied LB method induced uniaxial alignments of the MEH-PPV polymer main chains by the compression of the lm with a barrier on the water surface. Mobility anisotropies were also clearly observed, and quasi-onedimensional charge transport along the polymer main chain demonstrated. The strong anisotropy was due to the weak charge transport across the interchain direction, of which side chains acted as an insulating layer for charge carriers transport.

# 3.3 Growth of high quality crystalline micro/nanostructures

Although different methods have been applied to order conjugated polymer molecules in lms, inevitable large amounts of defects and grain boundaries dramatically affect the further improvement of device performance and the access of intrinsic properties of materials. In comparison, organic crystals exhibit perfect long-range molecular packing, free grain boundaries and minimized defects, and are the best candidates for excellent device performance.84–86 It has been a big challenge to grow high quality conjugated polymer crystal due to their high molecular weight and complex intermolecular interactions. These highly organized polymer crystals can help us answer questions about the mechanism of charge transport in conjugated polymers and estimate their intrinsic charge transport properties. The literature on polymer and semiconductor single crystals or highly crystalline self-assembled nanostructures isscarce.87 Flexible chain segments in conjugated polymers are easily tangled, so if the polymer chains are prepared to spontaneously form ordered states from the solution phase or melt, large free energy barriers are needed to be overcome for reorganizing polymer conformations into ordered states. Cho et al.88 reported the rst conjugated polymer single crystals based on P3HT materials, by using a self-seeding method on a silicon substrate modied with octadecyltrichlorosilane. In these P3HT microwires, molecules packed in an orthorhombic crystal unit cell and the $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction was along the long axis of the microwire (as shown in Fig. 5a and b). Later on, poly(3- butylthiophene) (P3BT) single crystals with extended-chain lamellar packing were reported through a developed solventassist crystallization (SAC) method by Yan et al.89 Based on these studies, other methods have been used to grow polythiophene single crystals90 and a high eld-effect mobility of $0 . 6 2 \ c m ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for P3OT single crystals.91,92

Thanks to the tremendous efforts in this eld, highly ordered nanostructures and single crystals of other conjugated polymer materials have also been reported. Recently, Dong et al.93 produced high quality crystalline nanowires from a rigid rod polymer semiconductor of TA-PPE by a slow self-assembly process from dilute solutions under a certain solvent vapour. TA-PPE nanowires exhibited typical single crystal diffraction spots, the polymer backbone of the TA-PPE chains within the nanowire packed parallel to the nanowire long axis with their side chains vertical to the substrate and charge transport along the polymer backbone chain direction (as shown in Fig. 5c and d). A very high eld-effect mobility approaching $0 . 1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was demonstrated for such polymer crystalline nanowires, which was about 3–4 orders higher than that of its corresponding thin lm transistors.

Recently, highly oriented single bers of a donor–acceptor copolymer system, cyclopentadithiophene–benzothiadiazole copolymer (CDT–BTZ) was reported by Mullen¨ et al.94 The macromolecules were directionally self-assembled using chlorobenzene as the solvent and solvent vapor. Selected area electron diffraction demonstrated the alignment of the CDT–BTZ backbones along the ber axis (as shown in Fig. 5e and f). The single ber of CDT–BTZ also exhibited an excellent hole mobility of $3 . 3 \pm 0 . 3 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ owing to the high molecular order in the crystals and the alignment of CDT–BTZ backbones along the ber axis.

# 4 Conclusions and perspectives

Molecular ordering and orientation signicantly inuence the physical properties of organic materials and their corresponding device properties. This minireview highlights recent progress in the various techniques including rational chemical design, optimizing solution-processes and growth of high quality crystalline polymer micro/nanostructures for ordering conjugated polymer molecules to achieve high performance eld-effect transistors. Although remarkable achievements have been made in this eld, it is a scientic challenge to control polymer molecular ordering with desired alignment in the solid state due to their high molecular weight, complex intermolecular interactions and easily tangled exible chain segments in the molecular structure, which need to overcome multiple free energy barriers associated with the self-assembly process. Moreover, some studies in this eld are still in a primary stage and there are numerous unsolved issues remaining to be addressed. For example, although very high anisotropic transport properties for aligned polymer thin lms has been achieved, it is very difficult to estimate the intrinsic optoelectronic anisotropy between conjugation direction and $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction because of the inevitable existence of chemical or physical charge trapping sites. The crystallization of conjugated polymer molecules has rarely been investigated, so there are not enough experimental results and feasible theory to guide the crystallization of different conjugated polymer systems. Therefore, there is still more work needed in the aspects of solving the remaining questions regarding the polymer crystallization mechanism, the relationship between structure and property and employing these approaches in real devices.
