# Nonhalogenated Solvent Processable and High-Density Photopatternable Polymer Semiconductors Enabled by Incorporating Hydroxyl Groups in the Side Chains

Chenying Gao, Cheng Li,\* Yiming Yang, Ziling Jiang, Xiang Xue, Kaiyuan Chenchai, Junchao Liao, Zhichun Shangguan, Changchun Wu, Xisha Zhang, Di Jia, Fengjiao Zhang, Guoming Liu, Guanxin Zhang, and Deqing Zhang\*

Polymer semiconductors hold tremendous potential for applications in flexible devices, which is however hindered by the fact that they are usually processed by halogenated solvents rather than environmentally more friendly solvents. An effective strategy to boost the solubility of high-performance polymer semiconductors in nonhalogenated solvents such as tetrahydrofuran (THF) by appending hydroxyl groups in the side chains is herein presented. The results show that hydroxyl groups, which can be easily incorporated into the side chains, can significantly improve the solubility of typical p- and n-types as well as ambipolar polymer semiconductors in THF. Meanwhile, the thin films of these polymer semiconductors from the respective THF solutions show high charge mobilities. With THF as the processing and developing solvents these polymer semiconductors with hydroxyl groups in the side chains can be well photopatterned in the presence of the photo-crosslinker, and the charge mobilities of the patterned thin films are mostly maintained by comparing with those of the respective pristine thin films. Notably, THF is successfully utilized as the processing and developing solvent to achieve high-density photopatterning with ${ \approx } 8 2 0 0 0$ device arrays $\mathtt { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ for polymer semiconductors in which hydroxyl groups are appended in the side chains.

# 1. Introduction

In the past decades, a number of p- and n-type as well as ambipolar polymer semiconductors with high charge mobilities have been designed and prepared.!-10] Moreover, these polymer semiconductors have emerged as highly promising candidates for wearable and implantable electronics, due to their inherent advantages over inorganic materials. The advantages of polymer semiconductors encompass mechanical flexibility, solution processability, and biocompatibility,[11-18] The fact that polymer semiconductors can be solutionprocessed greatly simplifies the device fabrication processes and reduces the device production costs accordingly.[19,20] However, most of the high-performance polymer semiconductors are only dissolved in halogenated solvents such as chlorobenzene, chloroform, and 1,2-dichlorobenzene.[21,22] This can be ascribed to the strong interchain interactions induced by the planar and rigid conjugated backbones of polymer semiconductors. It noted that these halogenated solvents are not environmentally benign and utilization of these solvents is banned in industry. Consequently, the use of halogenated solvents to process polymer semiconductors will pose significant hurdles for advancing their practical applications in devices.

It is known that the efficient patterning of organic/polymeric semiconductors is one of critical steps in fabrication of organic integrated electronic devices.123-29] Several patterning methods were reported for organic/polymeric semiconductors.[26,30-34] We and others developed photo-crosslinkers that can generate nitrene and carben reactive species under UV light irradiation to induce the cross-linking of side alkyl chains, and the polymer semiconductors will be immobilized after exposure to UV light.35-39] After rinsing with the appropriate developers, the patterns of organic/polymeric semiconductors can be formed. Regrettably, halogenated solvents such as chloroform and chlorobenzene are again used as the developers for photopatterning. Therefore, the development of polymer semiconductors which show high charge mobilities and can be simultaneously processed with nonhalogenated solvents is highly demanding.

This involves the introduction of polar moieties such as oligoether groups,[42] ionic groups,[43] phosphonate groups,[44] or galactose groups!45] into the side chains of semiconducting polymers. But, most of these modifications compromise charge transporting performance to some extents, because the polar groups may weaken the interchain packing order and certain polar groups may act as potential charge carrier traps.[41] Only a few studies have led to polymer semiconductors which can be processed with nonhalogenated solvents and exhibit high charge mobility simultaneously. For instances, the random polymer which is composed of diketopyrrolopyrrole-selenophene-vinyleneselenophene and diketopyrrolopyrrole-bithienothiophene is processable with xylene and the charge mobility of the polymer thin film can reach $6 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ [46] The conjugated polymer that is constructed with diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP) units, which are flanked by two different aromatic moieties, exhibits a high hole mobility of 12.5 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \bf s } ^ { - 1 }$ and is processable with a binary solvent mixture of toluene and biphenyl ether.47] Nevertheless, nonhalogenated solvents processable polymer semiconductors with high charge mobilities are limited. Moreover, readily available molecular design rationale for high-performance semiconducting polymers with good solubilities in nonhalogenated solvents is still rare.

In this paper, we present a simple yet effective approach for developing semiconducting polymers with high solubilities in nonhalogenated solvents such as tetrahydrofuran (THF) by appending hydroxyl groups in the end of side alkyl chains as illustrated in Figure 1. Typical p-, n-type and ambipolar polymer semiconductors, which are referred to as PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PNDI2T-OH, and PDPP3T-OH (see Figure 1), become highly soluble in THF and is processable with THF, after incorporating the hydroxyl groups in the end of side alkyl chains. Notably, the hydroxyl groups are positioned in the end of side alkyl chains, and such minor modification of side chains may not affect interchain packing of conjugated backbones. Furthermore, the contents of hydroxyl groups in the side chains can finely adjusted by varying the molar ratio of the DPP unit with hydroxyl groups versus that without hydroxyl groups (see Figure S1, Supporting Information). This feature enables us to optimize the contents of hydroxyl groups to ensure the good solubility of the polymer semiconductor on the one hand and high charge mobility on the other hand. The results reveal that PDPPTT-OH shows a record solubility of $1 0 7 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ (see Table S1, Supporting Information, for details), and thin film of PDPP4T-OH exhibits a high hole mobility of $1 . 2 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ when processed with THF. In addition, with PDPP4T-OH as an example, we successfully achieved high-resolution and high density photopatterning by using THF as the developer and the diazirine-based photo-crosslinker,[35] reported by us previously. The patterning density is up to 82 000 patterned pieces $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . We further demonstrate the fabrication of a complementary-like inverter by stepwise photopatterning of PDPP4T-OH and PNDI2T-OH, which is processed and developed with THF.

# 2. Results and Discussions

# 2.1. Synthesis and Characterization

The preparation of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH started from the synthesis of M1 and M6 with hydroxyl groups (see Scheme S1, Supporting Information). M1 was synthesized following the reported procedure.136,48] M6 was obtained via a two-step synthesis with an overall yield of $7 \%$ involving alkylation with a branching alkyl bromide capped with $t { \cdot }$ butyldimethylsiloxy (TBDMS), followed by removal of TBDMS. PDPP4T-OH and their analogues with different contents of hydroxyl groups were prepared by the Stille copolymerization of M1 and its analogue without hydroxyl groups and the respective reagents $^ { 5 , 5 ^ { \prime } }$ -bis(trimethylstannyl) $^ { - 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ bithiophene, 2,5-bis(trimethylstannyl)thieno[3,2-b]thiophene, and 2,5-bis(trimethylstannyl)thiophene. Similarly, PNDI2T-OH was yielded via copolymerization of M6 (Scheme S2, Supporting Information) and its analogue without hydroxyl groups and the reagent $^ { 5 , 5 ^ { \prime } }$ -bis(trimethylstannyl) $^ { - 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -bithiophene. The contents of hydroxyl groups in the side chains of these conjugated polymers can be finely tuned by adjusting the molar ratios of M1 versus the analogues without hydroxyl groups. In this way, we prepared four DPP-bithiophene-based polymers, PDPP4TOH-1, PDPP4T-OH-2, PDPP4T-OH, and PDPP4T-OH-3 (see Figure 1), in which the molar ratios of the repeat units with hydroxyl groups versus those without hydroxyl groups are 1:0, 1:5, 1:10, and 1:20, respectively. The results show that PDPP4T-OH-3 with fewer hydroxyl groups is nearly insoluble in THF, while PDPP4T-OH-1 with higher contents of hydroxyl groups is readily dissolved in THF. As it will be discussed below, thin film charge mobilities of PDPP4T-OH and their analogues decrease by increasing the contents of hydroxyl groups in the side chains. Among them, PDPP4T-OH-3 exhibits highest charge mobility $( 1 . 4 4 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , Figure S1 and Table S2, Supporting Information). Considering that PDPP4T-OH shows high solubility in THF and also relatively high charge mobility, we focus our studies on PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH in which the molar ratio of the repeat unit with hydroxyl groups versus that without hydroxyl groups is set to be 1:10.

The number-average molecular weights $( M _ { \mathrm { n } } )$ of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH, which were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) using $o$ DCB as the eluent at $1 4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ are ranged from 24.3 to $7 8 . 0 \mathrm { k D a }$ (see Table 1). The thin film absorption spectra and cyclic voltammograms of these polymers were measured (Figures S2-S4, Supporting Information). On the basis of the respective onset absorptions and redox potentials, the optical bandgaps, highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH closely resemble the respective analogues without hydroxyl groups that were reported previously.149-52] These results demonstrate that the introduction of a small amount of hydroxyl groups at the ends of side alkyl chains does not significantly impact the electronic structures of the polymers. Moreover, the fact that the absorption spectra of thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH, which were left in ambient conditions for three weeks, were kept nearly unchanged indicates that these polymer semiconductors with hydroxyl groups in their side chains display good stability (Figure S5, Supporting Information).

The solubilities of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH in THF were determined by following the procedures reported previously.153-54] First, for each polymer five THF solutions at different concentrations were prepared, and they were then appropriately diluted to ensure the absorbance of each solution at the respective maximum absorption wavelength (760, 800, 801, and $6 9 7 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ for PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH, respectively) is lower than 1.5 by measuring the absorption spectrum of each solution under the same conditions (Figure 2a-d). Second, we made the plot of absorbance at the respective maximum absorption wavelength versus the respective molar concentration (the concentration in $\mathrm { m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ was converted into that in molar concentration on the basis of the molecular weight of the repeat unit of each polymer) for each polymer. As shown in Figure 2e-h, linear plot that relates the absorbance and molar concentration was obtained for each polymer. Third, the respective saturated solutions of PDPP4TOH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH were prepared separately by adding certain amounts of each polymer to $0 . 7 \mathrm { m L }$ of THF until precipitates appeared, followed by careful filtering the solution to remove the precipitates. Each saturated solution was carefully diluted with THF, followed by measuring the absorption spectrum of the diluted solution under the same conditions, until the absorbance (at the respective absorption maximum) is around 0.5. Finally, by comparing the respective plot of absorbance versus molar concentration in Figure 2e-h, the concentration of the diluted solution of each polymer can be deduced accordingly. On the basis of the respective dilution ratios, the solubilities of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH were estimated to be 89.6, 107.3, 56.1, and $4 9 . 0 \mathrm { m g }$ $\mathrm { m L ^ { - 1 } }$ , respectively.

We tried to characterize the THF solutions of PDPPTT-OH with different techniques including dynamic light scattering (DLS) and small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) experiments (see Figures S7-S9, Supporting Information). These results reveal that i) polymer chains can be dissolved in THF at concentrations below $0 . 5 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ , and ii) at high concentrations, polymer chains are assembled to form large aggregates that do not precipitate.

The highly soluble behavior of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH can be qualitatively explained by using Hansen solubility parameters (HSPs),[41,55] which encompass atomic dispersive interactions $( \delta _ { \mathrm { D } } )$ , permanent dipole molecular interactions $( \delta _ { \mathrm { p } } )$ , and molecular hydrogen bonding interactions $( \delta _ { \mathrm { H } } )$ . A polymer tends to dissolve more readily in the solvent with HSPs being closer to its own. ${ \mathrm { H S P s } }$ of THF are $\delta _ { \mathrm { D } } =$ 16.8, $\delta _ { \mathrm { P } } = 5 . 7$ , and $\delta _ { \mathrm { H } } = 8 . 0$ [56] The possible H-bonding among the hydroxyl groups in the side chains is expected to boost $\delta _ { \mathrm { H } }$ of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH, which becomes closer to that of THF by comparing with those of the analogues of the polymers without hydroxyl groups. As a result, PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2TOH become more soluble in THF. Apart from THF, all the four polymers can also be dissolved in other nonhalogenated solvents such as 2-methyltetrahydrofuran, toluene, xylene, mesitylene, and cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME) with solubility of at least $5 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ (Figures S10 and S11 and Table S4, Supporting Information).

# 2.2. Charge-Transporting Performance and Photopatterning

Thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH were successfully prepared by spin-coating the respective THF solutions onto the OTS-modified $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ substrate, and the thin films were used to fabricate the respective bottom gate-bottom contact (BGBC) field effect transistors (FETs). Based on the respective transfer curves (Figure S1, Supporting Information), the hole mobilities of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, and PDPP3T-OH, were estimated to be 1.22, 0.44, and $0 . 5 2 {  { \mathrm { ~ c m } } } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, and electron mobilities were $0 . 0 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for PDPP3T-OH, and $0 . 1 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for PNDI2T-OH. FETs with thin films of PDPP4T-OH and its analogues with variable contents of hydroxyl groups in the side chains were also fabricated. The results show that charge mobilities of these polymers decrease by increasing the contents of hydroxyl groups (Table S2, Supporting Information). However, FETs with the respective thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH exhibit go0d stability and durability since the on-state currents, charge mobilities, and threshold voltages of the devices were almost unchanged after being left over a period of three weeks (see Figure S12, Supporting Information).

Considering that the polarity of $\mathrm { O - H }$ bond is higher than that of $( \mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 } .$ C-H bond, the presence of hydroxyl groups in the side chains of these conjugated polymers will facilitate the crosslinking of side chains after being blended with the diazirine-based photo-crosslinker (see Figure 3) reported by us recently,[35] which can generate the reactive carben species. Figure 3c shows the thin film absorption spectra of the four polymers which were separately blended with the photo-crosslinker at $5 \%$ w/w before and those after UV light irradiation for $4 0 \mathrm { s }$ followed by rinsing with THF. The blending thin films before photo-crosslinking can be easily washed away with THF. But, almost all the absorbances of the respective maximum absorption bands of the blend thin films of the four polymers after photo-crosslinking and rinsing can reach ${ \approx } 9 0 \%$ of those of the blending thin films before UV light irradiation, indicating thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH became immobilized after photo-crosslinking, and the cross-linked thin films exhibit excellent solvent resistance.

As illustrated in Figure 3b, photopatterning of all the four polymers were successfully achieved with THF as the processing solvent. The blended thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTTOH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH (containing the photocrosslinker at $5 \% \mathrm { w } / \mathrm { w } )$ with a thickness of $\mathord { \sim } \approx 4 0 \mathrm { n m }$ onto $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ substrates were prepared from the respective THF solutions with the conventional spin-coating method. Then, masks were placed on top of the substrates, followed by exposure of the thin films to $3 6 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ ultraviolet light with a power density of $3 0 \mathrm { ~ m W }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ for $4 0 \ \mathrm { s }$ . The substrates were subsequently developed by soaking them in THF for $3 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ }$ . Finally, patterns were successfully transferred from the masks to the substrates (Figure 3d,e). With PDPP4T-OH as an example, striped patterns with a uniform width as small as $1 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ were readily generated as shown in Figure 3e. Photopatterning of polymer semiconductors with higher resolution can be achieved by using electron beam lithography (EBL). As shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information), the line widths of the patterned thin films of PDPP4T-OH could be further reduced by using EBL with THF as the developing solvent. It is expected that development of new photo-crosslinkers with high reactivity can further reduce exposure dose, thereby increasing the resolution of the photopatterning and reduce the line-edge roughness (LER) of the patterned thin films. These results demonstrate that all the four polymers are easily photopatternable with THF as the processing solvent and without using any halogenated solvents.

The comparison of these GIWAXS data with those of the polymers without hydroxyl groups in the side chains also shows that the influences of hydroxyl groups on the interchain packing of these polymer semiconductors is negligible because the content of hydroxyl groups in side chains is low and the hydroxyl groups are substituted at the ends of alkyl chains and surrounded by alkyl chains.[57,58]

The charge transport properties of the patterned thin films of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH were investigated by fabricating the BGBC FETs with the respective patterned thin films as the active layers. Masks with transparent rectangular patterns (length: $7 5 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ , width: $6 5 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ were placed on the thin films of the four polymers containing the photo-crosslinker (at $5 \% \mathrm { w / w } )$ on the octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS) modified $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ substrates with interdigital gold electrodes. After the masks were aligned to the region of the conductive channels, the thin films were exposed to ultraviolet light $3 6 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ $3 0 \mathrm { \ m W \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ) for $4 0 \ \mathrm { s }$ separately, and then developed with THF. Finally, rectangular patterns of all the four polymers were formed on the top of interdigital gold electrode, respectively (Figure S14, Supporting Information). The patterned thin films were thermally annealed at $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , and the transfer and output curves of the devices with the patterned thin films were measured (Figure 5). As expected, the patterned thin films of PDPP4T-OH and PDPPTT-OH show p-type transporting property, while those of PND12T-OH and PDPP3T-OH behave as n-type and ambipolar semiconductors, respectively. On the basis of the respective transfer curves, the hole and electron mobilities of the patterned thin films of all the four polymers were extracted and listed in Table 2. Average hole mobilities of the patterned thin films of PDPP4T-OH and PDPPTT-OH are up to 0.96 and 0.44 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. The patterned thin film of PDPP3TOH shows ambipolar semiconducting property with average hole and electron mobilities of 0.35 and $0 . 0 7 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, and the average electron mobility of the patterned thin film of PNDI2T-OH can reach $0 . 1 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . As listed in Table 2, charge mobilities of the patterned thin films are only slightly reduced in comparison with those of the respective pristine thin films (Figure S15, Supporting Information). This agrees with the observation that the patterned thin films show similar GIwAXS patterns as those of the respective pristine thin films (Figure 4). These results also show that the semiconducting performances of polymer semiconductors are negligibly affected by using THF as the developing solvent.

These results manifest that polymer semiconductors with hydroxyl groups in the side chains, which can be processed with THF as the nonhalogenated solvent, are useful for organic integrated circuits.

# 2.3. High-Density Photopatterning

High-density photopatterning of PDPP4T-OH, PDPPTT-OH, PDPP3T-OH, and PNDI2T-OH can be achieved by using THF as the processing solvent and the diazirine-based photo-crosslinker that was reported by us recently.[35] With PDPP4T-OH as the example, the designed photomask was put on the top of blended thin film which was prepared from the THF solution of PDPP4TOH and the photo-crosslinker, and was carefully aligned to the region of the semiconducting channels with a length of $5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ and a width of $2 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . By following the photopatterning procedures, 900 patterned species within $1 . 1 4 \times 0 . 9 6 \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ were formed. Each patterned thin film can be utilized to fabricate the FET device (Figure 6b). Thus, photopatterning with ${ \approx } 8 2 ~ 0 0 0$ device arrays $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ can be achieved with THF (the nonhalogenated solvent) as the processing and developing solvents. This is among the highest patterning density reported for organic and polymer semiconductors reported recently (see Table S6, Supporting Information). 100 devices were randomly selected to evaluate the semiconducting performances. The typical transfer curve is shown in Figure 6c, and the device exhibits p-type semiconducting property with a hole mobility of $0 . 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ ratio up to $9 . 3 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ , and a low off-state current of $1 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 1 }$ A. Delightedly, almost all the FETs show ideal transfer curves (see Figure S17, Supporting Information) and exhibit a relatively narrow distribution of charge mobilities and the average charge mobility of $0 . 5 5 \pm 0 . 0 9 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (Figure 6d).

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we present a simple but effective approach to enable p- and n-types and ambipolar polymer semiconductors to be highly soluble in nonhalogenated solvents such as THF by incorporating hydroxyl groups in the side chains in a simple and easy way. Importantly, the interchain packing and the charge transporting properties of the polymer semiconductors are hardly affected by the presence of hydroxyl groups in the side chains. Therefore, these polymer semiconductors with hydroxyl groups in the side chains not only exhibit high solubilities in THF, but also display relatively high charge mobilities. The solubility improvement enabled the high-resolution patterning with a remarkable accuracy of $1 . 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ and facilitated the fabrication of highdensity FET arrays with ${ \approx } 8 2 0 0 0$ device arrays $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ by using THF as the processing and developing solvents. This study introduces a novel approach for developing semiconducting polymers with exceptional solubility in nonhalogenated solvents, and opens up new possibilities for the utilization of polymer semiconductors in organic electronic circuits processed with nonhalogenated solvents.
