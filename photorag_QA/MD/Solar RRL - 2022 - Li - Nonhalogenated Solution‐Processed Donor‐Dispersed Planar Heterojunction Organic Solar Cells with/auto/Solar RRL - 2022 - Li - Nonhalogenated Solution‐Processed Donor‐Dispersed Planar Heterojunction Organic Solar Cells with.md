# Nonhalogenated Solution-Processed Donor-Dispersed Planar Heterojunction Organic Solar Cells with Enhanced Homogeneity in Vertical Phase Separation

Shilin Li, Tianze Jiang, Hong Zhang, Yanxun Li, Qian Cheng, Hui Kang, Ya-Nan Jing, Linge Xiao, Xuning Zhang, Guanghao Lu, Yuan Zhang,\* and Huiqiong Zhou\*

Realization of state-of-the art efficiencies in organic photovoltaics (OPv) generally relies on using toxic halogenated solution processing to arrive at the desired nanomorphology and optoelectronic responses, whereas the photovoltaic performance in nonhalogenated solution (NHS)-based OPVs is yet nonsatisfactory, mainly related to the difficulty of morphological control. Herein, a conceptual approach of donor-dispersed planar heterojunction (DD-PHJ) for improving the regulation of phase morphology and photovoltaic behaviors in NHS-processed OPVs is proposed, afforded by dispersing an ordered liquid crystal guest donor BTR-Cl into the nonfullerene acceptor host with sequential film deposition. The combined investigation shows that the inclusion of BTR-Cl plays a regulatory role in enhancing the crystallization, intermolecular donor/acceptor miscibility, and niscibility, and homogeneity in the donor-acceptor phase separation along vertical direction, ical direction, which is conducive to improved charge transfer and reduced photovoltage loss. Of importance, the described DD-PHJ approach is applicable to representative OPV material systems, leading to a champion efficiency of $1 8 . 2 1 \%$ in devices prepared with NHs. This work provides a promising prospect toward highefficiency and green solution-processed OPV devices.

# 1. Introduction

Bulk heterojunction (BHJ) organic photovoltaics (OPV) has received attention due to their advantages in light weight, high

fexibility, and processability with wet Chemistry.1-3 In recent years, the emergence of nonfullerene (NF) acceptor materials with high optoelectronic tunabilmance, with the highest power conversion efficiency. (PCE) reaching the $1 9 . 6 \%$ regime.45 These accomplishments, apart from the rational materials design, are attributed to the successful control on the nanomorphology of organic BHJ films. Among the efforts for improving the PCE of OPVs, the ternary approach via introducing additional donor or acceptor guest components to the binary quasiplanar heterojunction (Q-PHJ) blend host has become a widely adopted practice, with which increased absorption coverage of BHJ photoactive layers with modified phase morphology can be realized.[6-8] Obtaining the targeted morphology in the binary Q-PHJ or ternary blends is nontriv  
ial, as it is developed through a rapid film-drying kinetics involv  
ing coexisting donor (or codonor) and acceptor (coacceptor)   
components, subject to manifolds of factors. The parameters   
of processing solvents, solution concentration, solvent additives,   
or postprocessing are all needed to be considered, which can   
affect the crystallinity, domain size, phase separation, and molec  
ular ordering in the photoabsorbing layer. It appears obvious that   
using the single-step film deposition, the properties governing   
the ultimate PCEs of OPVs are difficult to be optimized. As   
an alternative method, sequential deposition (SD) has been   
employed for preparing Q-PHJ flms for OPVs, showing   
attractiveness that the morphologies of donor and acceptor   
components can be tuned individually with the vertical phase dis  
tribution in the Q-PHJ promoted, beneficial for charge collection   
and suppression of charge recombination.10-13 It is also of merit   
that the SD method is associated with reduced susceptibility to   
the stringently required processing conditions when compared   
to the one-step deposition, thus potentially more attractive for   
scaling up device production.1415] Based on the SD method,   
there has been the proposal of using the Hansen solubility   
parameters for screening the film processing-related parameters,   
with which OPVs with $\mathrm { P C E S } > 1 8 \%$ were achieved.[16,17] For   
ternary devices prepared with the SD method, a commonly

adopted route is via introducing fullerene or NF guest molecules to form coacceptor domains. By doing so, the electron transport and extraction could be facilitated due to modified carrier transporting networks and electron enrichment near the cathode.18 201 Moreover, the introduced guest acceptors (or donors) could help suppress the unfavorable molecular aggregation and nonradiative recombination loss, which is useful for the gain of open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ in the device.[21-24] We would point out that the success of ternary devices based on the SD (or one-step deposition) method heavily relies on the molecular compatibility between the host and guest acceptors, as well as the energetic matching between the constituting components.[25,26] In some circumstances, the intrinsically high crystallinity of acceptor hosts tends to be impaired with the incorporated guest molecules,27] causing negative changes in morphology and reduction in device performance.28] All these factors inevitably tighten the selection criteria for guest components, thus narrowing the selection and device optimization windows for OPvs.29]

It is also noteworthy that the majority of reported state-of-the art OPVs resort to the film process utilizing halogenated organic solvents, for example, chlorobenzene, chloroform, or dichlorobenzene to warrant sufficient solubilities and polarities for the donor (D) and acceptor (A) materials. These solvents with high toxicities are harmful to our human health and environment, unfavorable for use in large-scale device fabrication. To increase the commercialization viability of OPVs, using nonhalogenated solvents (NHS) to process OPVs toward high photovoltaic efficiencies has recived increased attention.30-321 In this aspect, while visible progress in the efficiency has been achieved, NHS-based OPVs are yet underperforming their device counterparts prepared with halogenated solvents.33-35 This performance gap can be related to the nonoptimal morphology of Q-PHJ (or BHJ) films processed with NHS such that the exciton diffusion length and/or geminate recombination are negatively affected.136] In previous studies, it has been shown that excitons generated in the Q-PHJ device are primarily populated in the areas proximate to the bottom electrode.371 In such situations, the effective transporting paths for electrons tend to be longer than for the hole counterparts, thus increasing the encounter probability between the two carriers and carrier losses to recombination. This provides a possible explanation for the device fill factor (FF) and PCE of Q-PHJ devices that are often suboptimal with regard to those based on the BHJ structure, except for a few cases.[38,39]

Despite the obtained insights and efficiency improvement approaches for Q-PHJ OPVs, methods to effectively control the phase morphology and the homogeneity in phase separation are yet lacking. These properties are of high relevance to charge carrier transport and photovoltage losses in OPVs and thus critically important. According to a series of previous related works, the majority of small-molecule donors as the guest component possess higher crystallinity, enhanced crystallinity of the donor, and have good compatibility with Y-series acceptors, thus effectively improving exciton dissociation, the charge carrier transmission, and extraction.40 42] We speculate that by introducing a highly crystalline guest donor molecule into the acceptor-rich domain coupled with the SD method, synergetic effects may be generated to realize mediated properties of phase separation, crystallinity, and eventual photovoltaic efficiencies.

This proposal would in particular help improve the morphology regulation for OPVs processed with NHSs.

In this work, in the perspective of NHS device processing, we propose a conceptual structure through dispersing a highly crystalline donor guest into the acceptor domain with the SD method to obtain improved regulation of nanoscale morphology and phase separation in OPVs prepared with NHS of o-xylene (o-XY). We chose the polymer donor PM6 (PBDB-T-2F) and NF-acceptor Y7-BO (BTP-BO-4Cl) as the testbed hosts. Given that the liquid crystal small-molecule donor (5,5'-[[4,8-bis[5-(2- ethylhexyl)-4-chloro-2-thienyl]benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']dithiophene-2,6- diyl]bis[(3',3"-dihexyl[2,2':5',2"-terthiophene]-5",5-diyl)methylidyne]] bis[3-hexyl-2-thioxo-4-thiazolidinone] (BTR-Cl) has the properties of high crystallinity with good solubility in common nonhalogen solvents, as well as molecules with liquid crystal characteristics usually exhibit strong intermolecular interactions, which is conducive to maintaining long-range directional order and high charge carrier mobility,43,4] it was chosen as the donor guest for the construction of DD-PHJs. Our combined analysis with film depth-sensitive optical spectroscopy and transient optoelectrical measurements shows that upon introducing BTR-Cl into the upper acceptor layer, the D/A phase separation at nanoscale is successfully optimized, leading to improving interfacial charge transfer  and suppressing bimolecular recombination. Furthermore, we found that the homogeneity in the D/A vertical phase separation is promoted in the proposed DD-PHJ, which beneficially reduces the energetic disorder and voltage loss via nonradiative recombination in the device. The described DD-PHJ approach is applicable to multiple representative OPV material systems. Based on the D18-Cl/BTP-eC9 host, the resultant DD-PHJ device (D18-Cl/BTP-eC9:BTR-Cl) delivers a champion PCE of $1 8 . 2 1 \%$ which is among the highest efficiencies obtained on OPVs processed from NHS.

# 2. Result and Discussion

With regard to PM6, the introduced BTR-Cl with a higher-lying HOMO energy was found not to adversely affect the transport of holes, as will be discussed later. In light of the significance of nanomorphology and phase separation that are speculated to be modified in the presence of BTR-Cl, we first exploited film depth-sensitive absorption spectroscopy (DSAS) to examine the phase separation and associated changes in the optical properties (see 2D plots of DSAS in Figure 2a,b). On the basis of the absorption of neat films (Figure 1c), we focused the analysis using the characteristic absorption peaks of PM6 $( 6 2 5 \mathrm { n m } )$ and Y7-BO $( 8 2 6 \mathrm { n m } )$ . For the Q-PHJ (PM6/Y7-BO) film, the changes of the relative peak ratio of $\mathsf { D } / \mathsf { A }$ along the film growth direction provide an indication that the acceptor is enriched at the top area of the film close to the PNDIT-F3N electron-transporting layer while the donor mainly assembles near PEDOT:PSS on the bottom area. Such phase separation with the SD method is generally more pronounced than in the one-step-deposition-prepared BHJ films, which is favorable for charge collection and suppression of bimolecular charge recombination.47,48] Upon adding BTR-Cl, we observe a stronger absorption at the $5 5 0 { \cdot } 6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ range, likely due to the effective dispersion of BTR-Cl inside the photoactive layer. Moreover, the position of PM6 absorption peaks shows weaker redshift throughout the middle blend area, indicating that the crystallinity of PM6 changes slightly at different depths, which may be beneficial to reduce energetic disorder and main. tain the integrity of hole-transport channels in DD-PHJ film.49] Another interesting phenomenon is seen that upon increasing the probe depth for the binary Q-PHJ film, the absorption peak of Y7-BO presents a redshift (up to $2 0 \mathrm { n m }$ ), progressively fading toward the bottom area. We attribute this behavior to the higher ordering in crystallization along the $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction of acceptor.50] Compared to this scenario, weaker variations in the absorption peak of Y7-BO are observed in the DD-PHJ film along the vertical direction. It seems to point toward less fluctuations of the transport energy levels, which help suppress the lower-energy (tail) states and promote charge transport with decreased charge recombination.51] We further extracted the compositional distribution profiles through fitting the sublayer absorption spectra of DSAs based on the linear spectral overlap with the absorption of pure components (see results in Figure 2c,d).[52-56] It appears that in the DD-PHJ flm, the enrichment of PM6 donor enlarges at the bottom area (compared to that of the binary Q-PHJ film). This peculiarity could promote the hole transport and collection, which is consistent with the mobility measurements discussed later. Of importance, within the intermixing zone (which is assumed based on the film depths corresponding to comparable contents of D/A), the relative concentrations of D/A components along the vertical direction change less pronouncedly with BTR-Cl. It provides a strong implication of the enhanced miscibility between PM6 and Y7-BO, modulated by the donor guest. In other words, a more uniform phase separation along the direction of film growth is achieved in the PM6/Y7-BO:BTR-Cl-based ternary film. This feature could be in favor of reducing the energetic disorder in the photoactive layer,57] thus beneficial for the mitigation of nonradiative recombination losses in the device.

By showing the enhanced uniformity in vertical phase separation, we numerically simulated the exciton distribution based on the results of DSAS in conjunction with the optical interference between different layers in actual photovoltaic devices,[51] The simulation was performed using an improved transfer matrix optical method reported in a previous study.[52] According to our results (see Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information), the excitons generated in the PM6 donor (around $6 0 0 \mathrm { n m } _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ and Y7-BO acceptor $( 7 5 0 - 9 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ domains mainly populate within the middle and bottom areas of Q-PHJ films. The Y7-BO acceptor exhibits a higher exciton generation rate than the PM6 donor. Compared to the binary Q-PHJ film, the ternary DD-PHJ film yields extra exciton populations (at about $5 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ ) near the film surface, which can be ascribed to the contribution of the BTR-Cl guest. We further present the simulated exciton generation rate G as a function of film depth (Figure S2c, Supporting

Information). Expectedly, the inclusion of BTR-Cl guest donor tends not to significantly affect the absolute value of distribution of exciton generation rates. In both films, the maximum G was found at a depth of ${ \approx } 5 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , approximately half of the film thickness $( \approx 1 1 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ . Our simulation results outline a scenario that the majority of photocarriers (following exciton generation) are created within the middle section of the photoactive layer, despite that the homogeneity in the distribution of phase separation is modified. The seems to contrast to the previous observations in Q-PHJ devices where populated excitons near the bottom of photoactive layers were identified.53] Regarding the picture indicated by our results of G, we will come later to the merits of the proposed DD-PHJ for promotion of charge transport properties.

To further understand the changes in the morphology of DDPHJ, time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) profiling technique was applied to detect the compositional distribution in these Q-PHJ films (see results of ToF-SIMS in Figure 2e,f). The signal intensity of cyano group $( \mathrm { C N ^ { - } } )$ , representing the Y7-BO acceptor, increases first at short time and then remains constant with the increase of sputtering time. The signals of the $\mathrm { F ^ { - } }$ ions, representing the PM6 donor, display a slightly increasing trend from the top toward the bottom area. Since both Y7-BO and BTR-Cl contain $\mathrm { C l } ^ { - }$ , only Y7-BO contains $\mathrm { C N ^ { - } }$ .Through comparing the evolution of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ and the disappearance of $\mathrm { C N ^ { - } }$ signal (see Figure S3a,b Supporting Information), we draw a subconclusion that the introduced BTR-Cl can disperse in the whole D-A intermixing region in DD-PHJ films. Moreover, the higher $\mathrm { C N ^ { - } / F ^ { - } }$ ratio at both middle and bottom area in DD-PHJ film indicated that BTR-Cl introduction has promoted the formation of a gradient vertical phase separation (shown in Figure S3c, Supporting Information).58] We consider that this feature will be helpful to reduce charge recombination charge generation in the DD-PHJ films.

For the sake of device operation in OPVs, it will be necessitated to have sufficiently long diffusion distance to warrant efficient charge carrier generation.13,67] In this context, we performed steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurements on these Q-PHJ films. The parameter of PL, defined by the steady-state PL quenching efficiency, provides an approximate index for the efficiency of exciton dissociation at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface. As depicted in Figure S8, Supporting Information, while the intensities of PL signals arising from the deabsorption (emission) of D and A components in the binary Q-PHJ and DD-PHJ films both reduce considerably when compared to the emission peaks of neat PM6 (at $6 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ Y7-BO films $( 8 8 5 \mathrm { n m } )$ , the ternary PHJ film features with a more pronounced PL quenching in the NIR region (APL is $8 0 . 4 \%$ for the binary Q-PHJ film and $8 4 . 7 \%$ for the DD-PHJ film.) These behaviors hint that with BTR-Cl, interfacial exciton dissociation and charge transfers at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interfaces are promoted, which in general is helpful for the reduction of geminate recombination losses.[68]

To have more knowledge on the impacts of BTR-Cl on charge transfer kinetics, we carried out transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS, see 2D color images of TAS in Figure S9, Supporting Information, and according TAs plots in Figure S10, Supporting Information). We first chose $8 3 0 \mathrm { n m }$ excitation to exclusively excite the Y7-BO acceptor which enabled us to obtain information on the interfacial hole transfer.[69] We focused on the decay profiles probed at $9 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and the results are displayed in Figure 3e. Based on fittings with an exponential decay model, the characteristic TAS decay time $( \tau _ { \mathrm { T A S } } )$ of the binary PM6/Y7-BO Q-PHJ flm was estimated to be ${ \approx } 3 2$ ps, whereas the ternary DD-PHJ film exhibited a shorter $\tau _ { \mathrm { T A S } }$ of ${ \approx } 1 5$ ps. This contrast indicates that the hole transfer occurs more rapidly (from Y7-BO to PM6) in the presence of BTR-Cl, which provides benefits for the reduction in exciton recombination.70] Following this analysis, we examined the overall charge generation process using higher-energy excitation (at $5 4 0 \mathrm { n m } _ { i }$ . The corresponding TA decay profiles are presented in Figure 3f, based on which the $\tau _ { \mathrm { T A S } }$ of the PM6/Y7-BO and PM6/BTR-Cl:Y7-BO films was determined to be 42 and 26ps, respectively. The indicated faster charge transfers involving both electrons and holes can be connected to the possibility that the excitons generated in the donor and/or acceptor domains are able to diffuse to the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface more efficiently in the DD-PHJ film. This phenomenon could arise from the reduction of exciton traps or modified domain sizes upon introducing the BTR-Cl molecular guest.[71]

Now we turn to explore how the modified morphology and photophysical processes are translated in the boost of photovoltaic properties. For interest, we further examined the stability of Q-PHJ devices affected by the introduced BTR-Cl donor guest. As shown by the results in Figure S13, Supporting Information, when applying thermal aging (at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , both binary Q-PHJ and ternary

In contrast, after ${ \approx } 1 0 0 \mathrm { h }$ of thermal aging, a retardation on the efficiency drops was found in the DD-PHJ device while the control cell displayed a faster PCE decay. The tendencies point to that the introduction of appropriate donor guest component in the upper layer of Q-PHJs offers the potentials for improving the thermal stability of OPVs.

In order to understand how the inclusion of BTR-Cl in the top acceptor layer impacts charge recombination and collection processes in these Q-PHJ devices, we performed light intensity $( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ -dependent analysis based on the PM6/Y7-BO binary Q-PHJ and ternary DD-PHJ devices. The dependence of $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ on $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ is generally described by the natural logarithmic formula, $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } { \approx } n ( k _ { \mathrm { B } } T / q ) \mathrm { l n } P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ $\lvert k _ { \mathrm { B } }$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature, and $q$ is the elementary charge).[73] As displayed in Figure 5a, the ideality factors $( n )$ extracted from linear fits are 1.24, 1.20 for the binary Q-PHJ and ternary DDPHJ OPVs, respectively. The reduced n-value (approaching unity) in the DD-PHJ device is suggestive of the suppression of trap-assisted charge recombination. We further assessed the efficiency of charge collection and exciton dissociation in these Q-PHJ devices based on the $J _ { \mathrm { { p h } } }$ (photocurrent density) versus effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ characteristics (see results in Figure S14, Supporting Information). The charge collection probability $( P ( E , T ) )$ was determined according to the relation $P ( E , T ) = J _ { \mathrm { p h } } / J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } ,$ where $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ is the saturated photocurrent density at sufficiently high $V _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ We identify that the DD-PHJ OPV is profited from higher charge collection probabilities with $P ( E , T ) = 9 9 . 2 \%$ at the short-circuit condition and $P ( E , T ) = 9 0 . 7 \%$ near built-in potential, whereas lowered $P ( E , T )$ values of $9 7 . 2 \%$ and $8 9 . 0 \%$ were found in the binary Q-PHJ cell at the same biasing conditions. It means that in the presence of BTR-Cl, exciton dissociation and charge collection occur more efficiently.68,74] These merits provide a physical rationale for the enhanced FF in the DD-PHJ device.

We employed transient photovoltage (TPV) to shed more light on the bimolecular recombination and the results are shown in Figure 5b. Compared with the binary Q-PHJ OPV where a TPV decay time $( \tau )$ of $2 4 . 6 6 \mu \mathrm { s }$ was identified, the DD-PHJ OPV exhibits an elongation of $\tau$ $( 2 7 . 0 4 \mu \mathrm { s } )$ , meaning a retardation of charge recombination in the presence of BTR-Cl. To enable better understandings on the photogenerated carrier transportation and mitigated recombination, we utilize Mott-Schottky analysis of impedance measurements to explore the carrier properties of different PHJ devices. As shown in Figure S15, Supporting Information, we observed a higher $V _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ in the DD-PHJ device. The gain in $V _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ can be explained by weakened recombination and accelerated charge extraction to alleviate interfacial charge accumulation. The enlargement of $V _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ would directly translate into higher internal E, driving charge separation in solar cells, which partly explains the accelerated sweep-out of charge carriers.26] Next, we explored the charge transport in different PHJ OPVs based on the well-established spacecharge-limited current (SCLC) method (see dark $J { - } V$ curves in Figure S16, Supporting Information), with which the electron and hole mobilities $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } , \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } )$ of Q-PHJ and DD-PHJ films were determined. As shown in Figure 5c and Table S8, Supporting Information, the introduction of BTR-Cl results in a simultaneous increase of electron and hole mobilities with $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ boosted from $5 . 9 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $6 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ from $2 . 7 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $5 . 5 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . As such, the transport of the two carriers in the presence of BTR-Cl becomes more balanced with the $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ value reaching 1.13 (vs a $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ ratio of 2.16 in the binary Q-PHJ film). By revisiting the mapping of exciton generation rates in Figure S2, Supporting Information, the majority of photocarriers in the Q-PHJs tends to travel through a comparable distance to reach the two electrodes. In this regard, the balanced charge transport (in our case the DD-PHJ) is highly favorable, as in a D-A two-component system, the mobility imbalance likely causes the waiting of fast carriers for slow carriers, raising the probability of recombination at the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ interface, prior to charge collection.75] with this rationalization, the enhancement of photocurrent, device FF, and ultimate photovoltaic efficiencies in the DD-PHJ solar cells are understood. # 3. Conclusion

To summarize, with a favored scope of applying NHS-based processing for OPVs, we have proposed a conceptual DD-PHJ structure through dispersing an ordered liquid crystal guest donor BTR-Cl into the acceptor domain to afford improvements in the regulation of nanomorphology and photovoltaic behaviors in OPVs prepared with NHS processing. Our combined analyses with film DSAS, ToF-SIMS measurements, and ultrafast optical spectroscopy coupled with numerical simulation provide unambiguous evidence that the incorporated BTR-Cl increases the uniformity in the nanoscale D/A phase separation and D-A intermolecular miscibility and promotes interfacial charge transfers. These modifications indicated that the BTR-Cl donor dispersion promoted the reduction of energy disorder in the heterojunction films; improved charge transport and further suppressed photovoltage losses are attained in the device. The proposed DD-PHJ approach can be applied to multiple representative OPV material systems to receive enhancements in the photon-harvesting efficiency. For example, upon adopting the DD-PHJ structure of D18-Cl/BTP-eC9:BTR-Cl, a champion PCE of $1 8 . 2 1 \%$ has been realized in the device prepared with $o { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { X Y }$ . which is among one of the highest values obtained on NHS-based OPVs.
