# 25th Anniversary Article: Key Points for High-Mobility Organic Field-Effect Transistors

Huanli Dong,\* Xiaolong Fu, Jie Liu, Zongrui Wang, and Wenping Hu\*

Remarkable progress has been made in developing high performance organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) and the mobility of OFETs has been approaching the values of polycrystalline silicon, meeting the requirements of various electronic applications from electronic papers to integrated circuits. In this review, the key points for development of high mobility OFETs are highlighted from aspects of molecular engineering, process engineering and interface engineering. The importance of other factors, such as impurities and testing conditions is also addressed. Finally, the current challenges in this field for practical applications of OFETs are further discussed.

# 1. Introduction

Organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), using $\pi \cdot$ conjugated systems including small molecules, oligomers and polymers as the active semiconducting layer, have attracted particular attention as the key elements for realizing the next generation of electronics, that is, flexible and printed organic electronics.1-7] Intensive research efforts from both academia and industry over the past decades have been performed in achieving superior performance of OFETs and remarkable progress has been made in this field. For instance, charge-carrier mobility, a crucial parameter of OFETs referring to the drift velocity of the charge carrier under per unit applied electric field has experienced significant increase from the initial value of $1 0 ^ { - 5 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ first reported in $1 9 8 0 \mathrm { s } ^ { [ 8 ] }$ to the state-of-the-art value of $4 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for hole mobilities[9] and $1 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for electron mobilities.[10] It is no doubt that such significant improvement in OFET performance is essentially driven by a combined success of multiple approaches: development of novel organic semiconductors, optimization of device physics including device fabrication and interface engineering as well as increasing attentions on other affecting factors such as impurity and testing conditions.

Since polythiophene was introduced in OFETs in 1980s,[8] developing high performance organic semiconductors from the aspects of synthetic chemistry, molecular structure, chemical/environmental also determined by the real molecular orders/orientation, grain sizes, grain boundaries etc., especially at the dielectric/semiconductor interface.28-33] It is noteworthy that even for devices with the same organic semiconductor, the reported field-effect mobilities often vary largely, even over an order of magnitude due to different fabrication processes, device structures and interface perfect production equipments are all challenging tasks to be solved.

Aiming at addressing these fundamental scientific issues in this field, the purpose of this article is to give a meaningful guideline for key points of high mobility OFETs from the aspects of developing superior organic semiconductors, controlling molecular solid state structures with desired molecular packing and orientation by adjusting processing conditions, improving charge carrier injection and transport by optimizing interface properties. Firstly, by introducing the most representative organic semiconductors, we will systematically illustrate how the molecular structure affects their packing modes, the transfer integral as well as reorganization energy, and in turn the field-effect mobility. Secondly, we will discuss the relationship of molecular solid structures/morphology and their charge carrier mobilities, and how to realize the desirable molecular packing in solid state by fabrication techniques for high charge carrier mobility. Then, key points for interface engineering in OFETs including the reduction of trap states at the interface, adjustment of interface compatibility for good contact and energy levels alignment for efficient charge injection and transport are also illustrated. Finally, the importance of other affecting factors such as impurities and testing external conditions for OFET performance is also highlighted. All of these aspects are integrated together in our article, in order to give a concept of key points for high performance OFETs (Figure 1) and provide guidlines to advance their prac tical applications.

# 2.1. Theoretical Calculation: Towards Large Transfer Integral and Small Reorganization Energy

Different from their covalent or ionic-bonded inorganic counterparts, organic semiconductor solids are formed by relatively weak Van der Waals interaction between molecules, exhibiting a wide band-gap and distinct charge transport mechanism. Recently, it is generally accepted that at low temperature, a term of band-like motion could be used for describing the charge carrier transport in ultrapure organic semiconductors principly without any defects. In this situation, the charge carriers are delocalized over several chains or free chains and the hole and electron mobilities are governed by the total valence and conduction bandwidths that are built from interaction of the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) levels.28] While at higher temperatures (e.g. near room temperature), the charge carrier mobility of organic materials is often determined by a thermally activated polaronic hopping transport process, in which the charge carriers are expected to be localized over a single molecule unit (due to the strong electron-phonon coupling and the effect of splitting energy levels, which is also a major characteristic of organic conjugated materials).(38,39] In fact, considering most organic electronic devices need to be operated at room temperature and that the impurities and defects are inevitable (see Section 5.1), charge hopping process appears to be a more appropriate model to describe the charge carrier transport in most organic systems. These systems are generally characterized by a significant static energetic and/or spatial disorder. In such cases to form a current flow the electron or hole needs to transfer from one molecule to another through a hopping process. According to semi-classical electron transfer theory and extensions theory,28,40,41] there are two key parameters critically determining the self-exchange rates and then the ultimate charge carrier mobility. One parameter is the transfer integral (t), which is related to the electronic couplings between adjacent molecules and strongly dependent on the mode of molecular packing, including the degree of $\pi$ orbital overlaps, $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance, intermolecular interactions, etc. Another parameter is the reorganization energy $( \lambda )$ , which includes both internal and external contributions. The external contributions arise from changes in the surrounding media that accompany the charge transfer, which is often neglected in this stage of theoretical calculation. While the internal reorganization energy originated from the molecular deformations upon charging is largely related to the intramolecular freedom degree, the molecular rigidity, the conjugation length, etc. Theoretically speaking, large transfer integral and small reorganization energy are favorable for speeding up the charge transfer rate according to Equation (1),42] and thus for high hopping carrier mobility in organic semiconductors.

# 2.2.3. High Mobility Small Conjugated Molecules

Acenes and their derivatives. Acenes, a class of aromatic hydrocarbons with fused benzene rings, are the typical linear conjugated molecules and have attracted intensive interest over the past decades due to their good electronic properties.[11,12] Among them, anthracene (1, Figure 4a) with three fused benzene rings is the smallest acene compound reported so far with the transistor characteristics, however, only $0 . 0 2 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ of field-effect mobility based on its single crystals was achieved.[50] The poor field-effect behavior for anthracene is probably due to the relatively small $\pi$ conjugation systems and surface scattering in experiment.[51] with the extension of $\pi$ conjugation systems, the carrier mobility was also increased accordingly. For example, tetracene (2, Figure 4a), with four fused benzene rings, exhibited a tested carrier mobility of $0 . 1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in its lowered reorganization energy (from $1 3 8 ~ \mathrm { m e V }$ for molecule 1 to $7 9 \mathrm { m e V }$ for molecule 4, Figure 4a), thus leading to a higher carrier mobility.[56] The good consistence of theoretical and experimental results for acene compounds demonstrates that it is really a straightforward and effective approach to enhance the charge carrier mobility through a simple extension of the $\pi$ conjugation backbone.

Figure 4c shows the variation trend of acenes with the increasing benzene rings and many efforts have recently been devoted to developing novel acene derivatives in such a way as to (i) improve the materials' stability in case of still maintaining their good carrier mobility; (ii) further optimize their solution processability for advancing their practical applications in low-cost, large-area electronic industry; (ii) generate new crystal structures, such as 2D lamellar packing with enhanced intermolecular interactions and electronic couplings with expectation for higher transport properties. One of the most promising effective approaches is incorporating conjugated substitutions at the end-positions of small acenes in the case of maintaining the original large band gap as well as extending the $\pi$ conjugation length for efficient charge carrier transport.

It is found that anthracene derivatives with symmetrical phenylenevinylene substituents at both ends exhibited remarkably high charge carrier mobility, environmental stability, and device repeatability,60,61] DPVAnt (5, Figure 4a) and DPPVAnt (with two hexyl chains at the end positions of DPVAnt, not shown here) are two representative examples guided by such a molecular design strategy. Compared to pentacene, large band gaps of around $2 . 6 \mathrm { e V }$ was obtained for both DPVAnt and DPPVAnt as predicted, nearly $1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ wider than that of pentacene $( 1 . 7 7 \mathrm { e V } )$ ensuring their good environmental stability. Such an change at the end of acene conjugated backbone does not alter the original herringbone molecular packing but leads to a more densely packed crystal structure with a small crystal cell of crystals (a $= 5 . 8 8$ A and $\mathrm { ~ b ~ } = 7 . 3 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ ) compared to pentacene $( \mathrm { a } ) = 6 . 2 7$ A and $\mathrm { b } = 7 . 7 9 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ Figure 4b1), suggesting stronger intermolecular electronic couplings for higher carrier mobility. A high mobility of $1 . 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was observed for DPVAnt thin films (further increased to $4 . 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for its single crystals)[62] and $1 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ mobility was found for DPPVAnt thin films. These experimental results demonstrate that the conjugation extension of small organic molecules through vinylene substituents is an effective approach to improve the charge carrier mobility due to its small volume for more condensed molecular packing. Such a design concept with vinylene- or ethynyl- as conjugated bridge to extend $\pi$ conjugation has also been successfully applied for other conjugated systems, such as heteroacenes and oligothiophenes for tight molecular packing and high carrier mobility.[63,64]

Different from acene derivatives with end- substitutions, incorporating substitutions at the peri-positions of acene cores is more conductive to generate cofacial $\pi$ stacking by adjusting the molecular packing modes from herringbone to a cofacial herringbone or even a 2-D bricklayer' arrangement (Figure 4(b2-b4)). Rubrene (6, Figure 4a) is one of the most representative examples of this class compounds, which is also usually considered as a benchmark for organic semiconductors due to its good electronic properties. Very high carrier mobility of $1 5 { \sim } 4 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was observed for rubrene single crystals based on different device structures and measurement conditions.[14,65] Structural analyses demonstrated that, in solid state rubrene adopts a cofacial herringbone arrangement with enhanced $\pi$ stacking interactions along the a-axis direction, resulting in efficient electronic couplings and large transfer integral along this direction.[66] 5,11-BTBR, a rubrene derivative with $t { \cdot }$ butyl sidegroups at the 5,11 positions (not shown here) also exhibited a high carrier mobility of $1 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for its B-phase single crystals.[67] This molecular design concept that is to change molecular arrangement by peri-position substitution opens a new promising way to modulate the typical herringbone arrangement with strengthened cofacial $\pi$ stacking for high mobility organic semiconductors. DCT (7, Figure 4a), $\mathrm { T C T } ^ { [ 6 8 , 6 9 ] }$ (8, Figure 4a) and $\mathrm { D C P } ^ { [ 7 0 , 7 1 ] }$ (9, Figure 4a) are three representative compounds guided by this design concept, which pack in a cofacial herringbone with a small $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance (for example, $3 . 4 8 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for DCP, Figure 4b2). Charge mobility of $1 . 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { \bar { 2 } } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 7 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was observed for DCT and TCT single crystals, respectively. Much higher carrier mobility of $9 . 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for DCP nanoribbon crystals due to the extension of $\pi$ conjugation as well as the abundant C-H...Cl interactions between neighboring molecules. Actually, molecular packing modes and charge transport properties are strongly dependent on the types, number, sizes as well as the symmetry of substituents on the core conjugated backbones. For instance, in contrast, 5-chlorotetraceneonly and 5-bromotetracene only with one halogen atom at the 5-position of tetracene, exhibited a sandwich herringbone packing with a lower carrier mobilities of $1 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \bar { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $2 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively.[68]

In order to further increase $\pi$ orbital overlap for efficient charge transport, another effective derivatization approach was developed by Anthony and co-workers,[50,72] where introducing appropriate bulky solubilizing groups at the peri-positions of pentacene. Intriguingly, the molecular packing modes are strongly dependent on the relative length of the substituent and conjugated backbone core. # 2.2.4. High Mobility Conjugated Polymers

Conjugated polymers have exhibited great potential for the next generation of low-cost, large-area and flexible organic electronics due to their good solution processability and superior mechanical properties. Nevertheless, compared to small organic molecules, the development of high performance conjugated polymers has been lagged far behind because of (i) the severe lack of structure-property relationship for guiding rational molecular engineering design, (ii) the large difficulty of chemical synthesis and purification process for high-quality polymer semiconductors. Furthermore, the complex molecular structure and large amount of atoms in one polymer chain make it extremely challenging for theoretical calculation to reliably simulate the optimal molecular structure for efficient carrier transport. Over the past decades, the charge carrier mobilities of conjugated polymers were apparently low (lower than amorphous silicon (-Si)), or even much lower. Excitingly, a remarkable breakthrough was achieved for high mobility conjugated polymers in the past few years, approaching a benchmark value of $1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ [27] Different from small organic molecules, the molecular packing mode of conjugated polymers looks is relatively simple and only two typical molecular arrangements are adopted by polymer molecules in the solid state, i.e. face-on and edge-on packing, both of which can be seen as a type of supra 2D packing motif just with different orientation of conjugated backbone plane relative to the substrate. In coplanar configuration of OFETs, edge-on molecular packing is a prior arrangement in the conducting channel, which provides 2D efficient charge carrier transport along both intrachain- and interchain- directions. The formation of edge. on packing mode in solid state is strongly dependent on the native conjugated polymer molecules and also the subsequent processing conditions (more detailed discussion in Section 3). For high performance conjugated polymers, some unique considerations are needed in molecular engineering, such as the effect of molecular regioregularity, molecular weight distribution and building block symmetry.[23,24,27,126,128]

Polythiophene unit: a prototype for conjugated polymers. Polythiophene as the prototype building block for conjugated polymers have been widely studied including both their chemical synthesis techniques and charge transport properties since they were first reported in 1980s.8,11,1] Among them, regioregular poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) has received particular attention and set the first record mobility of over $0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for conjugated polymers in 1990s.[129] However, the low ionization energy (IP, around 4.8 V) of P3HT makes it suffer from poor stability which severely hinders its practical applications in organic industry. The design concept of lowering the degree of aromaticity in conjugated backbone to improve the materials' stability has also been successfully applied in conjugated polymers. One effective approach is to introduce the unsubstituted thiophene moieties in polythiophene backbone to increase the rotational freedom along backbone for the decrease of aromaticity and increase of IP values. A class of PQT polymers with different length of alkyl side chains have been developed by Ong et al.130,131] This class of polymers had an IP of $4 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ (around $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ higher than P3HT) and carrier mobility as high as $0 . 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { s } } ^ { - 1 }$ carrier mobility was observed for $\mathrm { P Q T - C } _ { 1 2 }$ thin film after annealing process. Another useful approach for increasing IP of polythiophene-based materials is using collectively incorporating large conjugated units, such as thiophene-fused rings in the conjugated backbone because the assistance of resonance stabilization energy effect on the electronic coupling between the neighboring monomers, i.e. decreased degree of $\pi$ electron delocalization.132-134] It has been then confirmed this approach are effective for developing stable conjugated polymers with high IP values (about $0 . 2 { \mathrm { - } } 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ higher than P3HT) and meanwhile retaining their high charge carrier mobilities (in the range of $0 . 1 { - } 0 . 7 \ \mathrm { c m ^ { 2 } \ V ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } }$ . One of the major milestone examples under this approach is PBTT'T, first reported by McCulloch and co-workers in $2 0 0 6 ^ { [ 1 3 2 ] }$ and then widely studied as a strategic polymer model in various fields.[135,136] PBTTT demonstrated $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ higher of IP than P3HT and mobility as high as $1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ after post annealing treatment. Although two approaches mentioned above can both work effectively in increasing IP values of conjugated polymers, eliminating the presence of regioisomers as well as improving molecular selforganization for efficient charge carrier transport, the drawback is that low solubility for the decrease of side chains in backbone is unfavorable for their solution processability to various organic solvents. More recently, it is found that the introduction of alkyl-substituted thienylenevinylene (TV) unit in polythiophene backbone can further improve the processability of polymers and meanwhile do not lower their device performance and stability.[137] Slightly increased carrier mobility of up to $1 . 0 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ has been observed for PC12TV12T, which is also the highest mobility value for polythiophene semiconductors reported so far. Donor-acceptor copolymer: a promising candidate for developing durable high performance conjugated polymers. Although polythiophene derivatives have been widely studied in OFETs, further improvement of the charge carrier mobility for this class of conjugated polymers has met a large bottleneck. Recently, it was found that selective introduction of an electron-donating unit (D) and an electron-accepting unit (A) into an alternating D-A conjugated backbone is a promising design approach for further greatly enhance the charge carrier transport in conjugated polymers, though such a kind of D-A copolymers have already been widely used in photovoltaic devices. More compact molecular packing with close $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking between adjacent polymer chains can be expected for this class of conjugated molecules due to the minimized electronic repulsion with a D-A slipping packing mode (Figure 11a), which would be favorable for strong intermolecular interactions and electronic coupling, leading to efficient charge transport by hopping.(27,129] Moreover, the electronic properties of alternating D-A copolymers can be feasibly modulated by adjusting the relative strengths of donor and acceptor moieties in the conjugated framework, from its fundament ambipolar properties to typical p-type or n-type behavior (i.e. stronger donor leads to predominantly hole transport while stronger acceptor result in electron transport). Presently, conjugated-thiophene groups are the usually used donor units, so searching for appropriate accepting moiety to develop superior D-A copolymers with high carrier mobility and good stability has been the intensive research topic in this field. Figure 11b summarizes the molecular structures of recently used acceptor units in D-A copolymers for OFETs. Obviously, with the increase of electron-accepting strength from BT to NDI, the electronic properties of resulting copolymers have been successfully transformed from p-type, to ambipolar and even n-type behaviors. Additionally, it should be stated that there are generally two typical ground states: (I) partial charge-transfer State and (II) biradical anion-cation State (Figure 11c). In order to maintain the semiconductor property for D-A copolymers, it is crucial to prevent extensive State (I) transferring to State (II) by modulating the relative strengths of donor and acceptor moieties in the conjugated backbone.27] Besides this key point, other molecular design concepts including $\pi$ conjugation extension, molecular weight, side chain engineering, building block symmetry etc., used for small molecules and polythiophenes are also applicable for D-A copolymers. In following section, we will highlight some of the milestone development in conjugated polymers from the aspect of molecular design engineering with some special points.

Molecular weight effect. The correlation between molecular weight and charge transport properties has been extensively investigated for polythiophene materials, where it was found that generally the higher the molecular weight, the higher the carrier mobility. This is probably due to the enhanced macroscopic molecular orders crystallite domains and their network structures in thin films for efficient charge carrier transport.138,139] Such a design principle is applicable for various conjugated polymers, including D-A copolymers. With the BTbased copolymers as research target, it was found that unprecedented high hole mobility of $3 . 3 \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ could be achieved for CDT-BTZ sample with a high molecular weight of $5 1 \ \mathrm { K } ,$ about one order of magnitude higher than that $( 0 . 2 \bar { 8 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ of low molecular weight samples (13 K).[140] Further investigation also confirmed the prediction that both pronounced crystallite connectivity and promoted charge transport along the long polymer backbones in high molecular weight polymers contributed to the boosting increase of carrier mobility. Additionally, extension of conjugated backbone with larger fused heteroacene as a donor unit was also applied to develop other BT-based conjugated polymers, such as IDT-BT.[141] The extended $\pi$ conjugation framework and enhanced planarity for IDT-BT molecules guaranteed its high charge carrier mobility of approaching $1 . 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Much higher carrier mobility for IDT-BT could be expected by further increasing the molecular weight, improving film crystallinity as well as molecular orders in the domains.

Side chain effect. The properties of substituents on conjugated backbone such as length, types and the substituting positions are critical for molecular packing modes, intermolecular interactions as well as electronic couplings, and thus significantly affects the resulting charge carrier mobilities.60,64,72] The same regulatory mechanism is also applicable to conjugated polymers, while the difference is that in order to effectively improve the solution processability of conjugated polymers with such a high molecular weight, bulky branched side chains are usually introduced into the conjugated polymer backbone because they can provide better solubility compared to linear alkyl sides/ short chains due to their high density of alkyl chains.[11,142,143] However, on the other hand bulky branched side chains on conjugated backbone can increase the $\pi$ stacking distance between adjacent polymer chains and result in weak intermolecular interactions and low charge carrier mobility due to the steric effect induced by their large volumes. Take IDD-based copolymer as an example, it was found that a large $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distance of ${ \sim } 3 . 7 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ between polymer backbones was produced for PHIT, when the branched side chain of $\mathrm { C _ { 1 0 } C _ { 8 } }$ was directly linked with conjugated backbone, and mobility $0 . 0 1 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ of carrier mobility was observed for this material. This bulkiness effect was reduced to a certain extent through extension of the conjugation backbone by introduction of a more unsubstitutedthiophene unit, and as high as $0 . 7 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , one order of magnitude higher than that of PIIT was achieved for PII2T.[144] Subsequently, Bao et al. found that this bulkiness effect could be further improved by an unconventional siloxane-terminated solubilizing group instead of bulky side chain of $\mathrm { C _ { 1 0 } C _ { 8 } }$ with the concept of moving away branching position from the backbone.145] A more compact packing with close $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distance of $3 . 5 8 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ formed for PIIT-Si copolymers and a remarkably enhanced hole mobility of approaching $2 . 4 8 \ c m ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained. Such a concept of controlling alkyl chain branching positions in conjugated polymers for both good solubility and tight molecular packing has also been further confirmed by Pei et al. in the same isoindigo-based copolymer systems system with the conventional bulky branched side chains with the same isoindigo-based copolymer systems.[24] A gradual decrease of $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking distance (3.75 A for $\mathrm { P I I } 2 \mathrm { T } { \cdot } \mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 }$ 3.61 A for PII2T $2 \mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 }$ 3.57 A for both PII2T $3 \mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 }$ and PHI2T$\mathrm { 4 C _ { 1 0 } H _ { 1 0 } } )$ was measured by grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXD), indicating stronger $\pi$ stacking interactions along with moving away the branching position from backbone with one C-C length to four C-C lengths. The highest carrier mobility of $3 . 6 2 \ c m ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained for PII2T- $3 \mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 } ,$ while an inconsistency between device performance and $\pi$ stacking distance was also observed for PII2T $\mathrm { \cdot C _ { 1 0 } H _ { 1 0 } }$ $( 1 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ and $\mathrm { P I I } 2 \mathrm { T } { \cdot } 2 \mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 }$ $( 0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , reminding us a combined effect of various factors, such as molecular weight, stacking conformation, etc., in developing high performance conjugated polymers.

Conjugated backbone effect. Tremendous efforts have been made in searching for new conjugated building blocks including both donor and acceptor moieties to develop high performance D-A copolymers. Similar to small organic semiconductors, conjugated polymer building block play the key role on molecular packing modes, intermolecular interactions, charge transport properties as well as electronic properties. Aforementioned extension of $\pi$ conjugation framework for compact molecular packing and efficient charge transport is also applicable for conjugated polymers. However, there are also some special points for conjugated polymer backbones to achieve high carrier mobility. (i) One affecting factor is the symmetry of conjugated building block, which may significantly affect the intermolecular $\pi \mathfrak { - } \pi$ stacking, lamellar packing and crystallinity. A systematical investigation of the correlation between polymer symmetry and electronic property has been carried out by Pei et al.[128] based on IDD-based polymers. It was found that generally higher carrier mobility could be observed for centrosymmetric polymers than that of axisymmetric polymers with similar conjugated backbone, providing a new concept in searching for new conjugated building block, which was also called "molecular docking' strategy by the authors. Such a molecular design concept of centrosymmetric effect of conjugated backbone is also applicable for other conjugated polymer systems, such as DPP-based polymers. Combination of centrosymmetric effect of polymer backbones together with side chain and molecular weight effect, high mobilities of approaching $8 . 3 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ has been observed for $\mathrm { P D V T - C _ { 1 0 } }$ and PDTT $\mathrm { C _ { 8 } C _ { 1 0 } }$ respectively, which have set the new mobility records for conjugated polymers.23,27] (ii) Alternating D-A copolymer structure provides us a feasible approach to modulate the electronic properties of resulting materials. However, when processing this strategy, we should keep in mind that proper adjustment of the donor and acceptor strength is crucial to prevent extensive ground State (I) transferring to State (II) (Figure 11c), avoiding the transistion of from semiconductor to conductor. Although almost all the D-A copolymers exhibit typical p-type behavior, guided by this D-A molecular design concept, superior ambipolar or even n-type conjugated polymers have been developed by increasing the strength of accepting unit. Introduction of stronger accepting moiety, such as BTT moiety in the conjugated backbone, both high balanced hole and electron mobility of $1 . 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, were found for PBBTT"r,[146] which were further improved to $1 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (hole mobility) and $1 . 3 2 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (electron mobility) for PBBT $\mathrm { \cdot } \mathrm { C } _ { 1 2 }$ DPP.[147] Similarly, further increasing the strength of accepting unit, such as with NDT acceptor, very high electron mobility of $0 . 8 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was observed for P(NDI2OD-T2) (not shown here), which is also the highest electron mobility reported so far for conjugated polymers.2] The chemical structures of representative D-A copolymers mentioned above are shown in Figure 12.

# 3. Process Engineering for High Mobility OFETs

Rational molecular design has led to remarkable breakthrough in developing high mobility organic semiconductors from the aspects of changing molecular structures to modulate their molecular packing modes in solid state through chemical approach. As a complex system, OFET performance is also significantly affected by many other factors besides the intrinsic properties of organic semiconductors. Generally, for a given organic semiconductor, large variations (sometimes up to orders of magnitude difference) in substantial device-to-device performance usually exist. One major reason can be ascribed to the large variations of properties in the resulting active organic layer including film morphologies, microstructures, molecular orders/alignments as well as molecular solid state phases, which can severely affect the charge carrier transport in the conducting channel, and thus the whole device performance.[29,148] It is generally accepted that the ideal molecular solid structures for efficient charge carrier transport in OFETs should meet the fundamental requirements, such as smooth film surface, reduced grain boundaries and large crystalline domain sizes, etc. Moreover, in each crystalline domain the molecular packing and orientation is also needed to be optimized for a maximized $\pi$ orbital overlap or $\pi$ conjugation polymer chain along the direction of charge transport for efficient carrier transport for polymers.28,57] Therefore, a careful optimization of fabrication process such as deposition techniques (vacuum deposition or solution process), deposition parameters (deposition rate, substrate temperature, substrate surface properties, solvents used and solution concentration, etc.) as well as post process (such as thermal annealing and solvent annealing) is required and imperative especially for those materials that theoretically could display high intrinsic carrier mobility with promising applications in organic electronics.29,149,150] Instead of making a comprehensive summary for all the aspects of progress in this field, in this section, with some representative examples we will specially focus on the important points that particularly relate to tuning the molecular packing modes including orders, orientation, polymorphs, as well as crystal lattice from such a physical approach.

# 3.2. Tuning Molecular Polymorphism

The electronic properties of organic semiconductors are also strongly dependent on their solid state phases. Compared to inorganic molecules, the presence of multiple solid state phases is a distinguished feature for most of organic molecules due to the weak intermolecular interactions, which are strongly dependent on both the intrinsic molecular structures and the processing conditions.11729,166] Generally speaking, there are two typical polymorphs for organic semiconductors, one is the more thermally stable polymorph I (also called bulk' or 'singlecrystal' phase) and another is the metastable polymorph II (thin film' phase), the latter of which is more sensitive to the deposition process. Take pentacene as an example, there are at least five thin film' polymorphs, that is lamellar, pyramidal, inclined, dendritic and giant grain depending on the thermal deposition parameters as well as the substrate properties,[167] resulting in different field-effect performance. Therefore, to improve the charge carrier transport properties, rational control of the processing conditions for enhancing intermolecular interactions and maximizing electron orbital overlap is crucial for further improvement of charge carrier mobility in OFETs. Vacuum thermal evaporation is an ideal deposition method for most of small organic semiconductors. Deposition rate and substrate temperature are two important parameters widely used to control the films growth mode as well as the solid microstructures. It is generally accepted that low deposition rate and high substrate temperature is preferred to achieving large crystalline domain, leading to efficient charge carrier transport and high carrier mobility.[29] From the dynamic point of view, at higher substrate temperature, the deposited molecules have sufficient energy to overcome substrate interactions and thereby prefer to stand on the substrate (edge-on orientation) and make it possible to self-assemble into large domains, while lower deposition rate provides enough time to the molecules to rearrange themselves and form condensed packing modes with high order.[29] This method is especially effective for organic semiconductors with multiple phases displaying thermodynamic properties. For example, TiOPc is a classical organic semiconductor which possesses polymorph including $\alpha \cdot$ phase, $\beta$ phase and $\gamma$ phase. Structural analysis demonstrates that in the triclinic $\alpha$ structure, TiOPc molecules form concave and convex pairs with significant intermolecular overlaps and very short intermolecular distances (3.145 $\mathring \mathrm { A }$ for the convex pair and 3.211 A for the concave pair), indicating possibility of efficient charge carrier transport in such an ideal 2D lamellar packing (Figure 14a-b). However, the other two phases are unfavorable for efficient charge carrier transport due to the reduced intermolecular $\pi$ orbital overlaps and weak intermolecular interactions. P3HT has been widely investigated and large variation with a factor of more than 100 in carrier mobility was observed when different solvents or deposition processes were used.69] Sirringhaus et al. found that the sig. nificant difference of carrier mobility for P3HT films was mainly due to the different lamellar structures, face-on and edgeon, which are strongly dependent on the processing conditions and severely affect the charge carrier transport.129 In planar OFET devices, edge-on molecular packing is superior in efficient charge carrier transport due to two-dimensional charge carrier transport along both intrachain and interchain directions. Further systematic studies demonstrated high boiling point solvent (such as 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene) and low solvent evaporation process (such as dip-coating or drop casting) are more helpful for the formation of desirable edge-on molecule packing for conjugated polymers with high crystallinity and strong intermolecular interactions, resulting in higher carrier mobilit.117,171] Such experience from self-organization of P3HT molecules was successfully applied to other conjugated polymer systems and high performance OFET devices were produced.172] Meanwhile, improved solution process such as solvent-assisted re-annealing process,173,174] slow self-assembly process under a certain solvent vapor[153] are also developed to control molecular packing as well as molecular orientation/ orders in the thin film' phases or bulk phases for improvement Of charge carrier mobility.

# 3.3. Tuning Crystal Lattice of Organic Molecules

The crystal lattice of organic molecular packing can also be tuned through optimizing the fabrication processes. This concept of lattice strain has been widely applied in inorganic semiconductors to increase the charge carrier mobility with a strained heterojunction films.[75] Recently, Bao et al. found that the $\pi \mathfrak { - } \pi$ stacking distance of TIPS-PEN (a representative organic semiconductor with ideal 2D lamellar packing mode and good solution processability) can be strained by optimizing the solution-shearing process (Figure 15a).(15] Detailed investigations demonstrated that the $\pi \mathfrak { - } \pi$ stacking distance along the (010) axis could be systematically decreased from 3.33 A to $3 . 0 8 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ along with the increase of shearing speed from $0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ $\mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to $2 . 8 \ \mathrm { m i n \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ , thus leading to an enhanced electron orbital overlap and electronic couplings between adjacent molecules (with calculated transfer integral of T1 from $1 1 . 7 ~ \mathrm { \ m e V }$ to $3 6 . 9 \ \mathrm { m e V } )$ and subsequently increase of charge transport properties. However, on the other hand, the lattice strain induced in the TIPs-PEN molecular packing along (010) direction also results in a molecular displacement along long and short axis, and an increase of crystal lattice along other direction, such as (101) axis (Figure 15b-d), so the maximized charge carrier mobility of strained TIPs-PEN films could only be obtained when with the charge transport direction is along the direction with large $\pi$ orbital overlap direction (Figure 15e), indicating the importance of molecular packing orientation relative to the conducting channel. And a high mobility of around 4.6 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for strained TIPs-PEN films prepared at a speed of $2 . 8 \ \mathrm { m m \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ , nearly six times higher than that of unstrained films in the identical condition (Figure 15f). This research provides us a novel concept that charge carrier mobility can be further improved by tuning the molecular packing of organic semiconductors especially straining the $\pi \mathfrak { - } \pi$ stacking distance for enhanced electron orbital overlap through a physical approach.

# 4. Interface Engineering for High Mobility OFETs

As a complex system comprising various components, including semiconducting layer, source (S)/drain (D) electrode, gate electrode and insulator layer, the ultimate performance of OFETs is not only determined by the intrinsic properties of each functional component, but also severely affected by the nature of their contact interface.176] During the OFET operation, when a gate voltage is applied, charges are first injected from the source electrode into the organic semiconductor and then accumulate at the interface between the gate dielectric and the organic semiconductor. When a source-drain voltage is applied, charge carriers transport in the conducting channel of the transistor from the source to the drain forming source-- drain current. Obviously, two crucial interfaces: the electrode/ semiconductor interface (I) and the insulator/semiconductor interface (II) play important roles on the charge transport properties and thus the device performance (Figure 16). Of course, other interfaces such as organic/organic layer (III) and organic layer/atmosphere interface (IV) can also affect the performance of organic electronic devices, which will not been discussed in detail here. Comprehensive summary regarding the interface engineering in OFETs has been reported in recent review articles,33,176-178] here we will briefly summarize the key requirements from the aspect of interface engineering of (I) and (II) to afford efficient charge injection and transport as well as good stability for high performance OFETs, hopefully providing constructive guideline for further investigation in organic electronics.

# 4.1. Electrode/Semiconductor Interface: Towards Efficient Charge Injection as well as Charge Transport

To ensure eficient charge carrier injection, appropriate source/ drain electrodes possessing electronic energy levels matching with that of organic semiconductor layer are necessary for the reduce of contact resistance and efficient carrier transport. Additionally, the interface properties of source/drain electrodes also play an important role on the growth of organic semiconducting layer near the bottom source/drain electrodes, which then influence the film morphology and molecular orientation, and thus affect the charge carrier transport. Three approaches have been effectively applied for optimizing the electrode/ semiconductor interface to ensure high performance OFETs: (I) modifying the metal electrode with self-assembly monolayer (SAM) to not only modulate the work functions of metal electrodes but also improve the contact compatibilit;179,180] (I1) inserting buffer layer between electrode and semiconducting layer for both decreasing injection barriers and preventing penetration of metal atoms or other unfavorable $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } / \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ into the organic laer for high device stability181-13, (I1) developing new electrodes, such as polymer electrodes to optimize the electrode/organic semiconductor interface as well as reduce the cost of device fabrication[183,184 (Figure 17a). To date, the widely used SAMs are thiol-based monolayers, which can be anchored to various metal surfaces through the M-S bond, such as Au-S and Ag-S. The adjustment of SAM properties including their types, chain lengths, functional-end groups, etc., can lead to the significant improvement of device performance. For example, with bottom-contact P3HT field-effect transistors as a research model, Hamadani et al.[180] found that by using an electronpoor fluorinated oligo(phenylene ethynylene) (F-OPE) SAM to modify the gold electrodes, an enhanced charge transport property was achieved due to the reduced contact resistance, while with an electron-rich SAM $( \mathrm { N H } _ { 2 } . \mathrm { O P E } )$ modifying the gold electrode, high hole injection barrier resulted in low device performance, suggesting a general experience that electron-rich SAM modification layer can lead to higher metal work function versus electron-poor SAM modification leading to lower metal work function. On this basis, a series of aromatic thiolSAM treatment was used to modulate the work function of silver electrode in p-type transistors, and the highest mobility of $0 . 1 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for TIPS-PEN transistors with the strongest electron-poor SAMs, pentafluorothiophenol (PFTP)- modified silver as electrode (from the initial value of $- 4 . 7 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for Ag electrode increased to $- 5 . 3 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for PFTP-Ag electrode) due to the ideal Ohmic contact.179] The length of SAM alkyl chain length also exerts crucial influence on the charge carrier injection and characteristics of OFETs.193] By utilizing a series of alkyl chains from C2-C16 modified the gold electrode in pentacene FETs, Stoliar et al. found an interesting 'odd-even' effect on the carrier mobility, where the devices with even-numbered SAMs modified electrode showed in higher charge carrier mobility compared to that of devices with odd-numbered SAMs modified electrode, while an exponential decay occurred when alkyl chain length exceeding ${ \mathrm { C } } _ { 8 }$ . This phenomenon was probably ascribed to the anisotropic coupling between the alkanethiol terminal o bond and the HOmO level of ordered pentacene molecules, providing us a new concept for optimizing the electrode/semiconductor interface from the aspect of selecting alkyl SAMs. Besides SAMs modification, Di et al.[177] developed an effective interface approach for low-cost electrodes, such as copper and silver, the low work function of which makes them unsuitable for most of p-type organic semiconductors. Through chemically modifying the Cu or Ag electrodes with 7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (TCNQ), significantly improved fieldeffect mobility could be achieved for most of p-type OFETs due to the enhanced work function for efficient charge injection as well as the optimized organic layer growth near the electrodes. Such a concept of induced crystallization of an organic layer by electrode interface modification[185] has been further successfully applied for controlling the location and crystal growth of various organic layers on the pre-patterned electrodes for largearea OFET devices. A mobility of as high as $4 . 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ has been achieved for the patterned-rubrene devices, comparable to that of single crystal, due to the combination effect of enhanced crystalline grain sizes, minimized grain boundaries as well as good electrode/semiconductor contact. Moreover, the concept of introducing appropriate buffer layer between electrode and organic semiconductor layer has also been well applied to reduce the charge injection barriers as well as improve device stability, which is applicable for both BC and TC device configuration. Significantly enhanced device performance has been achieved for pentacene-based transistors coupling a $\mathrm { M O } _ { 3 }$ layer between pentacene and electrode due to the reduction of contact barrier $( \mathrm { - 0 . 9 e V } )$ and the prevention of penetration of metal atoms into the pentacene active layer.[182] Figure $1 7 \mathrm { b }$ summarizes the work functions of some representative metal electrodes and their corresponding modified electrodes, suggesting the vital importance of electrode/semiconductor interface engineering for modulating the applications of electrodes in OFETs.

# 4.2. Insulator/Semiconductor Interface: Towards Efficient Charge Transport as well as Good Device Stability

Insulator/semiconductor interface, another crucial important interface in OFETs, significantly influences the charge carrier transport as well as the device stability. Since charge carrier transport in OFETs is mainly located in the first few monolayers of an organic layer next to the dielectric surface, the properties of insulator surface including charge trap states, contact compatibility with the top-deposited organic layer significantly influence the efficient formation of conducting channel, growth mode of organic layer and molecular orders/orientation in turn affect the whole device carrier mobility. Moreover, insulator/ semiconductor interface can also dramatically affect the device stability especially for n-type OFETs from the aspects of localizing the electron transport induced by the surface traps, film instability/aggregation induced by the mismatched surface energy, etc.176] To ensure high charge carrier transport as well as good device stability, low density of charge traps as well as good contact compatibility are crucial requirements for insulator/semiconductor interface engineering. Similar to electrode/semiconductor interface, modification of the interface with chemically-bonded SAMs is also an effective approach to improve the insulator surface property. For the generally used $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ substrate, there are abundant of -OH groups existing on the surface, which usually work as the interfacial charge traps, significantly inhibiting the charge transport. Improvement for both hole and electron mobilities could be improved through such a SAM-modified approach. This concept has also been applied by inserting a polymer buffer layer, such as BCB, which is an excellent hydroxyl-free material, and whereby n-channel FET conduction and/or ambipolar FET behavior could be observed for most conjugated polymers, such as P3HT, which normally only show only p-channel activity.[186] Simultaneously, high performance n-type poly(benzobisimidazobenzophenan throline) (BBL) polymer thin film transistors, with excellent device stabilities, have been achieved by Kim et al.[187] through modification of the dielectric/semiconductor interfaces with a polymer buffer layer. The authors carried out a systematic study on the effect of the polymer dielectric constant on the n-type device performance and found that the electron mobility and device stability could be significantly increased by decreasing the dielectric constant of the polymer layers due to the reduced energetic expense of the charge-carrier/dipole interaction.

# 5. Other Factors

Efficient charge carrier transport requires that the charges can move from molecule to molecule and not be trapped or scattered during this process. Besides molecular packing modes, molecular orders in solid state and interface (insulator or electrode) properties as we mentioned above, charge carrier transport is also influenced by many other factors including presence of impurities, testing temperature, applied electric field and external pressure etc. The goal of this section is to emphasize the effect of these factors, some of which can be skillfully used to extend the application fields of OFETs, such as sensors and other fields.

# 5.1. Impurities

Although they usually appear in very small concentration (even lower than 1 ppm), impurities could dramatically influence the charge carrier transport and the device performance. For example, the presence of impurities can induce amounts of defects and trapping states in the energy gap, immobilizing or scattering charge carrier transport and in turn suppressing the whole device performance.37,188,189] Additionally, these impurities can also act as unintentional dopants to increase the conductivity of organic materials, leading to high "off" current and thereby low "on/off" ratio for OFETs.190-193] Moreover, some kind of impurities can also serve as catalyst for the slow deg. radation of semiconductors, leading to poor device stability.[194] Hence, the presence of any impurities and defects in organic semiconductors is a critical limitation for further enhancement of related device performance, hindering the measurement of intrinsic charge transport properties and observation of a variety of new phenomena in these materials, such as the quantum Hall effect, ballistic charge transport, or superconductivity etc.136,173,195,196 Particular attention has been paid on the impurities of organic semiconductors in the past few years. Depending on the types and sources of impurities, various methods have been explored to remove the impurities existing in organic semiconducting materials. Considering that amounts of impurities (such as residual catalysts, or some insoluble byproducts, etc.) and defects (such as structural defects) are directly introduced during the chemical synthesis process, the choice of suitable synthetic methodology plays a critical role on the materials' purity, both for small molecule and polymeric organic semiconductors.97-199 Actually, organic impurities especially that are structurally similar to the semiconductor are often very difficult to be separated from the desired material and some impurities are formed during the later degradation or deposition process. So further purification processes are needed and of them, multiple vacuum sublimation is the most common used and effective approach to purify materials, especially for small organic semiconductors.200] For example, pentacene, is very unstable and easily degrades with the formation of oxygenated species, such as dihydropentacene and pentacenequinone, and after careful removal of these species before single crystal growth, untrahigh field effect mobility of $5 8 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { V s }$ could be reached.37] Rubrene, another star organic semiconductor, also suffers from easy decomposition and a significant improvement of electron carrier mobility of $0 . 8 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { V s }$ was achieved by performing multiple purifications of rubrene single crystals, which was usually suppressed in the impurity samples.[188] Moreover, oxygen and water in ambient condition also usually serve as the impurities in organic semiconductors and affect their transport properties. Removal of impurities is crucial for high mobility OFETs though it is still highly challenging for the availability of ultrapure organic materials, especially for polymeric semiconductors, which suffer from inevitable batch to batch variation and structural imperfection.

# 5.2. External Conditions

The charge carrier mobility of OFET devices is also strongly dependent on the testing conditions including the testing parameters, such as temperature and electric field as well as the external atmosphere. Band-model description and hopping transport mechanism are two mainly models generally used to describe the charge transport process in single crystals and disordered materials. No matter whether it is band-like transport or hopping transport, the carrier mobility is remarkably related to both temperature and applied electric field. Theoretically, low temperature and low electric field is favorable for high charge carrier mobility in ultrapure single crystal with a standard band-like transport process; while in disordered materials determined by the hopping transport, high temperature and high electric field in an appropriate range can lead to high charge carrier transport properties.1173] However, from a pointview of application room temperature and low electric field is desirable. Additionally, charge carrier mobility is also influenced by the external conditions during operation, such as humidity, gas and pressure. For example, a systematic investigation of the relationship between carrier mobility and humidity demonstrated that there was about $43 \%$ drop in the mobility of PDVT-10- based transistors when relative humidity was changed from $20 \%$ to $78 \%$ [23] On the other hand, the dependence of carrier mobility on these external conditions provides us new application fields of organic electronic sensors, such as humidity sensors, gas sensors as well as pressure sensors.[100,110,201,202]

# 6. Conclusions and Perspectives

As a novel emerging science, OFETs have attracted worldwide attention since 1980s, from academia to industry over the past decades. Remarkable progress has been made in developing high performance OFETs and the state-of-the-art mobility of OFETs has approached the values of polycrystalline silicon $( > 1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ thanks to the integrated contributions of molecular design engineering, processing engineering, interface engineering as well as other aspects. The successful fabrication of integrated prototype electronic devices based on OFETs, such as RFID, electronic paper and flexible displays indicates their gradual maturity from laboratory to industry.

However, there are still some fundamental challenges in this field: (i) the correlation of molecular structure and electronic property is still unclear, although from the point of theoretical calculation 2D lamellar packing mode is preferable for high mobility, many organic semiconcuctors reported with high mobility adopt herringbone packing motif, such as pentacene, rubrene, BTBT-derivatives; (ii) some effective chemical approches have been developed successfully for the design of organic semiconductors with high performance, however, a theory-guided molecular design engineering is urgently necessary for the realization of molecular rational design; (iii) most recent reported organic semiconductors with high performance are in crystalline state, which exhibit seriously anisotropic charge transport and are unfavorable for large-area fabrication process, while high mobility amorphous organic semiconductors with isotropic charge transport properties are crucial for large-area, low-cost organic electronics; (iv) the long-term and dynamic operation stability of devices needs further improvment concerning the industry application; () currently, hightemperature fabrication or annealing process for both small and polymer semiconductors are generally required to obtain films with high crystallinity, which is crucial for efficient charge transport. However, this high-temperature process not only makes the fabrication process complex but also increases the energy consumption. (vi) the development of solution-processable organic semiconductors, especially with enviromentallyfriendly solvents, is also improtant for the practical application of organic electronics for reducing environmental pollution.
