# Optical Design of Transparent Thin Metal Electrodes to Enhance In-Coupling and Trapping of Light in Flexible Polymer Solar Cells

Jose-Francisco Salinas, Hin-Lap Yip, Chu-Chen Chueh, Chang-Zhi Li, Jose-Luis Maldonado, and Alex K.-Y. Jen\*

Polymer solar cells (PsCs) have potential to be used as flexible, lightweight, and low-cost solid-state power sources for clean energy production if their performance can be further improved.1 Because the rather short exciton diffusion length in organic semiconductors, it is challenging to improve PSC performance due to this "exciton bottleneck" limits the active layer thickness for efficient light absorption. Although polymer/ fullerene bulk heterojunction (BHJ) has been employed to increase the number of interfaces for facilitating charge separation, the low carrier mobilities and tortuous transport paths in these structures increase recombination losses in thicker devices.l Therefore, it is important to develop efficient lighttrapping structures that can increase light absorption and at the same time be compatible with processes used for fabricating high-performance flexible thin film PSCs.[3]

In general, a BHJ-based solar cell consists of several photoactive and charge selective layers that are sandwiched between two conductive electrodes. Optical transparency for at least one electrode is required for harvesting light. Currently, the most commonly used electrodes are mainly based on metal oxides that combine high transparency and low sheet resistance, such as indium tin oxide (ITO), fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO), and aluminum doped zinc oxide $\left( \mathrm { Z n O : A l l } \right)$ [4] In particular, ITO combines both high optical transparency in the visible range $( - 8 2 \% )$ and low resistivity $( 1 0 { - } 2 0 \Omega / \bigsqcup )$ enabling it to be the most commonly used transparent electrode for PSCs.

However, indium is a rare and valuable element used in manufacturing ITO-based flat panel displays and portable electronics. Due to rapid expansion of the consumer electronics market and natural scarcity of indium, the price for ITO has increased significantly in recent years. Therefore, it is critical to find viable options that can replace ITO.5,6] Besides, the low mechanical ductility and high sheet resistance $( \sim 6 0 ~ \Omega / \Pi )$ of ITO on flexible substrates also need to be improved.[7-9]

It is known that the performance of large-area solar cells is dictated by their series resistance which significantly affects fill factor and PCE.10-12] Therefore, the proposed ITO replacement should possess even lower resistance than ITO. Several alternatives have been tried, including the use of conducting polymers,[13] carbon nanotubes,[14] graphene flms,[15] metal nanowires,[16] metal grids,[17] and ultrathin metal films (UTMF)[18] Among them, metal grids and UTMF combine the characteristics of high electrical conductivity of metals and good mechanical ductility of thin films $( - 5 { - } 4 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ . Especially, the UTMFs posses good compatibility with most organic materials and can be simply applied by thermal evaporation. They are also compatible with large-area coating process used for metalized plastic films in food packaging.

However, the application of UTMFs has been limited by their considerable absorption and reflection, which cause poorer device performance than ITO. Among all metals attempted, $\mathrm { A g }$ exhibits the lowest resistivity $( 1 . 6 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 8 } \Omega \mathrm { m } = 1 . 6 2 \mu \Omega \mathrm { c m } )$ the highest optical transparency, and is extremely ductile (only surpassed by Au), making it the preferred choice for UTMF electrodes. The conductivity and optical properties of thin metal films are strongly dependent on the quality of the films such as roughness, grain size, and film continuity, which is governed by the nucleation and growth kinetics of metal on a particular substrate.

The initial steps of Ag thin film growth have been extensively studied and identified with different growth modes, like the Volmer-Weber mode (i.e., island mode) or the StranskiKrastanov mode (ie. layer-plus-island mode).[19-21] They are strongly dependent on the type of substrate and the rate of vacuum deposition. In particular, the poor wettability of $\mathrm { A g }$ on electrically insulating substrates often leads to undesirable rough surface morphologies, high sheet resistances, and poor optical quality.[22]

It is known that conductivity and optical quality of UTMFs can be improved considerably by applying an adequate seed layer. For example, the wetting angles of molten silver over substrates treated with $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ $( - 5 0 ^ { \circ } )$ have been reported to be much lower than that using other metal oxides like $\mathrm { Z n O }$ or $\mathrm { T a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 }$ $( \sim 9 0 ^ { \circ } )$ .[23] $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ not only are transparent to visible light but also have quite low melting points ( $7 3 3 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $7 9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ respectively), allowing them to be easily evaporated over glass or plastic substrates before the deposition of silver.

PSC determines the local electromagnetic field in the cell and the charge generation within the BHJ. The transfer matrix method (TMM) is a mathematic formalism that can be successfully applied to estimate the electric field in the stack.[24,25] This calculation requires knowledge of the optical properties of the materials specified by the complex refractive index $( n ( \lambda ) = \eta ( \lambda ) +$ $i \kappa ( \lambda ) )$ . The imaginary part $\kappa ,$ is known as the extinction coefficient and is responsible for absorption in the medium, the real part $\eta$ determines the wavelength and speed of light in the material.

Since charge generation is affected by the spatial distribution of the optical electric field within the device; a careful selection of film thickness or insertion of additional transparent layers as optical spacers will contribute to higher current densities and improve overall performance.[26,27]

In this work, we have applied a high refractive index $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ layer to facilitate the growth of smooth, highly conductive, and optically transparent thin silver films by thermal evaporation. By coupling with extensive optical design and high refractive index films, it allow us to improve the collection and trapping of light, leading to superior device performance that even exceeds that using ITO as electrode. Although similar approaches have been tried in improving the performance of OLED and PSC devices,[28,29] our result shows the highest efficiency.

When a very thin silver electrode $( 1 2 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ is considered, a portion of the incoming light is absorbed or reflected in the UTMF, reducing the total intensity within the device (Figure 2a, red plot). By introducing a highly transparent $( \kappa \sim 0 )$ , high refractive index $( \eta \sim 2 . 1 5 )$ $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film of approximately a quarter wavelength (more details in the SI) in resonance with the $\mathsf { A g \mathrm { - } A g }$ microcavity, the total reflection of the UTMF can be dramatically reduced and results in an increased electric field within the BHJ.

Because every wavelength of light absorbed will contribute to the overall performance of the device, it requires tuning of both active layer and $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ thicknesses to maximize the field intensity over the whole optical range in order to optimize the device performance. The exciton generation rate was calculated at each point in the BHJ by using the electric field calculated by TMM for all meaningful wavelengths $( 3 0 0 - 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , Figure 2b), the absorbance in the BHJ $( \alpha = 4 \pi \kappa / \lambda )$ , and the amount of photons associated with the source of light (intensity spectrum of the solar simulator).

Assuming IQE is $100 \%$ , the rates of exciton generation and charge generation will be the same, therefore, we can estimate an ideal current density with no loses. $\left( \mathrm { J _ { s c - 1 0 0 \% } } \right)$ by integrating the generation rate over the whole active layer. As a result, the election of the thickness of both active layer and $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film that maximizes $\mathrm { J _ { s c - 1 0 0 \% } }$ is an efficient way to optimize the performance. The real thicknesses of $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ and BHJ layers used in our devices $\langle d _ { T e O 2 } =$ $4 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ $d _ { B H J } = 6 6 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ are very close to the ones determined by the TMM modeling (considering the whole optical range) to maximize $\mathrm { J _ { s c - 1 0 0 \% } }$ $( d _ { T e O 2 } = 4 0 \mathrm { n m }$ $d _ { B H J } = 6 5 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 2b).

To clarify the effect of high refractive index layer on the $\int _ { \mathrm { s c - 1 0 0 \% } }$ value, we have simulated two materials, $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 } \left( \eta \sim 1 . 7 5 \right)$ and $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ $( \eta \sim 2 . 1 5 )$ , and compared the results with the same device using ITO as electrode (Figure 3a). Both materials have a positive effect on the generated current. However, the higher refractive index $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ allows the system to reach higher $\int _ { \mathrm { s c - 1 0 0 \% } }$ (when a $4 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film is used) than that calculated for ITO.

Concerning the thickness of $\mathrm { A g }$ for electrode, a too thin or too thick $\mathrm { A g }$ film will lead to high sheet resistance (originated by electron scattering from the surface and grain boundaries) or low transparency.8,33] Fortunately, high transparency and low sheet resistance $( \mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { s h e e t - T e O 2 } } = 7 . 0 2 \ \Omega \ \Pi ^ { - 1 }$ compared to $1 3 . 5 3 ~ \Omega ~ \bigcirc ^ { - 1 }$ for ITO) can be achieved when high refractive index $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ is used in combination with $1 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ thick Ag. The detailed relationship of transmission and resistance as a function of Ag thickness is depicted in SI, Figure S1. Although the transparency of the hybrid $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { A g }$ electrode is lower than ITO, the resulting microcavity resonant condition amplifies the electric field within the cell to give higher performance than the ITO based devices.

The PCE and $\mathrm { J } _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ of devices on glass and PEN with different $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ thickness are plotted in Figure 3c and 3d. In both cases, the best device performance was found to be the one based on using a $4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ which fits very well with the results from simulation. The J-V curves of the best device based on ITO and $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { A g }$ coated on glass and PEN are presented in Figure 3b. Due to high deposition rate used, it is difficult to keep exactly the same thickness of $\mathrm { A g }$ for each device. The slight fuctuations observed in Figure 3c,d are attributed to small variation of the $\mathrm { A g }$ thickness that changes the transparency or conductivity of the films which affects device performance. The PCE of the devices using $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ $( 4 0 \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { A g }$ substrates $( \mathrm { P C E _ { g l a s s \cdot A g } } = 6 . 6 \%$ $\mathrm { P C E _ { P E N \cdot A g } } = 5 . 8 \% )$ surpasses those observed on ITO $( \mathrm { P C E } _ { \mathrm { g l a s s \mathrm { - } I T O } } = 6 . 5 \% ,$ $\mathrm { P C E } _ { \mathrm { P E N - I T O } } = 5 . 6 \% )$ . Although few reports have also shown good device performance when UTMFs were used, the highest achieved efficiency is only $4 . 4 \%$ [29] Therefore, this is very encouraging that high efficiency PSC can be achieved.

To evaluate possible degradation that may be caused by mechanical bending of flexible PSC, a series of tests have been performed on devices with ITO and $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { A g }$ electrode on PEN (as illustrated in Figure 4) using a cylinder (radius $R = 0 . 6 4 ~ \mathrm { c m }$ to keep the same bending curvature. The ITO based flexible PSC exhibited a dramatic decrease of its performance starting from the first bending and continued to decrease when bending cycles proceed. In contrast, the performance of the devices with $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ (40 nm)/Ag ( $1 2 \mathrm { n m } )$ electrode are much more stable even after greater than 100 bending cycles. In deed, the performance of the $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ based device increased slightly with the bends, growing from an original $\mathrm { P C E } = 5 . 7 \%$ to $5 . 8 \%$ after 91 bending cycles.

Although in principle $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film should not really affect the behavior of the cell because the Ag-Ag microcavity remains unchanged, the EQE data revealed that the resonant conditions in the microcavity could be affected by changing the $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ thickness. Since multiple layers are involved in a PSC device, it requires a delicate balance between their dimensions to allow proper charge formation and transport, while keeping the transparent electrode with optimal transmittance and sheet resistance. As an external element with no specific role in the electric dynamic of the cell, the $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film can be modified to facilitate light absorption to fully explore the potential of the system without affecting the operation of other elements in the cell.

In conclusion, enhanced light trapping and conductivity could be achieved by combining high refractive index $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ with thin Ag film as hybrid transparent UTMF electrode. As a result, PSCs with performance higher than those with ITO electrode on both glass $( \mathrm { P C E _ { T e O 2 / A g } } = 6 . 6 \%$ $\mathrm { P C E } _ { \mathrm { I T O } } = 6 . 5 \% )$ and plastic $( \mathrm { P C E } _ { \mathrm { T e O 2 / A g } } = 5 . 8 \%$ $\mathrm { P C E } _ { \mathrm { I T O } } = 5 . 6 \% )$ substrates could be achieved. The flexible devices on $\mathrm { P E N / T e O _ { 2 } / A g }$ also showed excellent bending stability compared to devices made from ITO electrode. The EQE of these cells could be easily tuned by simply changing the thickness of the $\mathrm { T e O } _ { 2 }$ film to offer the possibility of further optimizing light absorption in a cell without affecting the electric attributes of nano morphology based BHJ devices.

Device Fabrication: To avoid possible etching of $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ by the aqueous solution of PEDOT:PSS, a layer of PEDOT:PSS/IPA/butanol in proportion of 1/2/2 was applied first over the $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ substrate and then the aqueous solution of PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated. PhanQ $( 5 ~ \mathrm { m g / m L } )$ and $\mathsf { P C } _ { 7 7 } \mathsf { B M }$ $( 7 5 ~ \mathrm { \ m g / m L } )$ were dissolved in dichlorobenzene, stirred overnight at ${ 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C }$ and then spin-coated in a glove box under $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ atmosphere to afford a film of ${ \sim } 6 6 \ \mathsf { n m }$ . The surfactant film $( - 8 \mathsf { n m } )$ was also applied within the glove box. Finally, a $1 2 0 \mathsf { n m }$ -thick $\mathsf { A } \mathsf { g }$ film was then evaporated through a mask to define a PSC area of $\mathsf { 1 0 . 0 8 ~ m m ^ { 2 } }$ . The thickness of each layer was measured by using atomic force microscopy (AFM).

Device Characterization: The J-V measurements were performed under $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ using a Keithley 2400 source meter. A $3 0 0 \mathsf { W }$ xenon arc solar simulator (Oriel) with an AM 1.5 global filter operated at $\mathsf { l o o } \mathsf { m w } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ was used to simulate the AM 1.5G solar irradiation. The illumination intensity was corrected by using a silicon photodiode with a protective KG5 filter calibrated by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was recorded under monochromatic illumination with another $4 5 0 ~ \mathsf { W }$ xenon lamp in ambient atmosphere. The incident beam was chopped with a mechanical chopper, and the photocurrent was recorded with a lock-in-amplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR830). The irradiance spectrum of the solar simulator (used for the numerical simulation) was measured using an Ocean Optics spectrometer.

Optical Simulator and Curve Fitting: The TMm simulation assumes planar interfaces and isotropy for all the films. The optical nature of each material is represented by their complex refractive index $( n ~ = ~ \eta ~ + ~ i \kappa )$ , which one was acquired by using a Variable Angle Spectroscopic Ellipsometer from J. A. Woolam.
