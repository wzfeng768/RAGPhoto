# Mapping Polymer Donors toward High-Efficiency Fullerene Free Organic Solar Cells

Yuze Lin, Fuwen Zhao, Yang Wu, Kai Chen, Yuxin Xia, Guangwu Li, Shyamal K. K. Prasad, Jingshuai Zhu, Lijun Huo, Haijun Bin, Zhi-Guo Zhang, Xia Guo, Maojie Zhang, Yanming Sun, Feng Gao, Zhixiang Wei, Wei Ma, Chunru Wang, Justin Hodgkiss, Zhishan Bo, Olle Inganas, Yongfang Li, and Xiaowei Zhan\*

Organic solar cells (OsCs) based on electron donor (D)/electron acceptor (A) bulk heterojunction (BHJ)[1] are a promising costeffective alternative for utility of solar energy, and have attracted much attention because of their advantages, such as low cost, light weight, and the capability to fabricate flexible large-area modules.2-7] In past years, a large number of electron donor materials including small molecules and polymers were developed to match the typical fullerene acceptors, and fullerenebased OsCs exhibited power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of $1 0 \% { - } 1 1 \%$ [8-15] In the fullerene-based BHJ, donor materials make major contributions to photocurrents, because fullerene acceptors have very weak absorption in the visible and nearinfrared (NIR) region. Key requirements for high-efficiency donors are narrow energy bandgap $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } )$ and broad absorption to utilize sufficient solar energy, and low-lying highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level to achieve high photovoltage. For high-performance narrow-bandgap absorbers $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } < 1 . 6 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ there is a trade-off between high open circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ and short circuit current density $( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } )$ . This trade-off feature is one of the main limitations for further development of OsCs.[16]

Relative to fullerene acceptors, fused-ring electron acceptors (FREAs)17) have flexible electronic energy levels and easily tuned absorption range from blue to red light, even extending into NIR region. Generally, one organic semiconductor is unlikely to have silicon-like absorption ability to cover the visible and NIR solar spectrum $( 3 0 0 - 1 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Thus, it is very critical for FREAs to have complementary absorption with electron donors in visible-NIR range.

So far, considerable efforts have been dedicated to the development of FREAs.[18,19] Imide-functionalized rylene acceptors[20-28] are usually synthesized by multistep reactions for a subtle balance between charge transport and molecular aggregation, and have shown PCEs of $8 \%$ in devices.29-33] In comparison, FREAs based on electron-donating extended fused rings (e.g., indacenodithiophene (IDT) and indacenodithieno[3,2-b]thiophene (IDTT)) with acceptor-donor-acceptor (A-D-A) structure are easily synthesized by simple reactions, and have exhibited PCEs of up to $9 \% - 1 1 \%$ [34 42] However, most reports on high-performance FREA-based OSCs focus on one or two specific D/A systems and do not systematically probe D/A compatibility.

A large number of high-performance donors were designed and explored for fullerene-based solar cells, while the selection criteria of donors for nonfullerene OsCs have been rarely investigated. Establishing general design guidance of donor materials in nonfullerene OsCs is more important but more complicated than that in fullerene OsCs as the performance of a nonfullerene acceptor heavily depends on the donor choice. In an attempt to identify promising donor materials for nonfullerene OsCs, we propose the following criteria as being important:

i) The energy levels of the donor need to be appropriate for a given high-performance nonfullerene acceptor to achieve driving force of exciton dissociation, small energy loss, and high $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ;$   
ii) The donor should have complementary absorption with the nonfullerene acceptor to achieve high incident photon to converted current efficiency (IPCE) and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } ,$ for example, wide-bandgap donors could be better choices for narrowbandgap FREAs, relative to typical narrow-bandgap donors which yield high PCEs when blending with fullerenes; and   
iii) The donor should have morphology compatibility with the acceptor to achieve ideal phase separation, good charge transport, high fill factor (FF), and $\operatorname { h i g h } J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ . For a fullerene acceptor, its spherical geometry allows it to be compatible with a large number of donor materials, whereas for a FREA acceptor, its flat geometry leads to its aggregation behavior different from that of fullerene.

Herein, we choose five polymer donors with distinct chemical structures with thiophene, benzodithiophene, and dithienobenzodithiophene on the main chains (Figure 1a), to blend with a planar FREA acceptor IDIC (Figure 1a), which exhibited high performance in fullerene free OSCs.36,40] The D/A compatibility is investigated by using as-cast OsC, which show PCEs of $5 . 2 \% - 9 . 2 \%$ Then we optimize two promising systems, PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC, by using 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) as solvent additive to tune the morphology and nanoscale phase separation; use of $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO boosts the PCE of OSCs based on PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC up to $1 0 . 3 7 \%$ and $1 1 . 0 3 \%$ , respectively. We probe charge photogeneration and recombination dynamics in the best system of PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC and find extremely efficient charge generation from both donor and acceptor channels.

Five polymer donors (PTB7-Th,[43] J51,[44] PDCBT,[45] PDBTT1,(46] and PTFBDT-BZS[47l) with different structure, absorption, and energy levels are selected to fabricate OsCs with structure of indium tin oxide (ITO), $\mathrm { \ Z n O } _ { I }$ active layer/ $\mathrm { M o O } _ { x } / \mathrm { A g }$ by blending with IDIC without additional treatments. Table 1 summarizes the device data of OsCs based on these polymer donor/IDIC pairs. Among these systems, wide-bandgap polymers (PDCBT, J51, PTFBDT-BZS, and PDBT-T1):IDIC-based as cast devices show PCEs of $6 . 2 8 \%$ $6 . 9 4 \%$ $8 . 0 6 \%$ , and $9 . 2 0 \%$ respectively (Figure 1b), which are higher than that of narrowbandgap polymer (PTB7-Th):IDIC-based devices $( 5 . 2 4 \% )$ . The $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of PTB7-Th:IDIC-based devices is smaller than those of the wide-bandgap polymers:IDIC-based devices with complementary absorption between PDCBT, J51, PTFBDT-BZS, or PDBT-T1 and IDIC (Figure S1, Supporting Information).

PTB7-Th:IDIC-based devices show obviously lower IPCE from 500 to $6 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 1c). The $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of OsCs calculated from IPCE is ${ < } 5 \%$ mismatch with those measured by $J { - } V$ measurements (Table 1). The $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of these devices is in the range of $0 . 8 \mathrm { - } 0 . 9 2 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ which is related to the HOMO of the donors (Figure S2, Supporting Information). PTFBDT-BZS with the deepest HOMO yields the highest $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ while PDBT-T1 with deeper HOMO yields higher $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ than PTB7-Th, PDCBT, and J51 with higher HOMO.

In addition to absorption and energy levels of D/A pairs, morphology miscibility is another critical factor to affect the photovoltaic performance. The morphology of as-cast BHJ films is investigated using atomic force microscope (AFM) and grazing incident wide-angle X-ray diffraction (GIWAXS). These five systems exhibit very smooth surface with small rootmean-square roughness of $0 . 6 { - } 1 . 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The GIWAXS results of these five systems are displayed in Figure S5 in the Supporting Information (the GIWAXS of IDIC neat film in Figure S4 in the Supporting Information). All as-cast films show similar sharp $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking peaks, which indicate ordered $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking. AFM images and GIwAXS analysis of these as-cast blended films suggest that IDIC has good miscibility in morphology and crystallinity with all polymers we used. The as-cast blend systems based on different polymers and IDIC exhibit hole mobilities of $( 1 . 7 – 4 . 0 ) \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and electron mobilities of $( 1 . 2 – 7 . 5 ) \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ measured by space charge limited current method (Figure S6 and Table S1, Supporting Information). J51:IDIC and PDBT-T1:IDIC exhibit relatively higher hole mobilities than PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC, PDCBT:IDIC, and PTB7- Th:IDIC, which are beneficial to higher FF.

Since as-cast OSCs based on PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDTBZS:IDIC exhibit the best performance among these five systems, we further optimize PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDTBZS:IDIC by using DIO as solvent additive (Figure 2a and Table 1). Relative to the as-cast devices, the devices based on PDBT-T1:IDIC with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO additive show much higher FF, slightly higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } ,$ and finally higher PCE $( 1 0 . 3 7 \% )$ . For 20 devices, the average PCE of PDBT-T1:IDIC-based OSCs with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO is $1 0 . 1 6 \%$ PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC based devices with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO show even higher PCE up to $1 1 . 0 3 \%$ (the average value of $1 0 . 9 7 \%$ for 20 devices), which is much higher than that of the as-cast devices $( 8 . 0 6 \% )$ . This PCE of over $1 1 \%$ is one of the highest values reported on single-junction OSCs, and the average PCE of 10.97 is the highest average value for fullerene-free OSCs. For both PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDTBZS:IDIC, solvent additive DIO improves the IPCE (Figure 2b). Both PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC show the IPCE maximum up to ca. $80 \%$ We measure current density under different light intensities $( P )$ to study charge recombination of the devices without and with DIO under the short circuit condition (Figure 2c). Both PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC without and with DIO show the relationship of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \propto \ P ^ { 1 . 0 }$ which indicates almost all free carriers are swept out and collected at the electrodes prior to bimolecular recombination.

To understand the role of the solvent additive DIO, the morphology of PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films without and with DIO additive is investigated and compared. From AFM images (Figure S7, Supporting Information), the PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO show increased roughness and crystalline size relative to their as-cast films. GIWAXS is used to compare the molecular-level structural information such as molecular orientation and packing of PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films without and with DIO solvent additive (Figure 3). At the first glance, we can observe that all the peaks become sharper/stronger with the addition of DIO, which indicates that DIO improves the molecular packing of both donor and IDIC in the blend films. The $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking peaks of PDBT-T1, PTFBDT-BZS, and IDIC are located at 1.70, 1.67, and $1 . 8 2 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively (extracted from the scattering profiles of pure films in Figure S4 in the Supporting Information). The coherence length (CL) of molecular packing along a certain direction can be calculated via Scherrer equation: $\mathrm { C L } = 2 \pi K / \mathrm { F W H M }$ where $K$ is a shape factor (here uses 0.9) and FWHM is the full-width at half-maximum of the peak.[48] The coherence length of PDBT-T1 and IDIC $\pi - \pi$ stacking is 2.8, $3 . 1 \mathrm { n m }$ and 4.5, $5 . 2 \mathrm { n m }$ for the blended films without and with DIO, respectively. The coherence length of PTFBDT-BZS and IDIC $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking is 2.4, $2 . 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and 4.9, $4 . 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the blended films without and with DIO, respectively. The $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking coherence length of these two donor polymers and IDIC is improved after processed with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO. The enhanced $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking ordering is beneficial to the charge transport; the PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT:IDIC with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO show higher hole and electron mobilities (Table S1 and Figure S8, Supporting Information) relative to their as-cast films. Therefore, PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films processed with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO exhibit higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF, finally higher PCE, and IPCE contributed by donor and acceptor is improved. The $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking of these four blended films in the out-of-plane direction is stronger than that in the in-plane direction, which indicates a predominant "face-on' molecular orientation relative to the electrode substrates. The vertical $\pi - \pi$ stacking is favorable to charge transport between anode and cathode of solar cells.

Resonant soft X-ray scattering (R-SoXs) is used to probe the phase separation in PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films. Soft X-ray which has low photon energy can match the characteristic spectral transition energy of the constituent atoms (such as carbon atoms).149) By selecting the suitable photon energy, R-SoXs provides highly enhanced scattering contrast of different materials because of the huge difference in their optical constants. This allows to probe the distribution of domain size and the relative domain purity in the blend films.50,51] Figure 4 shows the R-SoXs scattering profiles in log scale for PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blended films without and with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO. A photon energy of $2 8 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ $( 2 8 4 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ is employed to provide enhanced contrast between PDBT-T1 and IDIC (PTFBDT-BZS and IDIC). The characteristic mode length (domain spacing, $\xi )$ can be obtained via $\xi _ { \mathrm { m o d e } } = 2 \pi / q _ { \mathrm { m o d e } }$ and the mode domain size is the half of $\xi _ { \mathrm { m o d e } }$ . The peak of PDBT-T1:IDIC as cast film near $q = 0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { n m } ^ { - 1 }$ is generated from mass variation, which is confirmed with the scattering profile at $2 7 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The as-cast PDBT-T1:IDIC shows $1 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ domain size. After processed with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO, the scattering profile shows two peaks located near $q = 0 . 0 4$ and $0 . 1 3 \ \mathrm { n m } ^ { - 1 }$ , corresponding to domain sizes of ${ \approx } 8 0 $ and ${ \approx } 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively. The domain size of ${ \approx } 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ is obtained for the PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC as-cast film. After processed with $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO, the domain size is reduced to ${ \approx } 4 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . Combined the GIWAXS and R-SoXS analysis of PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC systems without and with DIO, we can see DIO solvent improves the crystallinity, enhances phase purity, and optimizes phase separation of blended films. For the most promising system of PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC, DIO solvent additive can significantly boost the device efficiency from $8 \%$ to $1 1 \%$ We use highly sensitive electroluminescence (EL) and photoluminescence (PL) techniques (Figure 5) to obtain complementary information on the relationship between the morphology and the $40 \%$ enhancement of the PCE. From the EL measurement, the blended films show the emission from the charge transfer (CT) state,[54] and the addition of $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO shifts the CT peak to lower energy. This redshift indicates lower CT energy in the blend processed with DIO and hence smaller $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ agreeing well with what we observe in $J { - } V$ curves in Figure 2a. In addition, DIO also enhances the emission from the pure acceptor (IDIC). This enhancement in emission of IDiC can be attributed to either increased IDIC domain size or enhanced domain purity.[55] The R-SoXS analysis in Figure 4 has shown that the domain size decreases upon the addition of DIO. Therefore, the enhanced emission of IDIC in the additive processed devices might be mainly due to enhanced domain purity of IDIC, facilitating charge transport. This observation is fully consistent with the R-SoXS results, which show that the relative domain purity of PTFBDTBZS:IDIC blended films without DIO is 0.74. The PL measurements (Figure 5b) also show that the emission contribution from the IDIC acceptor increases significantly upon the addition of $0 . 2 5 \%$ DIO, consistent with the above EL measurements which show increased peak of IDIC acceptor relative to the as-cast film. Again, this is consistent with the above R-SoXS, EL measurements, also the following transient absorption measurements.

In order to probe charge photogeneration and recombination dynamics of the most promising system PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC, we carried out transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy using the system described previously (Figure 6 and Figures S10-S15 (Supporting Information).[56,57] Figure 6a shows the spectral dynamics for the blend without DIO, along with the neat donor and acceptor. The TA spectra of PTFBDT-BZS and IDIC exhibit characteristic ground-state bleach signals $( \Delta T / T > 0 )$ resonant with their visible absorption, along with photoinduced absorption peaks $( \Delta T / T < 0 )$ in the near infrared and stimulated emission $( \Delta T / T > 0 )$ - around $7 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ in the case of PTFBDT-BZS and overlapping with the ground-state bleach around $7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ in the case of IDIC. The TA spectra of the neat materials represent excitons that decay with amplitude weighted lifetimes of $\left. \tau \right. = 6 8$ ps (PTFBDT-BZS) and 93 ps (IDIC) (Figures S13 and S15, Supporting Information) with minimal spectral shifts (Figures S12 and S14, Supporting Information).

After exciting the blend at $7 1 2 \mathrm { n m }$ , where only IDIC absorbs, IDIC exciton features are evident within the instrument response, along with additional ground-state bleaching in the bluer PTFBDT-BZS region. Energy transfer from IDIC to PTFBDT-BZS is excluded on thermodynamic grounds, as well as by the absence of PTFBDT-BZS exciton absorption around $1 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ A new photoinduced absorption feature near $1 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ $( 9 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ is seen from 200 fs in the blend. This peak can be attributed to the prompt formation of charge pairs, which are also associated with ground-state bleaching in the visible region from both the donor and acceptor components.

We can monitor the exciton-to-charge conversion process via the normalized kinetics at selected wavelengths shown in

Figure 6b. The IDIC exciton photoinduced absorption peak at $1 . 4 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ $( 8 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ initially decays dispersively with $\langle \tau \rangle = 1 . 0$ ps, leaving a residual signal from the overlapping charge peak. By comparison with the 93 ps exciton lifetime of IDIC, we conclude near unit charge transfer efficiency. Prompt and delayed charge generation components are evident from the $1 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ $( 9 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ signal, although its dynamics are also affected by the overlapping IDIC exciton peak. The charge generation profile is cleanly probed at $5 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ (2.4 eV), where the bleaching signal comes exclusively from charges occupying PTFBDT-BZS, without any contribution of excitons. Approximately half of charge pairs are formed within 200 fs, with a slower phase of charge commensurate with the 1.0 ps timescale of exciton quenching and complete by ${ \approx } 3 0$ ps. This charge generation profile is qualitatively similar to efficient polymer:fullerene blends,[56,58] but with a comparatively higher fraction of delayed charge generation in this case. The prompt component reflects interfacial (including intermixed) regions[57,58] along with delocalized excitons,[59] and the delayed component reflects exciton diffusion through pure phases - in this case, pure IDIC domains. Similar behavior is observed when initially exciting the polymer component of the PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC blend (Figure S10, Supporting Information), except that very short-lived polymer exciton signal is initially seen with $\left. \tau \right. = 7 0 0$ fs. The absence of a longer-lived polymer exciton phase may reflect the smaller polymer domains, as well as an intermediate energy transfer step to IDIC. In any case, TA using both excitation wavelengths confirms extremely efficient charge generation from both donor and acceptor channels, highlighting their excellent complementarity. Figure 6b also reveals that $\approx 3 0 \%$ of charges recombine within 1 ns. This fast timescale, along with its fluence independence (Figure S11, Supporting Information), suggests that geminate recombination via CT states can explain the lower $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of this blend compared with the blend processed using DIO. This in turn relates to the higher phase purity of the DIO processed blend, which is shown above to affect the nature of interfacial charge-transfer states.

In summary, the FREA IDIC has planar structure and narrow bandgap, which is different from the typical fullerene acceptors, and exhibits high performance in fullerene free OSCs. To investigate universality of IDIC in OSCs and to identify promising donor materials for nonfullerene OsCs, we survey five polymer donors with distinct chemical structures (thiophene, benzodithiophene, and dithienobenzodithiophene on the main chains) and different electronic properties in IDIC-based OSCs. All wide-bandgap polymers we used, such as PDCBT, J51, PTFBDT-BZS, and PDBT-T1, show better device performance than the control device based on narrow-bandgap PTB7-Th. In particular, both PDBT-T1 and PTFBDT-BZS show good morphological compatibility with IDIC acceptor, which can be further improved simply by using solvent additives; the as-cast devices based on PDBT-T1:IDIC and PTFBDT-BZS:IDIC show PCEs of $8 \% - 9 . 2 \%$ ; solvent additive DIO further enhances the PCEs to $1 0 . 3 \% { - } 1 1 \%$ . DIO improves the crystallinity and phase purity and optimizes phase separation of blended films, leading to very little bimolecular charge recombination and better charge transport. TA from both excitation wavelengths of PTFBDT-BZS and IDIC confirms extremely efficient charge generation from donor and acceptor channels.
