ABSTRACT: Developing scalable and robust processing methods with low material waste remains a challenge for organic solar cells (OSCs) to become a practical renewable energy source. Here, we present a novel low-cost processing approach termed as soft porous blade printing (SPBP), which uses a layer of soft porous material such as filter paper as the printing blade. The inherent porous microstructure of the blade offers high shear rates that facilitate the alignment, crystallization, and orientation of active materials during printing. Moreover, by eliminating the suspended liquid meniscus, SPBP relaxes the stringent requirement of gap control and enables continuous ink delivery for uninterrupted film fabrication with adjustable thickness. Higher photovoltaic performances are achieved in the SPBP-printed OSCs than those of the spin-coated counterparts for two nonfulerene-acceptor active-layer systems (Y6:PM6 and PTQ10:IDIC). Y6:PM6 cels printed by SPBP without any additive exhibit power conversion effciencies up to $1 4 . 7 5 \%$ which is among the highest reported to date for non-spin-coated OSCs.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic solar cells (OsCs) based on nonfullerene acceptors (NFAs) have attracted much attention recently because of the advantages of NFAs such as tunable energy levels, broad-band light absorption, and reduced losses in open-circuit voltage $( \breve { V } _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ 1-3 Power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of nonfullerene OSCs have reached over $1 6 \%$ for single-junction cells,- rendering the OsCs a viable photovoltaic (PV) technology. However, one of the remaining challenges for the commercialization of OsCs is the scalable and low-cost fabrication method that does not sacrifice device performances.

Mainstream scalable OSC processing techniques include doctor-blade coating and slot-die coating.7- 7-12 Blade coating involves preloading of the liquid solution (ink) on the substrate and subsequent smearing of the ink by a blade. This process is very effective for creating a large-area device on flat substrates; however, it lacks basic patterning capability, and its solution feeding is noncontinuous. Slot-die can realize simple stripe patterning and operate continuously with precise metering of the ink supply. However, a significant amount of ink $\mathrm { ' 1 - 1 0 ~ } \mathrm { m L } )$ needs to be consumed to fll the "dead volume' of the fluidic lines and the reservoir, which makes it expensive to explore a wide parameter space for many academic research labs given the high cost of OSC materials ${ \sim } \$ 1000$ per gram). Both doctor-blade and slot-die have a gap of several hundred microns between the coating tool and the substrate, and a liquid meniscus must be formed to bridge the gap. The suspended liquid meniscus requires a precise gap control, which becomes increasingly challenging for large or curved areas. In addition, the meniscus imposes a coating speed limit in the $1 { - } 5 0 \ \mathrm { m m \ } s ^ { - 1 }$ range, yielding a shear rate of $\bar { 1 0 } - 1 0 0 \ s ^ { - 1 }$ . This shear rate, although shown to assist the alignment and crystal growth of organic semiconducting polymers, is still quite low.13-16 Shearing plate with microstructures could effectively enhance conjugated polymer alignment and increase the degree of crystallinity of the donor film by $9 0 \%$ at a shear rate of $1 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { s } ^ { - 1 } }$ 14 However, the microstructures on such plates rely on sophisticated and expensive microfabrication steps such as deep reactive-ion etching (DRIE).14-16 The influence of shear rates on flm morphology is especially important for nonfullerene active layers because of the anisotropic nature and crystallization properties of NFAs.

Here, we report a novel soft porous blade printing (SPBP) approach for OSCs based on low-cost soft porous materials such as filter papers. SPBP is a robust process because it eliminates the suspended liquid meniscus as the soft blade is in direct contact with the substrate. The inherent porous microstructure in the filter paper offers both high shear rates and extensional strain rates facilitating the alignment and orientation of organic molecules, similar to the shearing plates with microstructures. Two photoactive systems, PTQ10:IDIC and PM6:Y6, are chosen because IDIC and Y6 are distinct in stereochemistry: the fused rings in IDIC are more planar, while Y6 has more twisted branches (Figure 1a).17,18 Therefore, different morphologies were formed from the SPBP process due to different responses to shearing force for the two types of NFAs. The results show that the strong shearing flow enhances the degree of crystallinity, aggregation order, and molecular packing for the polymer:NFA blend films. To further understand the charge recombination in the SPBP films, we measured the response of $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ and $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ to varying light intensities $( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ 26-28 The semilogarithmic plot of $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ a function of $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ exhibits a linear relationship, i.e., $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } \propto s \times$ $\ln ( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } ) ,$ where $s = n k T / q$ is the slope, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature, and $q$ is the elementary charge. The coefficient $n$ is close to 1, which suggests that the trap-assisted recombination is negligible in the active layers. Specifically, the slopes of the spin-coated and SPBP PTQ10:IDIC films are $1 . 3 1 ~ k T / q$ and $1 . 1 1 \ k T / q ,$ while those of the spin-coated and SPBP PM6:Y6 films are 1.50 and $1 . 4 3 \ k T / q ,$ respectively. The lower slopes of the SPBP films for both systems suggest that the SPBP films have less trapassisted recombination. The suppressed trap-assisted recombination indicates that the SPBP processing may help mitigate "pin-holes" or "island" defects in the active layers to certain extents. The combination of the more efficient charge dissociation, higher charge mobility, and weaker trap-assisted recombination should contribute to the increased FFs of the SPBP devices compared to the spin-coated references.

$J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ are fitted as $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } \propto ( \mathrm { \bar { \boldsymbol { P } } _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } } ) ^ { \alpha } \mathrm { . }$ where the exponent $\alpha$ 28 $\alpha = 1$ represents the degree of bimolecular recombination:28 means that all dissociated free carriers are collected at the corresponding electrodes, while $ { ~ \alpha ~ } < ~ 1$ indicates that bimolecular recombination may exist in the donor:acceptor blends. The SPBP films of both systems (PM6:Y6 and PTQ10:IDIC) exhibit a lower $\alpha$ than that of the spin-coated counterparts, which helps to explain that despite the obviously higher charge mobilities and lower trap-assisted recombination in SPBP devices, $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ is only slightly higher due to more bimolecular recombination in the SPBP active layers.

2.5. Additional Remarks on the Benefits and Limitations of SPBP. SPBP combines multiple benefits from several scalable thin-film coating techniques: (i) high shear rates by the inherent microscale porous feature (as in microstructured shearing plates); (ii) precision ink delivery via active flow control (as in slot dies); and (ii) high tolerance to imperfection of the substrate (as in brush paintings9-31). In addition, SPBP has a very small dead volume, hence less material waste.

We also acknowledge that the SPBP based on flter paper has a few limitations. First, the filter paper is not considered durable32 and not recommended for prolonged operation. We performed the durability evaluation tests of the SPBP by continuously printing for up to $3 \mathrm { ~ m ~ }$ using pure solvent as well as with PM6:Y6 solutions. Figure 7a shows the SEM images of the edge of the SPB of pure solvents after $0 , 1 , 2 ,$ and $3 \textrm { m }$ of coating length. After $2 \textrm { m }$ coating, the fibers become slightly loosened with the blade thickness increasing from 73 to $8 1 \mu \mathrm { m }$ After $3 \ \mathrm { m } ,$ SPB shows visible signs of delamination and the blade thickness is doubled. These data suggest that the filter paper behaves differently after a few meters of printing. Figure 7b shows the edge morphology after SPB-coating the substrate with active materials. The SPB with dried active materials does not exhibit an obvious change in morphology, perhaps it is because the polymer residue behaves as a glue that binds fibers. Figure 7c shows AFM images of the film surface for processing lengths of 0, 1, 2, and $3 \mathrm { m }$ All films exhibit low roughness from 1.1 to $1 . 4 6 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . Even after $3 ~ \mathrm { m }$ of printing length, there is no notable change in film thickness variation (Figure le). It suggests that both ink delivery (Figure S8) and film thickness are uniform within $3 \textrm { m }$ of processing, and smooth film is achievable with SPBP. In addition, the low cost and easy accessibility of the filter paper may make filter paper blades disposable and compensate the nonideal durability. Second, the fibers in the filter paper are nonuniform and randomly arranged; therefore, we can only model the flow and shear rate based on the characteristic lengths that may deviate from real scenarios. Nevertheless, the SPBP based on filter paper is a meaningful step toward scalable manufacturing of solar cells because it provides a balanced trade-off among strong average shear rates, simplicity, scalability, and cost. Starting from results reported in this work, researchers can develop more advanced soft porous blades to address the durability and irregularity of the microstructures, making SPBP a viable technique for industrial scale production of OSCs.

# 3. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we introduce a novel approach for printing OSCs using soft porous blades made out of flter papers. The inherently microstructured printing blades produce strong shearing flow that enhances the crystallinity of both donor and nonfullerene acceptors. The film characterizations show that the active layers printed by the soft porous blade exhibit a higher degree of crystallinity, more ordered aggregation, and closer molecular packing than the spin-coated counterparts. Different NFAs such as IDIC and Y6 respond to shearing differently due to their distinct stereochemistry. SPBP produced OSCs with better performance for two NFA systems (PM6:Y6 and PTQ10:IDIC) than that of the reference devices. The best PM6:Y6 cell printed by the soft porous blade without any additive achieves a PCE of $1 4 . 7 5 \%$ , which is among the highest to date for non-spin-coated OSCs. The results show that SPBP is a promising technique for printing organic semiconductor thin flms due to the low cost, scalability, speed, and compatibility with nonplanar substrates.
