# Improving Process Compatibility of Multilayer Organic Fiber Photodetectors by Combining Coaxial Thermal Evaporation with Dip-Coating

Jiaao Wu, Yi Li, Xueyuan Wu, Xiaoran Zhang, Qingxia Liu, Shuai Qu, Liu Yuan, and Yang Wang\*

Fiber photodetectors (FPDs) are promising candidates for wearable applications due to their flexibility, knittability, and full-angle detection. The current state-of-the-art FPDs based on inorganic photosensitive materials show drawbacks in mechanical flexibility. The organic FPDs (OFPDs) are superior, while the fabrication is limited by multi-layer process compatibility issues. Herein, a diode-type five-layer OFPD with Zn/ZnO/PBDB-T:ITIC-Th, $M \circ \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ /PEDOT:PSS structure is prepared by combining thermal evaporation with a successive dip-coating method. It is worth mentioning that the uniform coaxial $\mathbf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ interfacial layer is processed by the proposed fiber-compatible thermal evaporation technique, while the other functional layers are enabled by the solution-based dip-coating method. At the bias of $- 0 . 5 \mathtt { V } ,$ the optimized OFPD exhibits a dark current density of $\mathbf { 8 . 4 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 8 } A c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , a responsivity of $8 6 . 4 \ : \mathrm { m A } \ : \forall ^ { - 1 }$ - $( @ 7 6 0 \mathsf { n m } )$ , and a specific detectivity of over $\mathbf { \widetilde { l 0 } } ^ { 1 1 }$ Jones in the visible range of $4 0 0 { \ - } 7 6 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ -. Besides, the device displays a fast response speed of 0.44 and 0.14 ms for rise and fall time, showing the capability of full-angle detection. The device is demonstrated in photoplethysmography for real-time heart-rate monitoring, suggesting its potential for practical application in wearable electronics.

# 1. Introduction

Flexible photodetectors (PDs) that can convert optical signals into electrical signals are an important part of wearable devices and provide opportunities for the realization of intelligent monitoring, 24/7 healthcare, and environmental exploration.[1-4] In terms of geometrical form, wearable PDs can be divided into two-dimensional (2D) planar PDs and one-dimensional (1D) fiber PDs.[1] In particular, 1D photodetectors prepared on fiber substrates have flourished due to their portability, knittability, and mechanical strength.15-91 They are also better suited for future wearable applications in terms of integration with everyday textiles. The fiber may be further processed into 1D yarns, 2D fabrics, and three-dimensional (3D) spacer fabrics via textile technology, and its hierarchical nature makes it ideally suited for mechanical design in wearable devices.[5,10] In addition, compared to planar PDs,[3,11] fiber photodetectors (FPDs) have the ability to uniformly detect $3 6 0 ^ { \circ }$ full-angle incident light perpendicular to the axis and offer superior comfort and air permeability, all of which make them better used in wearable smart fabrics.[8]

However, for devices with such special geometric structures, the preparation process of FPDs remains challenging.[12,13]

Therefore, various attempts on preparation have been made to further advance wearable electronics. The reported FPDs are mostly based on photodiode type and can be divided into three categories according to the photosensitive material: inorganic heterojunctions, inorganic/organic composite heterojunctions, and organic heterojunctions.5l For inorganic heterojunctions, several ingenious structures have been reported, confirming the feasibility of inorganic FPDs.14-16] Although the related technology of inorganic 2D planar PDs is becoming mature and reliable, the application of pure inorganic heterojunctions in wearable devices is still limited to a large extent. This is due to their complicated preparation process, limited mechanical flexibility, and incompatibility of certain photosensitive materials with fibrous substrates.!ll For heterojunctions of inorganic or inorganic/organic composite materials, the inorganic component typically consists of clusters of nanowires, nanotubes, or nanorods oriented perpendicular to the fiber surface to obtain better mechanical flexibility.[14-21] Such configuration can provide FPDs with a high surface-to-volume ratio, a clearly defined charge transfer path, and strong adhesion to the metal substrate.

In inorganic/organic composite devices, the unconstrained organic semiconductor then completely covers the inorganic functional layer and forms a "soft" contact to smooth its rough surface, which provides a margin for deformation, allowing the device to operate stably even in different bending states.[17] For organic heterojunction FPDs, Kim et al.[22] utilized the structure of Au/PTB7-Thz $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } _ { I }$ carbon nanotube and the prepared organic fiber photodetector (OFPD) exhibited an $I _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } / I _ { \mathrm { d a r k } }$ ratio approaching $7 \times 1 0 ^ { 3 }$ under $- 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ bias and $5 0 \mathrm { \ m w \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ incident light. According to the reports in the past decade, the performances of FPDs have been gradually improved with the proposal of various preparation and optimization schemes.[23]

However, compared with the latest and even decades-ago 2D planar PDs, 1D FPDs still suffer from lower performance, such as insufficient photoresponsivity and slow response speed.[12,13,24] There are many possible reasons. Many film preparation processes suitable for planar substrates are no longer suitable for curved substrates,[25] thus the existing film-forming methods introduce many defects due to the unique fiber structure or the instable processes.[18] Besides, it is difficult to apply high-performance multilayer device structures on fiber substrates.26-291 For example, in organic photodetectors, the appropriate electron/hole transport layers are usually introduced between the photosensitive layer and electrodes[30-32] to improve carrier transport properties and reduce dark current. While in the currently reported FPDs, the two functional layers are almost not included. Because if multiple functional layers are continuously prepared on fiber substrates, factors such as surface wettability, solvent orthogonality and work function matching of each material layer must be considered. Therefore, to a large extent, the functional layer number and thus the performance of FPDs based on inorganic/organic composite or organic photosensitive materials are limited by multi-layer compatibility issues, hindering the application of FPDs in the wearable electronic field.

Herein, we present a five-layer coaxial OFPD with the structure of $Z \mathrm { n } / Z \mathrm { n O } /$ PBDB-T:ITIC-Th, $\scriptstyle { { \mathrm { ~ \# 0 0 } } _ { 3 } }$ /PEDOT:PSS. The ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ electron transport layer and photosensitive layer are prepared by orthogonal solvent strategy, and the $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ hole transport layer is prepared through our proposed fiber-compatible thermal evaporation technique. The optimized device with an $8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ -thick $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ layer exhibits excellent rectification characteristics and shows a specific detectivity $( D ^ { * } )$ of over $1 0 ^ { 1 1 }$ Jones in the range of $4 0 0 { - } 7 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ The fiber-compatible thermal evaporation approach settles the issue of process compatibility in preparing multilayer FPDs and can be extended to other fiber photoelectric devices.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is often used to analyze the film morphology and roughness. It is challenging to obtain the properties of fiber-based films because the scanning area of probes is limited by the curved surface and only a small area $( 1 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } \times 1 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ can be tested (Figure S5, Supporting Information). AFM images measured under this limitation show that the surface root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the $\mathrm { Z n O }$ layer is $0 . 8 4 \mathrm { n m }$ , which means films prepared by the dip-coating process are relatively smooth. The lower roughness is conducive to the transport of electrons and the deposition of the organic photosensitive layer. The 3D topography of ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ is arched. Calculation of the 3D topography of the ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ arch shows that the curvature exhibited by the AFM image is consistent with that of the zinc wire. Besides, it can be noticed that the 3D topography of ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ has some areas of depressions, which can be attributed to the defects on the surface of the zinc wire substrate. The RMS roughness of the PBDB-T:ITIC-Th organic photosensitive layer is $1 . 4 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , which is slightly increased compared with that of the zinc oxide layer. After depositing a certain amount of $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ the RMS roughness is $2 . 5 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure 2d,e), which suggests that high-quality $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ films can effectively reduce the dark current as a hole transport layer and provide a smooth interface for the subsequent coating of the outer transparent electrode. According to the surface morphology of the PEDOT:PSS thin film, its surface RMS roughness is $2 . 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , suggesting a good film quality. The outer electrode was evenly wrapped on the outer layer to realize the collection and transmission of carriers.

During the process of evaporating $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ , the film thickness was measured by a plane crystal oscillator probe, which repre. sented the plane thickness rather than the real film thickness deposited on the fiber surface. It can be expected that the film thickness was related to factors such as rotation rate, evaporation rate, and surface state of the substrate. Here, in order to facilitate quantification and description, the thickness detected by the planar crystal oscillator is regarded as the relative thickness of the cylindrical coaxial film obtained by evaporation. Maintaining a constant rotation rate and a stable evaporation rate, different thicknesses of $\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ were deposited, and the $J { - } V$ characteristics of devices were tested (see the Experimental Section for more details). $$

$$

where $J _ { \mathrm { { p h } } }$ is the photocurrent density $( \mathrm { A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and $L _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ is the incident light power density $( \mathbb { W } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } .$ . The $R$ of the device under $- 0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ bias is shown in Figure 3c. Besides, the $R$ at other bias voltages is shown in Figure S8a (Supporting Information). It was observed that the device had an excellent response in the visible light band, which was consistent with the absorption characteristics of adoptive organic photosensitive materials. $$
E Q E = \frac { J _ { \mathrm { p h } } } { L _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } } \times \frac { h c } { q \lambda } = R \times \frac { h c } { q \lambda }
$$

where $h$ is Planck's constant $( 6 . 6 2 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 4 } \mathrm { ~ J }$ s), $c$ is the light speed in vacuum $( 3 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { ~ m ~ s ^ { - 1 } }$ $q$ is the quantity of elementary charge $( 1 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 9 } \ \mathrm { C } )$ , and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the incident light. The response time refers to how quickly the photodetector reacts to incident light. When exposed to a pulsed light signal, the time it takes for the device to increase from $10 \%$ to $90 \%$ of the stable output value is known as the rise time, while the time it takes for the device to decrease from $90 \%$ to $10 \%$ is called the fall time.[291 As shown in Figure 3f, the response time of the device was tested at $_ { 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ bias using a $6 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ laser pulse. The rise time was found to be $0 . 4 4 ~ \mathrm { m s }$ while the fall time was $0 . 1 4 ~ \mathrm { m s }$ . This time response performance is quite fast in the reported devices, as shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

The OFPD with coaxial design exhibited complete photoresponse characteristics over a wide range of angles. To evaluate its sensitivity to light at different angles of incidence, the device was mounted vertically and a $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ laser light source was rotated $3 6 0 ^ { \circ }$ around it at a constant speed. Figure 4a demonstrates that the ratio between the minimum and maximum response points was over $80 \%$ , indicating the device had excellent uniformity with no noticeable defects, which made it suitable for detecting light from all angles.

In the flexibility test of electronic devices, there are two main types: one is to measure the change in photoresponse of the device at different bending angles, and the other is to measure the change in photoresponse after repeated bending at the same angle.22] As shown in Figure 4b, the length of the fiber device is $5 0 \mathrm { m m }$ . The center of the circle is used as a connecting line, and half of the angle at the center of the circle is also a bending angle. When bending at $4 5 ^ { \circ }$ , there is a displacement of $2 . 4 9 \mathrm { m m }$ left and right in the horizontal direction, so in the flexibility test device, two sliders need to be set to move $2 . 4 9 \ \mathrm { m m }$ to the center at the same time. Taking the bending at $4 5 ^ { \circ }$ as an example, the photodark current changed after multiple bending during the test period, as shown in Figure 4c. The schematic diagram of the flexure test is shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information). The photodark current was tested every 50 times of bending, and the total was 1000 times. It is found that during the 1000 times of bending, the photocurrent did not drop obviously (maintaining $86 \%$ of the initial value). The dark current increased slightly during the repeated bending process, and after 1o00 times, it increased by $8 \%$ compared with the initial value. Under the above definition, the light and dark current changes of the device after bending from $0 ^ { \circ }$ to $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ were performed every $1 5 ^ { \circ }$ , as shown in Figure 4d. Under different bending angles, the photocurrent of the device decreased slightly, as the photocurrent at $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ was $8 2 \%$ of the initial value $( 0 ^ { \circ } )$ , but remained stable on the whole. Since the flexible test platform had no electromagnetic shielding device, the dark current of the device was slightly higher. It should be noted that more bending times and larger bending angles have the potential to cause breakage and damage to devices, which leads to the deterioration of device performance. The attenuation in photocurrent is attributed to the decrease in carrier transport capacity caused by structural damage of transmission layer films after bending. The increase in dark current is due to defects such as pinholes in the bent film, which will cause the generation of alternative conductive paths and increase the shunt current.[44]

An important application of OFPDs is real-time health monitoring. Based on the photoplethysmography (PPG) method, the pulse signal of an adult male was measured under a $6 5 0 \mathrm { - n m }$ laser,[22] and the test system is shown in Figure 4e. The light emitted by the laser is partially absorbed and scattered in the body's tissues, and during contraction and relaxation, the optical density and path length of the illuminated tissue change (due to the variation in the orientation and concentration of erythrocytes and the light-absorbing hemoglobin they carry). Then the transmitted light is measured by OFPDs, and the signal waveform can reflect the state of venous contraction and relaxation. The heart rate can be determined as 70 beats $\mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ (Figure 4f). In addition, compared to planar devices, OFPDs can be flexibly wrapped around the human body to detect signals in specific dimensions. Because of its full-angle photosensitive characteristics, when body movement causes sensor misalignment, the change in signal magnitude caused by angle changes is smaller. This can simplify subsequent signal processing, demonstrating the potential of OFPDs in wearable electronics.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, a five-layer coaxial organic fiber photodetector was developed by thermal evaporation and dip-coating method. The $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ layer was prepared by a self-designed fiber-compatible thermal evaporation method, which ensured the stability and non-interference of the photosensitive layer and the outer transparent electrode. Based on zinc wire, the OFPD device was sensitive to visible light due to the PBDB-T:ITIC-Th active layer and its detection band could be changed with the organic semiconductors adopted, which reflected the advantages of organic heterojunctions. The extra two transport layers or barrier layers made the dark current density significantly lower than that of the previously reported devices, which fully demonstrated the superiority of multilayer FPDs. The photoresponse was also improved, reaching a high level in FPDs. The thinner device thickness made the time response much faster than that of composite heterojunction devices. Besides, excellent all-angle detection capability and bending resistance were also demonstrated. The application to heart rate measurement proved the practical value of this OFPD. These results can provide new insights into the design of fiber electronic devices, thus enabling FPDs to play a promising role as commercially available photodetection devices in the future market for wearable electronics applications.

# 4. Experimental Section

Solution Synthesis:  ZnO precursor solution: A $0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { m o l } \ : \mathsf { L } ^ { - 1 }$ zinc acetate dihydrate (from Aladdin) solution was prepared with dimethoxyethanol (from TCl) as a solvent. Ethanolamine (from Sinopharm) was added as a stabilizer in a ratio of 1:1 to the zinc source, and then 0.5 vol. $\%$ FS-31 (from DuPont) was added to increase the wettability of the precursor solution. The ${ \mathsf { M o O } } _ { 3 }$ layer was prepared by thermal evaporation deposition, and a special fiber device rotation device was designed to make the fiber rotate around the axis at a uniform speed so that the film could be deposited evenly.
