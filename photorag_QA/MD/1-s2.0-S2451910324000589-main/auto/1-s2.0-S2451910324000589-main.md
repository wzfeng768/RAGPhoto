# Review Article

# Electrical measurements on single semiconducting polymer wires

Daniel Felipe Duarte Sánchez, Ab F. Nieuwenhuis and Serge G. Lemay

# Abstract

Semiconducting polymers are a fascinating class of materials, both fundamentally and because of their broad applicability in electronics, photovoltaics, display technology and bioelectronics. Relatively few attempts have been made to explore the properties of individual polymer chains, however, and those studies have led to a broad range of observations that can be difficult to reconcile. Here we discuss these measurements with attention to the differences in experimental configurations that may underlie the breadth of experimental reports.

The earliest efforts were motivated by the broad goal of realizing molecular electronic devices and were performed in vacuum. As OFETs and OECTs became more widely employed, however, the focus was extended to electrochemically controlled environments.

Contacting a single polymer chain is far from trivial, for several reasons. First, most SPs are flexible and tend to adopt a disordered, compact or random-coil configuration in solution. The likelihood that a molecule assembles in an extended configuration spanning the electrodes e the ideal scenario e is therefore limited. This reality is often ignored in discussions of singlemolecule measurements. Second, means must exist to stabilize the electrical contact between the polymer and the electrodes, since polymers can rearrange their configuration on the sub-microsecond time scale. Third, it is often difficult or impossible to determine the polymer configuration during measurements, which leads to indirect interpretations of experimental results.

# Introduction

Semiconducting polymers (SPs) are electronic materials suitable for sustainable [1,2], cheap, wearable and biocompatible [3,4] electronics from which several technologies have been developed. These include organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) and electrochemical transistors (OECTs) for low concentration (bio)chemical sensing [5e7], where the main challenge at present is the selectivity-sensitivity trade-off [8,9]; organic solar cells with photoactive layers comprised entirely by SPs and exhibiting energy conversion

As summarized in Figure 1, two main strategies have been explored: scanning probes and nanogap electrodes. The former relies on the ability to control the position of a conductive tip with sub-A˚ resolution, while the latter employs fixed electrodes that are sufficiently close together that the SP can span the space between the two electrodes. Several methods exist for creating pairs of electrodes with spacings down to the few-nm level, and this aspect of the experiments can be considered quite mature. Because of the variety of methods, conditions, and systems investigated, however, a wide variety of observations and interpretations has been reported. Here we briefly review the different approaches and contrast the different findings.

# Observations in vacuum

A powerful technique to probe SPs is scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), in which a sharp, electrically conducting tip can be used to image, manipulate and perform electrical measurements on molecules resting on a conducting surface. It was shown that the end of a SP can be attached to a STM tip and lifted gradually away from the surface, thus controlling the length of SP connecting the tip to the surface (Figure 1a). With the tip and surface acting as electrodes, it is possible to measure the two-electrode current-voltage characteristics of a single polymer as a function of contour length. This approach has focused on SPs that are prepared in situ under ultrahigh vacuum conditions, while the electrical measurements were performed at cryogenic temperatures to ensure mechanical stability of the tip. A significant benefit of these measurements is that the polymer is known to be in an extended configuration during the measurement, which is explicitly not the case with molecules assembled onto fixed electrodes from solution.

In a tour de force experiment, Lafferentz [20] et al. reported electrical measurements on short $( \leq 3 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ ) polyfluorene wires. They found that the conductivity of the junction, $G$ , decays exponentially with polymer length $L$ , that is, $G ( L ) { \bf \bar { \Psi } } = { \bf \bar { \Psi } } { \cal G } ( 0 ) e ^ { - \beta L }$ . This is characteristic of direct, nonresonant tunneling, in which electrons tunnel from one electrode to the other without residing on the SP. While the presence of the SP assists the tunneling process (and the inverse the decay length $\beta$ depends on its properties such as the HOMO and LUMO energies), any charge carriers residing on the SP do not actively participate in conduction. The resulting conductance values ranged from $\sim 1 0 ^ { - 9 } \ : S$ to ${ \sim } 1 0 ^ { - 1 4 } \mathrm { \ s }$ for lengths ranging from $1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ to $3 \mathrm { n m }$ . Comparable results were obtained for other SPs, e.g., polythiophene [21]. Off-resonance tunneling is a short-range process, however. Employing conducting-tip atomic force microscopy (AFM), Choi et al. instead reported a crossover to a weaker length dependence in single-oligophenyleneimine wires with length ${ > } 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which they attributed to a transition to carrier hopping as dominant transport mechanism [22]. A conductance of ${ \sim } 1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } S$ was reported for $7 \mathrm { n m }$ polymers, which is far higher than would be expected for nonresonant tunneling. As a different mechanism for long-range transport, resonant tunneling has also been observed at high bias potentials, for example leading to conductance values of ${ \dot { \sim } } 1 0 ^ { - 8 } \mathrm { S }$ for biphenylporphyrin oligomers with length ${ > } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ [23].

In a distinct yet related approach, illustrated in Figure 1b, Hu et al. trapped poly(para-phenylene ethynylene) between electrodes spaced by $\sim 1 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ using thiol chemistry [24]. After assembly in solution, the device was transferred to a vacuum environment. Here also high conductance (order $1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } \ \mathrm { S }$ ) and resonant structures in the $I \mathrm { ~ - ~ } V$ characteristics were reported at very high bias (up to $6 \ : \mathrm { V } ,$ ), which were also attributed to resonant tunneling through the molecule.

In the two-electrode configuration employed in these experiments, no means were available to intentionally dope the SPs, either chemically or electrostatically [31]. This is a result of the synthesis process and the difficulty of positioning a gate sufficiently close to the SP for effective gating, respectively. Transport via mechanisms other than nonresonant tunneling therefore requires high bias to access the HOMO or LUMO voltages and inject carriers into the SP. While ideal for studying contact effects such as Fermi-level pinning [32,33], this renders comparison with experiments in solution, where an active gate is employed, rather difficult.

# Observations under electrochemical control

For measurements taking place in a liquid electrolyte, the situation is very different as a result of coupled ionic-electronic charge transport [34,35]. Here the potential of the solution can be set via a reference electrode, providing effective external control of the doping level of SPs even in a nanoscale gap. This is the gating mechanism employed in both OFETs, in which an electrical double layer induces charge carriers at the surface of the semiconductor, and in OECTs, where counterions penetrate into a SP thin film to compensate for the charge of the induced carriers [36]. The same principle is expected to hold for a single polymer, where the charge of the electrical double layer surrounding the SP can compensate the charge of the carriers residing on the SP.

He et al. introduced electropolymerization as a means of forming conducting polymer junctions between nanogap electrodes [37]. Here polyaniline (PANI) was deposited from solution onto the electrodes by controlling the potential of the solution containing the precursor molecule (aniline in this case). The deposition process was monitored electrically and interrupted once a conducting junction was formed (Figure 1c). The resulting devices, which initially consisted of multiple SP chains, behaved similarly to the bulk material for junctions $> 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Upon scaling down to spacings of $\sim 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , on the other hand, transport became characterized by abrupt switching between insulating and highly conducting states. This hysteretic switching was strongly dependent on the potential of the gate, and telegraphlike oscillations in time between the two states were observed in a narrow range of gate potentials near the switching transition. Further refinements of this approach using both PANI and polypyrrole (PPy) revealed multi-level telegraph-like oscillations, as illustrated in Figure 2a. These were observed in gap sizes ranging from $\sim 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ to $\sim 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , with corresponding conductance values of order ${ { 1 0 } ^ { - 6 } }$ S and $\dot { 1 } 0 ^ { - 8 }$ S, respectively [25]. Switching was attributed to the sudden oxidation or reduction of an entire SP chain, with resonant tunneling becoming possible in the oxidized state, as sketched in Figure 3a. When multiple levels were observed, these were ascribed to multiple SP chains spanning the electrodes and being independently reduced or oxidized.

# Discussion and outlook

Over the last two decades, there has been a number of reports on the properties of individual SP chains. The most sophisticated in terms of technique employed scanning probes, where off-resonance, resonant and hopping transport were reported for different biasing regimes and SP lengths. Unfortunately, however, these experiments take place in vacuum where it is not possible to exert electrochemical control on the redox state of the SP, making it difficult to directly connect these results to the properties of, e.g., OFETs and OECTs, where strong ionic gating is usually employed.

Measurements under electrochemical control are instead complicated by uncertainties in the exact configuration of the polymer molecules between the electrodes, whether the SPs were electropolymerized at the electrode itself or assembled from solution. This difficulty is exacerbated by the small electrode spacings employed, which range from 1 to 10s of nanometers, and the often irregular electrode geometries. Electropolymerized molecules at nm-scale gap electrodes have yielded conductance values consistent with those obtained on single chains in vacuum via scanning probe methods and with the off-resonance tunneling mechanism. For junctions over many nanometers, where carrier hopping is expected to be the dominant transport mechanism, conductance values differing by up to two orders of magnitude have been reported. It is unclear at this point to what extent these differences originate from differences in materials, solvents, choice of supporting salt, deposition methods, and the presence or absence of a solid substrate between the electrodes. Despite measurements spanning two decades, it is clear that more systematic studies will be required to elucidate the origin of these differences.

An important conceptual question is the origin of the telegraph-like fluctuations in current that are present in essentially all reported measurements with devices under electrochemical control. These can be remarkably similar in very different systems, as was illustrated in Figure 2. The fluctuations were initially attributed to abrupt fluctuations in the redox state of the SP, which was consistent with the observation that the fluctuations occurred primarily in a narrow range of potentials. The occurrence of similar fluctuations for larger gaps however led Lacroix and co-workers to suggest conformational rearrangements as a more likely cause in their experiments. Brunner et al. also suggested this mechanism for single alkanedithiols in mesitylene [40]. This is consistent with our interpretation for loosely adsorbed SPs on a surface, which can easily rearrange their configuration between the electrodes (Figure 3b). The anomalous scaling of the $1 / f$ noise with current [30] is a strong indication that the fluctuations do not have a conventional electronic origin, further supporting the interpretation that a different, conformation-based mechanism is at work. These fluctuations were not considered in the earlier works on nanogap electrodes, but they cannot be excluded. Thus, while it is possible that the two mechanisms co-exist depending on length scale, we conclude that conformational fluctuations are an intrinsic feature of transport through SPs at the single-chain level.
