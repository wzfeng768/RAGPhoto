# Reducing Trap Density in Organic Solar Cells via Extending the Fused Ring Donor Unit of an A-D-A-Type Nonfullerene Acceptor for Over 17% Efficiency

Jixiang Zhou, Dan He, Yawen Li, Fei Huang, Jianqi Zhang, Cheng Zhang, Yongbo Yuan, Yuze Lin,\* Chunru Wang, and Fuwen Zhao\*

The high trap density (generally $\overline { { 1 0 } } ^ { 1 6 }$ to $1 0 ^ { 1 8 } ( m ^ { - 3 } )$ in thin films of organic semiconductors is the primary reason for the inferior charge-carrier mobility and large nonradiative recombination energy loss $( \Delta E _ { \mathrm { n r } } )$ in organic solar cells (OSCs), limiting improvement in power conversion efficiencies (PCEs). In this study, the trap density in OsCs is efficiently reduced via extending the donor core of nonfullerene acceptors (NFAs) from a heptacyclic unit to a nonacyclic unit. TTPIC-4F with a nonacyclic unit has stronger intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, affording higher crystallinity in thin films relative to its counterpart BTPIC-4F. Thus, the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device achieves a lower trap density of $4 . 0 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ , comparable to some typical high-performance inorganic/hybrid semiconductors, with higher mobility and inhibited chargecarrier recombination in devices. Therefore, the D18:TTPIC-4F-based OSC exhibits an impressive PCE of $1 7 . 1 \%$ with a low $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { n r } }$ of $\pmb { 0 . 2 0 8 } \mathtt { e V } ,$ which is the best known value for A-D-A-type NFAs. Therefore, extending the donor core of NFAs is an efficient method for suppressing trap states in OSCs for high PCEs.

# 1. Introduction

Organic solar cells (OsCs), deemed as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation solar cells, have attracted widespread attention from the scientific and business community over the past decades because of their light weight, mechanical flexibility, semi-transparency, and ease of scale-up fabrication.1-3] The core part of the OSC is the active layer, which generally consists of a blend of p-type (donors) and n-type organic semiconductors (acceptors), and the blend film forms a bicontinuous interpenetrating network to facilitate exciton dissociation and charge-carrier transport.[4,5] With the emergence of the landmark A-D-A-type nonfullerene acceptor (NFA), ITIC,[6 power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of OsCs have achieved great progress in recent years.7-9] However, the PCEs of OSCs still lag behind those of inorganic/hybrid solar cells, such as crystalline silicon, gallium arsenide, and hybrid perovskite.10] This gap is attributable to the relatively large energy loss for the repressed open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ ,[11] and inferior charge-carrier mobility for the confined shortcircuit current density $( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } )$ and fill factor (FF).[12] The predominant reason for the large energy loss, particularly nonradiative recombination energy loss $( \Delta E _ { \mathrm { n r } } )$ and inferior charge-carrier mobility, is the high trap density (generally $1 0 ^ { 1 6 }$ to $1 0 ^ { 1 8 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 } )$ of organic semiconductors in OsCs for the localization of charge carriers.[13,14] Therefore, reducing the trap density in the active layer is important for boosting the PCEs of OSCs.

The trap states in solar cells originate from various sources, which can be divided into extrinsic and intrinsic. For OsCs, the main source of trap states is disorder (intrinsic traps), which includes dynamic and static disorders.13] In organic semiconductors, intermolecular interactions in the condensed state are weak, mainly depending on van der Waals forces. The thermal motion of molecules in the active layer causes inevitable dynamic disorder.[15] Moreover, structural defects and chemical impurities in OsCs, such as molecular chain twist,[16] grain boundaries,[17] packing dislocations,[18] and dopants,[19] owing to the inferior crystallinity of organic semiconductors, the introduction of guest molecules, and the fast film-formation process during device fabrication, would lead to intense static disorder. Therefore, reinforcing the intermolecular interactions and improving the crystallinity of organic semiconductors in the active layer would reduce disorder and suppress trap states to realize low energy loss and high charge-carrier mobility in OSCs. However, very few reports have focused on regulating disorder to reduce the trap density of organic semiconductors in OSCs,[20,21] 1et alone how to suppress trap states via material design.

Herein, we successfully reduced the trap density in OsCs by extending the $\pi$ conjugation of the donor core in NFAs from heptacyclic unit to nonacyclic unit (Figure 1a). Two A-D-A-type NFAs, viz., BTPIC-4F with heptacyclic unit and TTPIC-4F with nonacyclic unit, were prepared (Figure 1b). Compared with BTPIC-4F, TTPIC-4F exhibits smaller intramolecular dihedral angles and larger intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ overlap, contributing to the stronger electronic coupling and higher crystallinity in the neat and blend films. Thus, the D18:T'TPIC-4F blend film affords a finer morphology and a reduced trap density of $4 . 0 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ in devices, which facilitates chargecarrier transport for higher mobility and inhibits charge-carrier recombination for lower $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { n r } }$ Therefore, the D18:TTPIC-4Fbased OSC achieves an excellent PCE of $1 7 . 1 \%$ , which is the highest value for A-D-A-type NFAs. Moreover, the trap density of $4 . 0 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ for the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device is among the lowest values for organic semiconductor based devices,[14,20-22] even comparable to some of those based on typical high-performance inorganic/hybrid semiconductors,[23,24] indicating that it remains much room for NFAs, particularly A-D-A-type ones, to further enhance the PCEs of OSCs.[24]

# 2. Results and Discussion

The synthetic routes to BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F are shown in Schemes S1 and s2 (Supporting Information), and the corresponding details of the syntheses are presented in the Supporting Information. The chemical structures of the intermediates and NFAs are confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information) and mass spectrometry, respectively. The energy levels of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F were measured by cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of TTPIC-4F were determined to be $- 5 . 3 0$ and $- 3 . 8 9 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively, and both slightly shallower than those of BTPIC-4F $( - 5 . 3 5$ and $- 3 . 9 1 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ for HOMO and LUMO energy levels, respectively), owing to the stronger electron-donating capacity of the nonacyclic unit. Thus, the electrochemical bandgap of TTPIC-4F is $1 . 4 1 \mathrm { e V }$ and close to that of BTPIC-4F (1.44 eV). The solution and thin-film absorption spectra of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F were recorded using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. TTPIC-4F has a similar absorbance to BTPIC-4F, with a maximum peak at ${ \approx } 7 4 5 ~ \ \mathrm { n m }$ in chloroform but higher extinction coefficients from 500 to $7 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The absorption spectra of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F showed a remarkable redshift in the thin-film state (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The maximum absorption peak of the TTPIC-4F film was detected at $7 8 9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which was slightly larger than that of BTPIC-4F $( 7 8 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , while the absorption onset of TTPIC-4F was observed at $8 7 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which was slightly smaller than that of BTPIC-4F $( 8 7 6 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ (Table S1, Supporting Information). Thus, TTPIC-4F has a steeper absorption onset than BTPIC-4F, which implies a lower Urbach energy for the TTPIC-4F film.25,26] BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F have similar optical bandgaps (1.41-1.42 eV), which is consistent with the CV results obtained above. Furthermore, we chose the high-performance wide-bandgap polymer D18 with complementary absorption (Figure S5, Supporting Information) and matched energy levels (Figure S3, Supporting Information) to blend with BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F, respectively. Compared with D18:BTPIC-4F blend film, D18:T'TPIC-4F blend film exhibits stronger absorbance from 300 to $8 7 0 \mathrm { n m }$ with the maximum extinction coefficient of $6 . 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 } \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ at $7 9 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ (Figure S6, Supporting Information), which would benefit sunlight harvest. In addition, the absorption onset of D18:TTPIC-4F blend film is also steeper than that of D18:BTPIC-4F blend film, which is in line with the neat-film result.

To obtain more structural and intermolecular interaction information for BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F, single crystals were prepared via the diffusion method and analyzed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The structures of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F obtained from single-crystal XRD are shown in Figure 2a,b, and the crystallographic data are presented in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The dihedral angle between the donor unit and the end groups of TTPIC-4F is 5.4, which is significantly smaller than that of BTPIC-4F $( 2 1 . 5 ^ { \circ } )$ . Thus, the distance between O atom at end groups and S atom at the periphery of donor unit increases from $2 . { \overset { \cdot } { 6 } } 9 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for TTPIC-4F to $\bar { 2 . 7 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } }$ for BTPIC-4F. The shorter $\mathrm { S } \cdots \mathrm { O }$ distance for TTPIC4F can afford stronger intramolecular noncovalent interactions to sustain the planarity of the molecular backbone and inhibit the thermal motion of molecules to reduce the reorganization energy.[27,28] As shown in packing model (Figure 2c,d), BTP1C-4F and TTPIC-4F exhibit lamellar stacking with the $d$ -spacing of 8.64 and $7 . 6 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ , respectively, and $\pi - \pi$ stacking distances of 3.19 and $3 . 2 5 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ in each layer, respectively. It should be noted that TTPIC-4F possesses two rows of molecules in each layer with a larger intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking overlap between the end group and donor unit, whereas BTPIC-4F has one row of molecules in each layer with weak $\pi - \pi$ stacking, depending only on the slip-stacking of cyano at the end groups. The larger intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking overlap for TTPIC-4F resulted in stronger intermolecular interactions, facilitating molecular packing and improving crystallinity for lower static disorder.[29] Furthermore, the transfer integral of neighboring molecule pairs in the crystals was obtained by first-principles quantum-mechanical calculations. The maximum electronic coupling for electrons of the TTPIC-4F crystal is $1 9 . 1 4 \ \mathrm { m e V } ,$ which is almost one order higher than that of the BTPIC-4F crystal $( 2 . 4 6 ~ \mathrm { m e V } )$ (Figure S7, Supporting Information). This is ascribed to the larger intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ stacking overlap, facilitating charge-carrier transport.30,31] Therefore, extending the donor unit of TTPIC4F endows it with stronger intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, which would enhance crystallinity in thin films.

To explore the degree of molecular ordering and phase behavior in thin-film states, the morphologies of the neat and blend films of the NFAs were investigated. As shown in the 2D grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) patterns (Figure 3a,c), both BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F prefer to face-on orientation with $\pi - \pi$ stacking diffraction peaks at ${ \approx } 1 . 7 5 \ \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ relative to the substrate, corresponding to a $d$ spacing of ${ \approx } 3 . 5 9 $ A. However, the crystalline coherent length (CCL) for $\pi - \pi$ stacking in TTPIC-4F neat film is $1 6 . 6 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ which is significantly larger than that in BTPIC-4F neat film (8.4 A). In addition, TTPIC-4F exhibited (100) and (200) stacking diffraction peaks at 0.29 and $0 . 5 8 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the in-plane direction, respectively, whereas BTPIC-4F exhibited only a weak (100) diffraction peak at $0 . 3 3 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ The morphologies of D18:BTPIC-4F and D18:TTPIC-4F blend films were further determined to investigate the phase behavior. Similarly, both D18:BTPIC-4F and D18:TTPIC-4F blend films sustained a face-on orientation relative to the substrate, whereas the $\pi - \pi$ stacking diffraction peaks shifted to ${ \approx } 1 . 6 8 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ (Figure 3b,d) because of their merging with the $\pi - \pi$ stacking diffraction peak of D18 (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The CCL for $\pi - \pi$ stacking of D18:TTPIC-4F blend film in out-of-plane direction is $2 1 . 8 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ larger than that of D18:BTPIC-4F blend film $( 2 0 . 0 \mathring \mathrm { A } )$ . Moreover, the (100) and (200) stacking diffraction peaks of D18:TTPIC-4F blend film in in-plane direction are also higher and sharper than those of D18:BTPIC-4F blend film. The CCL of (100) stacking in D18:TTPIC-4F blend film is $9 1 . 9 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ larger than that in D18:BTPIC-4F blend film $( 8 5 . 1 \mathrm { \textrm { A } } )$ . In addition, the sharp diffraction peaks at 0.81 and $0 . 7 1 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ for D18:BTPIC-4F and D18:TTPIC-4F blend films in out-of-plane direction can be ascribed to the lamellar stacking of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F as in their single crystals. The CCL for the lamellar stacking of TTPIC-4F in the blend film is $6 8 . 6 \mathring { \mathrm { ~ A ~ } }$ which was larger than that of BTPIC-4F (53.4 A). Thus, BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F in thin-film states partially retain molecular packing in the crystal.

Moreover, as expected, TTPIC-4F exhibits higher crystallinity in both neat and blend films because of its stronger intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, which benefits charge-carrier transport. The phase separation of D18:BTPIC4F and D18:TTPIC-4F blend films was further studied using atomic force microscopy (AFM). As shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), the root-mean-square roughness of the D18:TTPIC-4F blend film is $1 . 1 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and smaller than that of the D18:BTPIC-4F blend film $( 2 . 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ , which benefits charge-carrier collection at electrode interfaces in devices.[32] Moreover, AFM phase images showed that D18:T'TPIC-4F blend film exhibited clearer phase separation with a considerably finer nanofiber structure than D18:BTPIC-4F blend film, which is in line with the GIWAXS results and would improve exciton dissociation and charge-carrier transport.

Considering the differences in crystallinities of the NFAs in thin-film states, Fourier-transform photocurrent spectroscopy external quantum efficiency (FTPS-EQE) of D18:BTPIC-4F- and D18:TTPIC-4F-based devices was conducted to investigate their impact on energetic disorder.[33] By fitting the FTPS-EQE onset, the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device exhibited a lower Urbach energy. $( 2 1 . 7 \mathrm { m e V } )$ than the D18:BTPIC-4F-based device $( 2 6 . 8 \ \mathrm { m e V } )$ (Figure 4a). The lower Urbach energy implies less energetic disorder in the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device, which can be attributed to the higher crystallinity of the materials in the blend film. To further describe the disorder in blend films, we quantitatively measured the total trap density of D18:BTPIC-4Fand D18:TTPIC-4F-based devices using the space-chargelimited-current (SCLC) method. where $V _ { \mathrm { T F L } }$ is the trap-filled limit voltage, $q$ is the elementary charge, $N _ { \mathrm { t } }$ is the total trap density, $L$ is the active layer thickness, $\varepsilon _ { 0 }$ is the permittivity of vacuum, and $\varepsilon$ is the relative dielectric constant. Thus, the total trap density in the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device was calculated to be $4 . 0 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ , which is lower than that in the D18:BTPIC-4F-based device $( 1 . 0 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 } )$ (Figure 4b). The lower trap density in the D18:TTPIC-4F blend film efficiently inhibited the localization of charge carriers, which improved the charge-carrier mobility and suppressed charge recombination in the devices.13] In addition, the trap density for the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device is among the lowest values for organic semiconductor based devices (generally $1 0 ^ { 1 6 }$ to $1 0 ^ { 1 8 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ ,[1,22] even comparable to some of those based on typical high-performance inorganic/hybrid semiconductors,[23,24] indicating that A-D-A-type NFA-based OSCs have potential to realize the PCE up to $20 \%$ [20,24] The density of states of D18:BTPIC-4F- and D18:TTPIC-4F-based devices was also determined by using thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS).[35] According to the TAS results (Figure 4c), the D18:T'TPIC-4F-based device exhibits a lower trap density of states (tDOS) of $3 . 5 { - } 6 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 3 } } \ \mathrm { e V ^ { - 1 } }$ than the D18:BTPIC4F-based device $( 0 . 4 { - } 1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 } \ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 3 } \ e V ^ { - 1 } } )$ within the energy depth of $0 . 1 5 { \mathrm { - } } 0 . 2 3 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ while D18:TTPIC-4F-based device has similar tDOS values with D18:BTPIC-4F-based device within the energy depth of $0 . 2 3 { \mathrm { - } } 0 . 4 0 \ { \mathrm { e V } } .$ It indicates that the trap density in D18:TTPIC-4F-based device is lower than that in D18:BTPIC4F-based device, which is quite consistent with the results from SCLC method. The charge-carrier mobilities of D18:BTPIC-4Fand D18:TTPIC-4F-based devices were determined using the SCLC method. As expected, the electron and hole mobilities of the D18:TTPIC-4F-based devices are $4 . 3 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $2 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, and almost one order of magnitude higher than those of D18:BTPIC-4F-based devices $( 4 . 2 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for electrons and $3 . 0 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for holes) (Figure S10, Supporting Information). The higher charge-carrier mobilities of the D18:TTPIC-4F-based device echo the lower trap density. The above results indicate that TTPIC-4F has greater potential in OSCs than BTPIC-4F.[13]

To estimate the photovoltaic performance of BTPIC-4F and TTPIC-4F, conventional OsCs with a structure of $\mathrm { I T O } /$ PEDOT:PSS/D18:NFA/PDINN/Ag were fabricated. The $J { - } V$ curves and photovoltaic parameters of the best OsCs are presented in Figure 5a and Table S3 (Supporting Information).

Evidently, the D18:TTPIC-4F-based OSC achieves a much superior PCE of $1 7 . 1 \%$ with a higher $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 8 3 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $2 5 . 2 4 ~ \mathrm { \ m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and FF of $7 6 . 6 \%$ compared with the D18:BTPIC-4F-based OSC $( \mathrm { P C E } = 1 0 . 3 \% )$ . The EQE values in the spectrum of the D18:TTPIC-4F-based OSC are remarkably higher than those of D18:BTPIC-4F-based OSC in the whole region, with a maximum value of $8 2 \%$ at $7 2 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The calculated integration currents, obtained from the EQE spectra for the D18:BTPIC-4F- and D18:TTPIC-4F-based OSCs, are 20.56 and $2 3 . 6 7 ~ \mathrm { \ m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ respectively, which agree well $( \approx 6 \%$ mismatch) with those from the $J { - } V$ measurements.

Consequently, an impressive PCE of $1 7 . 1 \%$ was achieved for D18:TTPIC-4F-based OSCs, which is the highest value for A-D-A-type NFAs and $66 \%$ higher than that for D18:BTPIC-4F-based OSCs. In addition, the trap density of $4 . 0 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ for the D18:BTPIC4F-based device is among the lowest values for organic semiconductor based devices, implying that A-D-A-type NFAs possess the potential to further enhance the PCEs of OSCs.
