# Donor Conjugated Polymers with Polar Side Chain Groups: The Role of Dielectric Constant and Energetic Disorder on Photovoltaic Performance

Bing Xu, Xueping Yi, Tzu-Yen Huang, Zilong Zheng, Junxiang Zhang, Amin Salehi, Veaceslav Coropceanu, Carr Hoi Yi Ho, Seth R. Marder, Michael F. Toney, Jean-Luc Bredas, Franky So, and John R. Reynolds\*

To better understand the correlation of the dielectric properties with the photovoltaic response in conjugated polymer:fullerene bulk heterojunction materials, the concept of introducing minimal structural change is employed to increase the polymer dielectric constant via polar cyano groups added to the end of butyl or octyl side chains in the poly(dithienosilole-thienopyrrolodione) system. Density functional theory calculations confirm that the polar groups do not affect the polymer electronic structure but can lead to an increase in overall dipole moment depending on the polymer chain conformation. Despite the increased dielectric constant (from 2.7 to 4.3 for cyano-octyl side chains and from 2.7 to 3.2 for the cyano-butyl analogues), the device characteristics employing the cyano-containing polymers are inferior to those of the devices made with unfunctionalized alkyl chains. It is found that the hole mobilities for the cyano-containing polymers are two orders of magnitude lower compared to those for the parent polymers and suggest this is due to an increase in energetic disorder caused by the strong local permanent dipoles associated with the cyano groups. The study highlights the complexity in the relationship between the dielectric constant of organic materials, the morphologies that are induced, and their photovoltaic performance.

# 1. Introduction

Organic photovoltaic (OPv) devices, as flexible, lightweight, low-cost alternatives to inorganic solar cells hold great promise in the building-integrated PV market and have received great attention over the past two decades.[1-3] In particular, polymer-based bulk heterojunction (BHJ) solar cells have been widely investigated.[4,5] With the simultaneous progress in the development of conjugated polymer electron donor phase materials that are paired with fullerene derivatives as acceptor phase materials, along with device processing and engineering advances, solution-processed single-layer polymer solar cells now yield power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) in excess of $10 \%$ [6,7] Recently, thanks to advances with nonfullerene acceptors, the PCEs for polymer-based solar cells have reached $12 { - } 1 4 \%$ [8-15] which is more than half way to their predicted theoretical efficiency limits of $2 0 { - } 2 4 \%$ [16]

One drawback of organic conjugated materials is their low dielectric constant. Upon photoexcitation, instead of generating free electrons and holes, tightly bound electron-hole pairs (excitons) are formed. It has been proposed that an increase in dielectric constant of the conjugated poly. mers may facilitate charge separation. An increased dielectric constant in photovoltaic polymers may lead to a reduction in the geminate recombination rate and the space charge effects in organic semiconductors.1718] With this, increasing the dielectric constant of the conjugated polymer has been one approach considered for improving OPV performance.[19,20]

A few studies have focused on increasing the dielectric constant of active-layer materials used in OPV devices.21] Using density functional theory calculations, Kraner et al.[22] have shown that adding polar groups could be an effective way to increase the dielectric constants of conjugated materials. Several groups have attempted to modify fullerenes to increase the dielectric constant by polar substituents, showing an increase in dielectric constants from 3.9 to ${ \approx } 5$ [23-25] While Donaghey et al. reported nonfullerene acceptors with remarkably high dielectric constants up to 9.8 by adding oligo(ethylene oxide) chains to the backbones, no device performance was reported.[26] In addition, oligo(ethylene oxide) chains have been attached to donor polymers, leading to an increase of dielectric constant from 2 or 3 for the polymers with alkyl side chains to ${ \approx } 5$ [27-31] Lu and Yang separately investigated polymers with fluorine atoms attached to polymer backbones and observed an increase in dielectric constant with fluorine substituents and an improvement in device PCE.32,33] The Jen group prepared diketopyrrolopyrrole-based polymers with cyano-ended side chains and observed enhancement in device PCE associated with the increase in polymer dielectric constant from 3.9 to 5.0.[34,35]

Following on these studies, we were inspired to modify wellperforming donor phase polymers with as minimal a change in structure as possible to tune the polymer dielectric constant with the smallest perturbation possible. It is also well known that introduction of polar functionalities in organic semiconductors can have deleterious impacts on charge carrier mobilities, by increasing energetic disorder as described by Borsenberger and Bassler.[36] Thus, the overall impact of introducing a cyano group in the molecule would likely result in a tradeoff in between, those advantageous factors described earlier, and decrease in the fill factor (FF) and short circuit current density $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ potentially resulting from decreased charge carrier mobilities. As such, we proposed to empirically test whether increasing donor polymer dielectric constant can effectively improve OPV performance or whether the potential negative impact introduction of energetic disorder would dominate. Importantly, we also sought to determine which parameters are most significantly impacted by these subtle changes. Inspired by the work of Jen and co-workers, our approach is to incorporate polar cyano (CN) groups on the side chains of dithienosilole-thienopyrrolodione (DTS-TPD) polymers without any other structural modification, as shown in Scheme 1, while preparing the polymers and active layer materials as identically as possible for making close comparisons. This design allows for a systematic investigation into the effect of a specific structural change, namely the addition of polar groups on the conjugated polymer side chains, on the dielectric, optoelectronic, charge transport, and molecular packing properties, and ultimately OPV device performance.

PDTSTPD) as a well-performing low optical gap copolymer when employed as the donor phase material in the active layer of OPV devices.[37-40] It absorbs strongly in the visible light region with an optical gap of $1 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and yields a high open circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { o c } } )$ up to ${ \approx } 0 . 9$ V with fullerene acceptors. Under optimized processing conditions, PCEs around $8 \%$ have been achieved based on DTs-TPD-C8.39] In addition, the synthesis of thieno[3,4-c]pyrrole-4,6-dione (TPD) units has recently been improved by Wolfe and Reynolds, which allows for versatile functionalization of TPD monomers with a reduced number of synthetic steps.[41]

Two pairs of polymers were designed with either C8 or C4 alkyl chains on thienopyrroledione (TPD) units, respectively (Scheme 1) and polar cyano groups were added to the end of the TPD side chains for polymers DTS-TPD-C8-CN and DTSTPD-C4-CN. The structural change is kept minimal within each polymer pair to avoid significant impact on polymer solubility and molecular packing. The idea of introducing a minimal structural change, which includes important aspects of identical polymerization and isolation methods, is an important approach when studying structure-property-performance relationships in complex materials.[42-45] The location of the polar functional groups a significant distance from the polymer backbone is expected to minimize their impact on the polymer electronic structure, where the two different side chain lengths are chosen to allow investigation of the impact of polar group location in terms of their distance from the backbone.

# 2.2. Geometric and Electronic Structure

We have designed and synthesized the family of polymers based on DTS-TPD as shown in Scheme 1. The parent polymer DTS-TPD-C8 was previously reported (typically abbreviated as

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the polymer geometric and electronic structure and excited-state properties were performed at the range-corrected level to test our design concepts. There are two potential planar conformations in the DTS-TPD repeat units; a syn-conformation (dihedral angle between DTS and TPD units is $0 ^ { \circ }$ and an anticonformation (dihedral angle is $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } ]$ . Repetition of the former leads to a bent or potentially helical structure, while the latter leads to a linear structure, as depicted in Figure 1. In the case of isolated, short oligomers, the bent conformation is energetically more stable $( \approx 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { k c a l \ m o l ^ { - 1 } }$ per repeat unit) than the linear, since it provides for an additional aryl hydrogen to carbonyl oxygen interaction between a hydrogen atom in the DTS unit and an oxygen atom in the TPD unit. A coplanar bent structure cannot extend beyond a pentamer as longer chains need to adopt an offset and possibly helical structure. In these calculations, we focused on the coplanar oligomers up to pentamers, at which point the electronic properties have fully converged. It must be borne in mind, however, that in actual samples, chains with both anticoplanar and syn-bent conformations likely coexist.

We have evaluated the energies of the lowest excited states by increasing molecular size from monomers to pentamers, where these energies converge to ${ \approx } 2 . 1 { - } 2 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ state of the antioligomers with and without cyano groups. The $\mathrm { S } _ { 0 }  \mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ Natural transition orbitals (NTOs), as shown in Figure 2, indicate that both hole and electron NTOs are located on the backbone, and have no contributions from the side chains. This validates our polymer design strategy that the cyano-containing polymers retain the same electronic properties as the parent polymer with all-alkyl side chains.

The overall dipole moments are also of interest since the desired dielectric constant increase in the cyano derivatives can be associated with an increase in dipole moment. The dipole moment in the antipentamer of DTS-TPD-C4, see Figure 3, is calculated to be around 17.3 Debye (D) in the ground state; it is aligned along the axis joining a donor moiety (DTs) to an acceptor moiety (TPD), with each repeat unit contributing some $3 \textrm { D }$ (see Table s2 in the Supporting Information). In the cyano-containing pentamer, the dipole moment jumps by some $^ { 2 0 \mathrm { ~ D ~ } }$ , which corresponds to the addition of the five cyano groups, each contributing some 4 D to the total moment. It is useful in this context to recall that the dipole moment of acetonitrile is 3.9 D.46] The syn oligomers have decreasing ground-state dipole moment past the dimer because of the compensation among the repeat-unit dipole moments as a result of the variation in their orientations (see Table S2 in the Supporting Information). Given the fluctuations in the backbone conformation expected in thin films, the dipole moments shown in Figure 3 should be considered as limit maximum values. We also note that the $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ state dipole moments, and their evolution with oligomer size, are very similar to the ground-state dipole moments.

Figure 3. Dipole moment (in Debye) of both the $\mathsf { S } _ { 0 }$ ground state (blue arrows) and the $\mathsf { S } _ { \mathsf { 7 } }$ excited state (red arrows) of the anti (DTS-TPD-C4) and (DTS-TPD-C4-CN) pentamers, as calculated at the SRSH@PBEh/6-31G(d) level.

Thus, what we learn from the range-corrected DFT calculations is that the overall dipole moment due to a polymer repeat unit has two essentially separate components: the first is due to the $\pi$ electron delocalization between the backbone donor and acceptor moieties and contributes some $3 \textrm { D }$ : the second is a strong, local component due to the intrinsic polarization of the cyano groups, which contributes some $^ { 4 \textrm { D } }$ . Although the latter does not directly influence the former, variations in the orientations of the cyano groups are susceptible to influence the motions of charge carriers on the backbone and increase the effective energetic disorder.

# 2.3. Polymer Synthesis and Characterization

The four polymers were synthesized by Stille polymerization, followed by end-capping steps and Soxhlet extraction purifications (Scheme S4, Supporting Information). Identical polymerization and purification conditions, as well as catalysts from the same batch, were employed for all samples, in-line with the strategy of having minimal change among the polymer family.[45] Table S3 in the Supporting Information summarizes the yield, molecular weights, and elemental analysis results; the latter match the calculated values. As determined by hightemperature gel permeation chromatography (GPC) at $1 4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene, all polymers have similar number average molecular weights ranging from 13 to $1 7 \ \mathrm { k D a }$ , which minimizes the influence of molecular weight difference on the polymer solubility, molecular packing and blend morphology. All polymers show good solubility in common organic solvents.

$^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra were taken to elucidate the chemical structure of the polymers (see Figure S13, Supporting Information, for the polymer $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra at room temperature). The side chain methylene protons adjacent to the cyano groups are evident near $2 . 3 \mathrm { \ p p m }$ and 2.5 ppm for DTS-TPD-C8-CN and DTS-TPD-C4-CN, respectively. In the aromatic region, multiple peaks are observed, despite the fact there is only one chemical type of aromatic proton on the polymer backbones when assuming a single conformation of the backbone; this is consistent with previous findings and likely a result of multiple backbone conformations being present,[38] as suggested as well by the results of the DFT calculations.

All four polymers exhibit very similar ionization energies and similar electrochemical bandgaps, which is in agreement with the theoretical findings that the polymer electronic properties are hardly affected by the cyano groups.

The low-frequency dielectric constants were measured for the four polymers by applying $2 0 \mathrm { m V }$ AC modulation at $1 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ using the following capacitor architecture: indium tin oxide (ITO)/polymer/Al. Thus, the cyano polar groups do indeed increase the dielectric constants of pristine donor polymers in agreement with previous work.[26,34,35] The greater increase in dielectric constant when the side chain is longer, might be result from a different population of backbone conformations between polymers with four-carbon and eight-carbon side chains, as mentioned above in our discussion of the computational studies. In addition, the shorter length of the alkyl side chain limits the free rotation of the polar cyano groups to follow the electric field, which may lead to a relatively small increase of the dielectric constant in the case of DTS-TPD-C4-CN.

As shown in Table S4 in the Supporting Information, when the polymers are blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { C M }$ , the dielectric constants further increase compared to those of the pristine polymers. Systems based on cyano-containing polymers show higher dielectric constants $4 . 8 \pm \ : 0 . 2$ for DTS-TPD-C8-CN and $4 . 7 \pm \ : 0 . 2$ for

DTS-TPD-C4-CN) than those based on the noncyano-containing parent polymers $( 4 . 0 ~ \pm ~ 0 . 1$ for DTS-TPD-C8 and $3 . 5 \pm 0 . 1$ for DTS-TPD-C4). The effect of blending on dielectric constant has been investigated in our previous studies.[48-50] It has been found that the blend dielectric constant is determined by polymer-fullerene interaction resulting in a change in the dielectric environment of the blend, which leads to a significant increase in dielectric constant in the blend upon blending the polymers with fullerene. Due to the substantial blending effect on dielectric constant, the dielectric constant of each individual polymer becomes less significant. In this case, since the blend dielectric constant is significantly higher than those in the pristine cyano-containing polymers, the blend dielectric constants become comparable between DTS-TPD-C8-CN and DTS-TPD-C4-CN.

# 2.4. Molecular Packing

Differential scanning calorimetry revealed no melting or glass transition in these polymers. In order to probe the effect of the cyano groups on polymer chain packing, grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was used to study the microstructures in thin films.[51] Figure 5 shows the 2D GIWAXS patterns for the pristine polymer films as well as the line-cut profiles in both out-of-plane and in-plane directions. The films were spin-coated onto silicon wafer substrates using the same processing conditions (see the Supporting Information), which was found to result in similar thicknesses for all four polymers.

A broad diffraction signal associated with $\pi - \pi$ stacking of the polymer backbone can be observed at $q \approx 1 . 7 5 \mathrm { \mathring { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the out-of-plane direction, indicating that the polymers predominantly adopt a face-on orientation relative to the substrate. The diffraction signal related to the (1oo) lamellar stacking for the pristine polymers appears at $q \simeq 0 . 3 \dot { 2 } – 0 . 3 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in both the inplane and out-of-plane directions. In addition, there is a distribution of orientations about the polar angles for both lamellar and $\pi - \pi$ stackings; the cyano-containing polymers show more isotropic distribution than the alkyl side chain parent polymers. These observations suggest that both face-on and edge-on orientations coexist in the thin film. The d-spacing values for the lamellar and $\pi - \pi$ stacking were calculated for each polymer; the results are summarized in Table S5 in the Supporting Information. As the backbones of the polymers are identical among all samples, the d-spacing for the $\pi - \pi$ stacking is similar for the four polymers, except for the C8-CN. This is due to strong overlap of the $\pi$ stacking peak in this polymer with the broad amorphous scattering from disordered portions of the polymer. On the other hand, the d-spacing for the lamellar stacking is larger for the polymers with eightcarbon side chains than that for the four-carbon analogues, as expected; also, the d-spacing for the lamellar stacking in the case of the cyano-containing polymers is slightly larger than that for the parent polymers. For instance, there is an increase from $1 9 . 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for DTS-TPD-C8 to $2 0 . 2 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for DTS-TPD-C8-CN, while DTS-TPD-C4 and DTS-TPD-C4-CN have lamellar distances of 18.3 and $1 8 . 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ , respectively. Such a behavior can be simply attributed to the length of the side chain and the additional volume of the cyano groups, which require larger spacing between packed polymer chains.

In general, all pristine polymer films exhibit similar molecular packing including preferred face-on orientation on the substrate with some polar angular distribution to this preference. The polymers with side chain cyano groups have relatively less degree of ordering in thin films compared to the ones with all alkyl side chains, as the diffraction intensities are relatively weaker for the cyano-containing polymers while the film thicknesses do not vary significantly. This suggests that the introduction of cyano groups reduces the overall film crystallinity. However, the molecular packing within the semicrystalline domains of each polymer sample are not significantly affected by the introduction of cyano groups to the side chain of these polymers except for slight changes in lamellar d-spacing. We have also probed the morphology of polymer $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blends via GIWAXS; the results are shown in Figure S15 in the Supporting Information. As in the pristine films, the four blend samples do not differ significantly, except that the cyanocontaining polymers have less intense diffraction signals and there is a weaker tendency for face-on orientation. This intensity difference is mainly due to the smaller thickness of the blend films based on cyano-containing polymers, as the blend films were processed using the optimized device fabrication conditions (see the Experimental Section and the Supporting Information).

# 2.5. Device Performance

As the characterization results discussed above underline that introducing polar cyano groups to the polymer side chains leads to increased dielectric constant without significantly altering the polymer optical gaps, electronic state energies, and packing in the solid state, it is expected that the cyano-containing polymers could perform well in OPV devices. The photovoltaic properties of the four polymers were studied in an inverted device configuration [ITO/ZnO/active layer/ $\mathrm { M o O } _ { x } / \mathrm { A g } ]$ , with individual optimization for each polymer sample. In fact, the PCEs achieved with the cyano-containing polymers turn out to be dramatically lower than with the parent polymers, as can be seen in Figure 6 for current density-voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ characteristics and in Table 2 for the average parameters $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } ,$ $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } ,$ FF, and PCE of the corresponding devices.

# 2.6. Charge Mobilities and Energetic Disorder

In order to understand the origin of the lower PCE values based on the cyano-containing polymers, space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurements of hole-only devices with the structure [ITO/MoOx/pristine polymer or active layer/MoOx/Ag] were carried out to determine the hole mobilities using the PooleFrenkel modified Mott-Gurney equation (Equation (1))

$$
J = \frac { 9 } { 8 } \varepsilon \varepsilon _ { 0 } \mu _ { 0 } \exp \left( 0 . 8 9 \gamma \sqrt { \frac { V } { L } } \right) \frac { V ^ { 2 } } { L ^ { 3 } }
$$

where $J$ is the current density, $\varepsilon$ is the relative dielectric constant, $\varepsilon _ { 0 }$ is the permittivity of free space, $\mu _ { 0 }$ is the zero-field mobility, $V$ is the applied voltage, $L$ is the thickness of the device, and $\gamma$ is a parameter related the strength of the field dependence.

For the polymers without the cyano groups, the hole mobilities are on the order of $1 0 ^ { - 4 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for both the pristine films and the blends with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ , which are comparable to the values reported for DTS-TPD-C8 (Figure 7).37] With the cyano groups, the hole mobility values decrease by about two orders of magnitude. For the pristine polymers, the values drop from $( 5 . 6 \pm 0 . 2 ) \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to $( 8 . 4 \pm 0 . 5 ) \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for the polymers with eight-carbon side chains and from $( 4 . 5 \pm 0 . 2 ) \times$ $1 0 ^ { - { \bar { 4 } } } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to $( 2 . 2 \pm 0 . 1 ) \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in the case of those with four-carbon side chains. A similar trend is also observed in polymer: $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blends, which further confirms the decrease of hole mobility for the polymers with the polar groups is due to the change of pristine polymer side chain rather than the mixing of polymer and fullerene. As the free charrier mobility directly determines the drift length and influences the competition between recombination and charge carrier collection,[52] the drastically reduced hole mobility is the primary cause for the lower $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ and FF values in the cyanocontaining polymers based devices.

The decrease in hole mobility after introduction of cyano groups might be correlated with the reduced crystallinity and the increased lamellar packing distance in the cyano-containing polymer films. In order to gain a better understanding, we performed temperature-dependent hole mobility measurements to extract the energetic disorder parameter using the Gaussian disorder model as shown in Equation (2)[53]

$$
\mu _ { \scriptscriptstyle 0 , \mathrm { h } } = \mu _ { \scriptscriptstyle \infty , \mathrm { h } } \exp \left\{ - \left( \frac { 4 } { 9 } \right) \left[ \frac { \sigma _ { \mathrm { h } } ^ { 2 } } { \left( k T \right) ^ { 2 } } \right] \right\}
$$

where $\mu _ { \mathrm { 0 , h } }$ is the zero-field mobility, $\mu _ { \infty , \mathrm { h } }$ the mobility at infinite temperature, $k$ the Boltzmann constant, and $\sigma _ { \mathrm { h } }$ the energetic disorder. In conjugated polymers, the charge carriers generally transport via a hopping process. Since each monomer unit is fuctuating energetically, the energy levels within a BHJ can be pictured as a Gaussian distribution with the energetic disorder parameter as the width of the density of states (DOS). Figure 8a,b shows the zero field hole mobility as a function of the inverse square of the temperature and allowing extraction of the energetic disorders.

The energetic disorders for the hole transport in the polymer:fullerene blends, with or without cyano groups, are shown in Table 3. While both of the two parent polymers with alkyl side chains exhibit energetic disorders of ${ \approx } 7 5 \ \mathrm { m e V } ,$ the corresponding values for the cyano polymers are much higher with DTS-TPD-C8-CN having a value of $1 1 0 . 6 \pm 3 . 4$ meV and DTS-TPD-C4-CN having a value of $9 4 . 0 \pm 1 . 8 \mathrm { m e V . }$ Such results indicate that introduction of cyano polar groups leads to a higher energetic disorder to the charge transport of the polymers and hence a significant decrease in hole mobility.[54] On the contrary, the presence of the cyano groups has an opposite effect on electron mobility and electron energetic disorder (Figure S18 and Table S6, Supporting Information). The electron withdrawing nature of the cyano groups allows stabilization of negative charges, resulting in enhanced electron transport, which has been demonstrated in a number of conjugated polymer systems.55,56] It should be noted, however, that the enhanced electron transport leads to a slight decrease in EQE in the fullerene-absorption range due to the strong charge imbalance of electron and hole transport, while the significant decrease in hole mobility leads to a substantial decrease in the polymer-absorption range (see Figure S17, Supporting Information).

In addition, it has been reported that energetic disorder in organic semiconductors plays an important part in governing device $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ [57] Garcia-Belmonte et al.58,59] have reported that the $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ limit is caused by recombination through tail states in BHJ polymer/fullerene solar cells. They demonstrated that the energetic disorder of the electronic DOS of donor and acceptor materials decreases the Fermi-level splitting of the electron and hole in bulk heterojunction solar cells, by confining the accumulation of carriers into the tails of the DOs. Such findings are consistent with the observations here that a lower $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ value is associated with higher polymer energetic disorders. Specifically, as the energetic disorder of the C4 polymers increases from $7 4 . 1 \mathrm { m e V }$ to $9 4 . 0 \mathrm { m e V }$ when the cyano group is added, the device $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ drops from $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { V }$ to $0 . 7 8 \mathrm { V } .$ The effect is even more dramatic with the C8 polymers, which show an even larger difference in energetic disorders $( 7 4 . 7 \ \mathrm { m e V }$ for DTS-TPD-C8 and $1 1 0 . 6 \ \mathrm { m e V }$ for DTS-TPD-C8-CN); the $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ values decrease more dramatically from $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ for DTS-TPD-C8 to $0 . 6 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ for DTS-TPD-C8-CN.

# 2.7. Energetic Disorder and $\pmb { V _ { \mathrm { o c } } }$

To explain the effect of energetic disorder on $V _ { \mathrm { o c } } ,$ the impact of polar cyano groups can be pictured in Figure 9. The accumulation of photo-generated electrons and holes thermalized in the intermediate DOs determines the quasi-Fermi levels for electrons $( E _ { \mathrm { F n } } )$ in fullerene and holes $( E _ { \mathrm { F p } } )$ in polymer.58] Therefore, the distribution of DOS plays an important role in the device $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ The one with a broader DOS will have a lower $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ as $E _ { \mathrm { F n } }$ will be lower for a given amount of electrons.

Here, adding of cyano polar groups at the end of side chain introduces more energetic disorder. In the parent alkyl-sidechain polymers, holes can move easily between neighboring monomer units due to a relative narrow distribution of DOS. However, a polar side group induces local potential variations and broadening of the density of states, generating traps for charges and resulting in a decrease in charge mobility. Additionally, the increased number of energetic disorder also has the detrimental consequence of shifting the Fermi level of donor material, therefore resulting lower $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ values. Overall, the effect can be illustrated in the schematic diagram in Figure 9.

$$

where $E _ { \mathrm { c t } }$ is the charge-transfer (CT) state energy, $L$ is the thickness of the active layer, $N _ { 0 }$ is the density of electronic states, $\tau _ { \mathrm { c t } }$ is the life time of CT state recombination, $f$ is the volume fraction of the active layer that is mixed or interfacial, and $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ is the short-circuit current. As the equation shown above indicates, the voltage loss is determined by three terms. The influence of the last term is small as it is quantified in a log scale. In this case, we only consider the loss from energetic disorder and $E _ { \mathrm { c t } }$ Using the disorder values as deduced from the mobility measurements, the $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { d i s o r d e r } }$ for DTS-TPD-C4 and DTS-TPD-C8 are $7 2 \mathrm { \ m e V }$ and $1 4 3 \mathrm { \ m e V } ,$ respectively, which is lower than the real $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ loss $1 1 0 \ \mathrm { m e V }$ for DTS-TPD-C4 and $2 3 0 \ \mathrm { m e V }$ for DTS-TPD-C8). Since the voltage loss is not only determined by energetics, the remainder of the loss could be due to other mechanisms, for example recombination.[61]

# 3. Conclusion

Polar cyano groups were introduced to the end of DTs-TPD polymer side chains as the only structural change in order to investigate the effect of this modification on polymer dielectric constant and the resulting OPV device performance. It was found that incorporating cyano groups help to increase the polymer dielectric constant, without significantly affecting the measured polymer optical gaps and energy levels, which was consistent with the findings by density functional theory calculations. The GIwAX data indicated that the introduction of cyano groups reduced the overall film crystallinity. However, the molecular packing within the semicrystalline domains was not significantly affected, leaving preferred face-on orientation on substrate and a certain degree of isotropic packing retained for all samples, except that the lamellar d-spacing increased slightly for the cyano-containing polymers. Nevertheless, the cyano-containing polymers resulted in considerably lower $J _ { \mathrm { s c } } ,$ $V _ { \infty } ,$ and FF values, and ultimately lower PCEs, compared to the parent polymers with all alkyl side chains. These results were attributed to the drastic drop in hole mobilities for the poly. mers with polar cyano groups, which was due to a higher energetic disorder in hole transport. Such decreased hole mobilities led to not only lower $J _ { \mathrm { s c } }$ values but also lower $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ values in the devices based on cyano-containing polymers. This work demonstrates that increasing the dielectric constant of active materials does not necessarily lead to enhanced device performance. In the attempts to improve OPV performance by having increased dielectric constant, one needs to make sure that other parameters, particularly charge mobility, are not sacrificed. However, this can be extremely challenging as the minimal change in polymer side chains investigated in the current work in fact induced significant differences in hole mobility.

# 4. Experimental Section

Device Preparation and measurements: DTS-TPD was blended with $\mathsf { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathsf { B M }$ in 1:1.5 ratio in chlorobenzene with the solution concentration of $2 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ . A thin layer of ZnO sol-gel was spin coated $( 4 0 0 0 ~ \mathsf { r p m } )$ onto the ITO substrate and baked at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ for $3 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ . The polymer solutions were then spin coated on top of the ${ \sf Z n O }$ Jayer. For the DTSTPD-C4 and DTS-TPD-C8, the active layer thickness is ${ \approx } 8 0 \ \mathsf { n m }$ ; For the DTS-TPD-C4-CN and DTS-TPD-C8-CN, the active layer thickness is ${ \approx } 5 0 ~ \mathsf { n m }$ . Then they were put in a thermal evaporator to deposit $8 \mathsf { \ n m }$ ${ \mathsf { M o O } } _ { x }$ and $1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { n m }$ of Ag. The device structure was ITO/ZnO/DTS$\mathsf { T P D } { \cdot } \mathsf { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathsf { B M } / \mathsf { M o O } _ { x } / \mathsf { A g }$ and the device area was $4 . 6 \mathsf { m m } ^ { 2 }$

Current density-Voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ characteristics were acquired using a Keithley 4200 semiconductor parameter analyzer along with a Newport Thermal Oriel 94021 1000 w solar simulator, at $1 0 0 m w c m ^ { - 2 }$ incident power. EQE measurements were conducted using an in-house setup consisting of a Xenon DC arc lamp, an ORIEL 74125 monochromator, a Keithley 428 current amplifier, an SR 540 chopper system and an SR830 digital signal processing (DSP) lock-in amplifier from Stanford Research Systems (SRS) HIOKI 3523-50 inductance (L)-capacitance (C)-resistance (R) meter was used to measure the device capacitance. Film thicknesses were measured using a Bruker's Dektak stylus profiler. To eliminate parasitic effects, devices were calibrated at the short and open circuit condition. The capacitance of the device was measured at $1 0 ~ \mathsf { k H z }$ with a small $2 0 ~ \mathsf { m V }$ AC modulation. The electron/ hole mobilities of the corresponding flms are extracted from fitting the dark $J { - } V$ curves using the Poole-Frenkel modified Mott-Gurney equation for SCLC. For the temperature-dependent measurements, liquid nitrogen was used for device cooling in an evacuated cryostat. For accurate temperature monitoring a silicon diode was attached to the back of the tested device.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations: The molecular structures were optimized at the DFT level, using the range-separated hybrid (RSH) functional RSH-oPBEh, which contains a $20 \%$ of Hartree-Fock exchange in the short range and $100 \%$ in the long range. The range-separation parameter $( \omega )$ was optimized in the isolated molecules via a gap-tuning procedure by minimizing the function $| ( \omega ) ^ { [ 6 2 ] }$

$$
J \left( \omega \right) = \left[ \epsilon _ { \mathsf { H O M O } } \left( \omega \right) - \mathsf { I P } \left( \omega \right) \right] ^ { 2 } + \left[ \epsilon _ { \mathsf { L U M O } } \left( \omega \right) - \mathsf { E A } \left( \omega \right) \right] ^ { 2 }
$$

Here, Ehomo and ELumo are the energies of highest occupied and lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals; while IP and EA denote the vertical first ionization potential and electron affinity of the system. The optimal values of the range-separation parameters are given in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. In order to account for the effect of the surrounding medium, the electronic properties were evaluated with the dielectric-screened range-separated hybrid functional SRSH-wPBEh,[63] with the dielectric constant taken from experiment; the excited-state energies were obtained by means of time-dependent calculations based on the Tamm-Dancoff approximation. The molecular geometry optimizations were performed with the Gaussian09 package,[64] while the electronic properties of the ground states and lowest excited states were obtained with the Q-chem 4 package.165,66] The 6-31G(d) basis set was used in all instances.
