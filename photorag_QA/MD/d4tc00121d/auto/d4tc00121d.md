# Achieving phthalide-based fluorescent materials with hybridized local and charge-transfer characteristics for efficient deep blue OLEDs†

Zijun Feng,‡a Futong Liu,‡a Zhuang Cheng,a Shuyuan Ge,a Yaxue Wang,a Zhenyu Yan,a Xiaobo Ma,a Yan Wang\*b and Ping Lu $\textcircled{1}$ \*a

The development of high-efficiency blue organic light-emitting materials is of significant importance for future illumination and display applications. In this work, three blue emitters, PPT, PPPT and PAPT with $\mathsf { D } { - } \mathsf { A } / \mathsf { D } { - } \pi { - } \mathsf { A }$ architectures have been developed, in which phthalide was first adopted as a functional acceptor. As revealed by theoretical calculations and photophysical experiments, PPT, PPPT and PAPT all display a certain degree of orbital overlap of the local excited state and exhibit typical hybridized local and charge-transfer (HLCT) characteristics. The multiple supramolecular interactions induced by phthalides endow them with high PLQYs. PAPT with the $\pi$ bridge of anthracene exerts the advantages of the LE-dominated HLCT excited state and high-lying reverse intersystem crossing (hRISC) via a hot exciton channel to obtain the highest PLQY and external quantum efficiency (EQE). As a result, the PAPT-based non-doped device achieved the maximum EQE of $7 . 5 \%$ at a high luminance of $1 8 0 0 ~ { \mathsf { c d } } ~ { \mathsf { m } } ^ { - 2 }$ . Particularly, the doped device based on PAPT exhibited superior performance with the maximum EQE of $1 0 . 2 \%$ and CIE coordinates of (0.151, 0.085), and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) was determined to be only $5 3 \ \mathsf { n m }$ . The results offer a simple and efficient approach for engineering $\mathsf { D } { - } \mathsf { A } / \mathsf { D } { - } \pi { - } \mathsf { A }$ type HLCT materials to achieve high-efficiency deep blue OLEDs.

# Introduction

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) offer attractive perspectives for future illumination and display technologies due to the many advantages they possess, such as high flexibility, low cost, good color quality, wide viewing angle and light weight.1–3 In OLEDs, the holes and electrons are respectively injected and transported to the organic emissive layer where they recombine and emit light.4–6 Thus, the development of OLEDs is highly dependent on the advancement of organic blue, green and red luminescent materials. As compared to the efficient and stable green and red light-emitting materials, blue emitters that meet the CIE coordinate requirements set by the National Television System Committee (NTSC) of (0.15, 0.08) show noticeably substandard electroluminescent performance being subject to an intrinsic large bandgap.7,8 According to spin statistics, singlet and triplet excitons are formed in organic materials at a ratio of 1 : 3 when the OLEDs are working under electrical excitation.9 Particularly, various blue luminescent materials that are capable of efficiently utilizing electrically generated triplet excitons have been developed to greatly improve the maximum external quantum efficiency $\mathrm { ( E Q E _ { m a x } ) }$ in blue OLEDs.10–12 A promising method of enhancing the exciton utilization efficiency is the rational combination of the donor (D) and acceptor (A) to form the CT excitons, which favors the electron flip.13,14 After years of endeavors, thermally-activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) and hot exciton materials, both of which possess CT characteristics and can effectively use the triplet excitons, have been successfully developed.15–23 TADF materials are generally constructed by adopting strong D and A units, which permit small exchange energies between the singlet (S1) and triplet $\left( \mathrm { T _ { 1 } } \right)$ excited states. However, the strong CT character of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ often results in a relatively small RISC rate constant and robust intramolecular interactions, which lead to poor color purity and the heavy accumulation of triplet excitons in blue OLEDs.24 The TADF OLEDs are mostly fabricated by complex doping technology, and severely decreased $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ at high brightness is also observed. As a distinct way to utilize the triplet excitons, hot exciton materials mainly collect the triplet excitons via high-lying reverse intersystem crossing (hRISC) channels with a high RISC rate constant and greatly reduced the gathering of triplet excitons.25–30 In such an approach, the $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ excited state could be adjusted to own weak CT properties, which allows for the selection of weak D and A moieties to construct materials with a high energy gap.31–35 The relatively high rate constant of hRISC $\mathrm { T } _ { n } \ \to \ S _ { 1 }$ , $n \geq 2 { \mathrm { ~ } }$ ) and shortened lifetime of triplet exciton also lay the foundation for preparing relative OLEDs by a simple non-doping technology.36–38

The pursuit of new building blocks is a never-ending task. Phthalide is a natural compound with anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, anti-diabetic, etc., properties, and is used traditionally for medicinal purposes.39,40 The introduction of the carbonyl group has been proven to be beneficial for strengthening the electron affinity. It has been applied as an efficient acceptor for room temperature phosphorescent (RTP) materials and promotes the RISC channel caused by electronic transitions from the n orbital to the orthogonally overlapped $\pi ^ { \ast }$ orbital.41,42 The carbonyl group in phthalide also endows it with a low LUMO energy of $- 2 . 6 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , showing the potential to function as a good electron acceptor in organic semiconductors. The introduction of the phthalide group into a pi-conjugated skeleton may be a feasible method for generating new deep blue luminescent materials with weak CT character and good electron-injection ability. On the other hand, the rational molecular design of phenanthroimidazole-based fluorescent materials towards highefficiency non-doped deep blue OLEDs (PI) has been employed as a prevalent building block to construct hot exciton molecules based on its high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), high thermal stability and appropriate energy level distribution for hRISC channels.43–46 Chemical modifications can be conveniently conducted at the C1 and N2 positions of the PI unit, which are beneficial for the inclusion of diverse various functional groups. Herein, we report that three novel molecules, PPT, PPPT and PAPT, based on the PI donor and phthalide acceptor connected by various $\pi$ bridges with $\mathbf { D } { - } \mathbf { A } / \mathbf { D } { - } \pi { - } \mathbf { A }$ architectures have been designed and successfully synthesized. Different $\pi$ -conjugation bridges of benzene ring and anthracene were inserted between the D and A moieties to induce the various twisting dihedral and further adjust the CT strength. In addition, their rigid and non-planar conformations contribute to hindering intermolecular pi–pi interactions to ensure high PLQYs. As a result, PPT, PPPT, and PAPT exhibited blue emission peaking at $4 6 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ , $4 3 6 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ and $4 3 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , with high PLQYs of $7 0 . 2 \%$ , $7 7 . 0 \%$ and $8 0 . 8 \%$ . They also exhibited HLCT excited state properties, and the theoretical calculations revealed a possible hRISC channel from $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ to $\mathbf { S _ { 1 } }$ states. All these materials can be applied in both doped and non-doped OLEDs. The non-doped device based on PAPT achieved the highest $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of $7 . 5 \%$ at luminance above 1000 cd $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ . Particularly, doped devices based on three compounds were also obtained, which exhibited greater EL efficiency and color purity than the non-doped ones. The $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } } s$ of OLEDs based on PPT, PPPT and PAPT reached $9 . 0 \%$ , $8 . 3 \%$ , and $1 0 . 2 \%$ , respectively, and the corresponding CIE coordinates were (0.154, 0.073), (0.155, 0.100) and (0151, 0.085). In particular, the PAPT-based doped device displayed a full width at half maximum (FWHM) value of $5 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , attributed to the enhancement of the LE component. This study provides a feasible strategy for engineering D–A/D– $\pi$ –A-type HLCT materials by employing phthalide as an acceptor to construct highly efficient deep blue emitters and provides valuable references for the further study and use of phthalides.

# Results and discussion

# Synthesis and characterization

PPT, PPPT and PAPT were produced via the synthetic route outlined in Scheme 1. The Suzuki coupling reaction between brominated phthalide with boronated anthracene was performed to afford AnPT, which was further brominated by NBS to yield BrAnPT (5-(10-bromoanthracen-9-yl)isobenzofuran-1(3H)-one). PPIB (1-phenyl-2-(4-(4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolan-2-yl)phenyl)- $1 H \cdot$ -phenanthro[9,10-d]imidazole) was prepared according to a previous report.23 The target compounds, PPT and PAPT, were yielded by Suzuki coupling reactions between PPIB and $\mathbf { P T } /$ BrAnPT. PPIPB was produced by the synthetic route shown in the ESI. $^ \dagger$ The product PPPT was gained via the Suzuki coupling reaction between PPIPB and PT. The target molecules were further purified by column chromatography and sublimation. All the intermediate products and final products were obtained in high yields of $6 2 { - } 9 7 \%$ . The chemical structure and purity of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were confirmed by mass spectral analysis as well as $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ and $^ { 1 3 } \mathrm { C }$ NMR spectroscopy.

# Crystal structures

We tried to grow the crystal structure by gradient temperature sublimation and recrystallization, and the single crystals of PPT and PPPT were successfully obtained, which allowed the investigation of the spatial structure and packing mode of these molecules. As shown in Fig. 1, the single-crystal X-ray diffraction measurements indicated that the combination of planar phthalide and rigid PI groups endowed the resulting PPT and PPPT compounds with non-planar conformations. The torsion angles between the PI, benzene rings and phthalide moieties were all around $3 5 ^ { \circ }$ in PPT and PPPT, illustrating that the molecular conjugation length was effectively confined to ensure the wide bandgap. In addition, only several $\mathrm { C - H } \cdot \cdot \pi$ interactions and hydrogen bonds were observed, and no $\pi \cdot \cdot \cdot \pi$ interactions were found for both molecules, which benefited the improvement of the PLQY in the solid state. The oxygen atom in phthalide induced three kinds of $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { H } { \cdot } \cdot \cdot \mathrm { O }$ hydrogen bonds with distances in the range $2 . 4 7 9 { - } 2 . 6 3 1 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ in PPT and four types of $\mathrm { C - H \cdot \cdot \cdot O }$ hydrogen bonds with distances of $2 . 5 0 2 { - } 2 . 7 0 1 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ in PPPT. These results suggest that phthalide could be employed for constructing emitters capable of producing multiple supramolecular interactions in the aggregated state, thereby inhibiting aggregation quenching and vibration relaxation, which are of great significance in increasing the PLQY in the solid state.

# Thermal and electrochemical properties

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed to investigate the thermal properties. As depicted in Fig. 2a, PPT, PPPT and PAPT exhibited high thermal decomposition temperatures $\left( T _ { \mathrm { d } } \right)$ of $4 3 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , $4 4 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , and $4 5 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , respectively, at a corresponding weight loss of $5 \%$ , which was gradually increased with the molecular weight. The rigid structures of PI, anthracene and phthalide endowed them with sufficiently high thermal stability and did not go through decomposition and sublimation during device fabrication and operation. In addition, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were carried out under ${ \bf N } _ { 2 }$ atmosphere at a scanning rate of $1 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } \ \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . As shown in Fig. 2b, PPT and PPPT also displayed gradually increased glass-transition temperatures $\left( T _ { \mathrm { g } } \right)$ of $1 4 3 \mathrm { ~ \ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $1 5 9 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , respectively. PPT also showed a crystallization temperature $\left( T _ { \mathrm { c } } \right)$ of $2 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and a melting temperature $\left( T _ { \mathrm { m } } \right)$ of $3 0 8 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . PAPT showed no discernible peak in DSC measurement, indicating its better thermal stability. These results suggest the potential of phthalide as a functional group for producing amorphous and uniform films through thermal evaporation, which is a prerequisite for application in vacuum-evaporated OLEDs.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted (Fig. S8a, $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ) to evaluate the frontier molecular orbital (FMO) levels. According to the onset of the oxidation potential, the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMOs) of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were almost identical and were determined to be $- 5 . 3 7 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , $- 5 . 3 5 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 3 4 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, originating from the PI unit. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMOs) of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were calculated to be around $- 2 . 6 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , which was the same as the LUMO level of phthalide, demonstrating that phthalide served as the A fragment in the three emitters. The LUMO of phthalide matched well with the widely used electron-transporting material of 1,3,5-tri(m-pyrid-3- ylphenyl)benzene (TmPyPB: -2.7 eV), facilitating charge injection from the adjacent transporting layers to the emissive layer.43

# Theoretical calculations

To further investigate the electronic structural properties of PPT, PPPT and PAPT, density functional theory (DFT) and timedependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed by Gaussian 09 program using the M06-2X/6-31G(d,p) level of theory. As shown in Fig. 3a, the optimized geometries of PPT and PPPT matched well with the single-crystal structures. The HOMOs of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were mostly distributed on the PI unit and the central $\pi$ bridges of benzene and anthracene, and the LUMOs of PPT and PPPT were simulated to be mainly located on the phthalide units, as well as the benzene rings between PI and phthalide. The LUMO of PAPT displayed a slightly different distribution that was mainly concentrated on the anthracene part with a limited extension on the phthalide unit, probably due to the inhibited conjugation by the large torsion angle between phthalide and anthracene.

![](images/6b790ec47d9b7456659af3a639130d33e4a408b56cbabb175139781eed9db03b.jpg)  
Fig. 3 (a) Optimized geometries of PPT, PPPT and PAPT. (b) NTO analysis of PPT, PPPT and PAPT. (c) Energy level diagrams of PPT, PPPT and PAPT.

The natural transition orbitals (NTOs) were calculated using the Multiwfn software.47,48 As displayed in Fig. 3b, the NTOs of $S _ { 1 }  S _ { 0 }$ of PPT and PPPT both appeared as typical hybrid local and charge transfer (HLCT) characteristics, while PAPT showed the typical local excited (LE) state-dominated HLCT, consistent with our design strategy of weak CT excited state molecules. In addition, the $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 } { - } \mathbf { T } _ { 1 }$ energy gaps of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were all larger than $0 . 6 8 \mathrm { e V }$ , and the $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 } { - } \mathbf { T } _ { 2 }$ energy gaps were calculated to be as small as 0.06 eV, 0.11 eV and $0 . 0 1 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, favoring the spin-flip through hot exciton channels. In particular, PPT and PAPT exhibited SOC constants of $0 . 4 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , suggesting that the $n$ -electron on the phthalide fragments could promote the spin–orbit coupling between T2 and S1. 24,49

# Photophysical properties

To investigate the photophysical properties of three compounds in the single-molecule state, UV-vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectra in dilute solution $\left( 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \textbf { M } \right)$ with solvents of different polarity were obtained (detailed in Table S2, $\mathrm { E S I \dagger }$ ). As shown in Fig. S14 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , the absorption band of $3 0 0 { - } 3 6 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ could be attributed to the combination of the $\pi - \pi ^ { \ast }$ transitions of PI, phthalide and benzene rings, and the weak absorption bands around $3 6 0 { - } 4 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ were assigned to the ICT transition. In particular, the absorption of PAPT could be the hybridization of the $\pi - \pi ^ { \star }$ transition of the anthracene backbone as well as the ICT transition. Their absorption spectra showed almost no changes in terms of absorption shape and peak position in different solvents, indicating small dipolar variations of the ground state in various solvents. The PL solvatochromic behavior demonstrated the existence of a CT moiety in the excited state. As displayed in Fig. 4, the PL spectra of PPT and PPPT exhibited that the vibrational structure gradually disappeared and emission peaks were red shifted. PPT showed a red-shift of $4 6 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ from low polarity $n$ -hexane to high polarity acetonitrile $( 4 2 3 - 4 6 9 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ , and PPPT had a redshift of $4 7 \mathrm { n m }$ $\left( 4 2 7 - 4 7 4 ~ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ ). PL spectra of PAPT also presented a gradually disappearing vibrational structure with an insignificant red-shift of $8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $4 3 6 { \mathrm { - } } 4 4 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , suggesting limited CT enhancement with increased solvent polarity. As shown in Fig. 4d, the linear correlation between the Stokes shift and the solvent polarity of PPT illustrated that the attributes of the $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ state were barely changed in low polar solvents and highly polar ones.

The dipole moment was calculated to be $1 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { D }$ , indicating that $\mathbf { S _ { 1 } }$ of PPT was a quasi-equivalent HLCT state. Meanwhile, the Lippert–Mataga curve of PPPT showed distinct two sections, a small dipole moment of ${ 7 . 1 \mathrm { ~ D ~ } }$ and a large dipole moment of $1 6 . 3 \mathrm { ~ D ~ }$ with distinct LE and CT components. PAPT exhibited a similar linear relationship, but the value of the dipole moment decreased to $4 . 5 \ \mathrm { D }$ . The small dipole moment changes in PAPT in different solvents indicated a LE-dominated $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ excited state with very little CT component. As a result, the LE component was gradually increased in the sequence of $\mathbf { P P T } < \mathbf { P P P T } <$ PAPT, which agrees with the results from theoretical calculations. As shown in Fig. S16 $\bigl ( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \bigr )$ , to investigate the triplet energy levels, low-temperature fluorescence and phosphorescence spectra of PPT and PPPT were obtained in THF, and the energy gaps $( \Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } } )$ between $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ were calculated as $0 . 6 4 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $0 . 7 3 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, which failed to meet the requirements of the RISC process on going from $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ to $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ . However, a small signal was observed in the delayed spectrum of PAPT due to the low generation probability of its $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ exciton. As displayed in Fig. S17 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , the phosphorescence spectrum of PAPT was successfully characterized using platinum(II) octaethylporphine (PtOEP) as the sensitizer to promote the generation of the $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ state of PAPT in the THF matrix at $7 7 \ \mathrm { K }$ . The $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ energy level of PAPT was finally determined to be $1 . 8 1 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ , which suggested a greater value of $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ (1.1 eV) and further excluded the RISC $( \mathbf { T _ { 1 } }  \mathbf { S _ { 1 } } )$ channel.

To further investigate the PL properties in the solid state, the PL spectra and lifetimes of the respective neat films and $2 0 \mathrm { w t \% }$ doped films in the host matrix $^ { 4 , 4 ^ { \prime } }$ -bis(9-carbazolyl)-1,10- biphenyl (CBP) based on PPT, PPPT and PAPT were characterized. The non-doped films of PPT, PPPT and PAPT all showed sky-blue emissions at $4 7 5 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ , $4 7 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and $4 6 9 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ , and the PLQYs were measured to be $5 3 . 5 \%$ , $6 7 . 2 \%$ and $7 2 . 1 \%$ , respectively. In doped films, they all exhibited bluer emissions as compared with the non-doped ones as shown in Fig. 5. The emission peaks located at $4 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , $4 3 4 \mathrm { n m }$ , and $4 3 8 \mathrm { n m }$ for PPT, PPPT, and PAPT had the blue-shifts of $1 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , $4 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ and $3 1 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ , respectively. The PLQYs of doped films based on PPT, PPPT and PAPT all increased to $7 0 . 2 \%$ , $7 7 . 0 \%$ and $8 0 . 8 \%$ , respectively, owing to the reduced aggregation quenching effect. The tendency that PLQY was increased in the sequence of $\mathbf { P P T } \ < \ \mathbf { P P P T } \ < \ \mathbf { P A }$ PT with the enhanced LE component was observed in both neat films and doped films. The short lifetimes at the nanosecond level were obtained in all kinds of films and excluded the possibility of a TADF channel. The lifetime of each material in the doped film was shorter than that in the non-doped film. Associated with higher PLQYs, the values of the radiative transition rate constants $\left( k _ { \mathrm { r } } s \right)$ of doped films were calculated to be larger than those of non-doped ones. The details are listed in Table 1.

# Electroluminescence properties

To evaluate the potential of PPT, PPPT and PAPT for application in OLEDs, non-doped devices were fabricated with the structure of ITO/HATCN $( 6 \ \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { T A P C }$ $\left( 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m } \right) / \mathrm { T C T A }$ $\left( 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ )/emitters $( 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { T m P y P B }$ $\mathbf { ( 4 0 ~ n m ) / L i F }$ $\left( 1 \ \mathrm { n m } \right) / \mathrm { A l }$ $\left( 1 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ by vacuum evaporation as displayed in Fig. 6a. In the non-doped devices, indium tin oxide (ITO) and Al were the anode and the cathode, respectively, 1,4,5,8,9,11-hexaazatriphenylene-hexanitrile (HATCN) and LiF were used for the hole injection layer and the electron injecting layer, di-(4-(N,N-ditolyl-amino)-phenyl)cyclohexan (TAPC) and $^ { 3 , 3 ^ { \prime } }$ -[50 -[3-(3-pyridinyl)phenyl $\mathbb { I } [ 1 , 1 ^ { \prime } { : } 3 ^ { \prime } , 1 ^ { \prime \prime } .$ terphenyl]- ${ } ^ { \cdot 3 , 3 ^ { \prime \prime } }$ -diyl]bispyridine (TmPyPB) served as the transporting layer of holes and electrons, and tris(4-carbazoyl-9- ylphenyl)amine (TCTA) was selected as the exciton blocking layer. The energy diagram of the device structure is given in Fig. S18 (ESI†). As illustrated in Fig. 6c and Table 2, among the three non-doped OLEDs, PAPT realized the best EL performance. The electroluminescent peak of PAPT was located at $4 6 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ with CIE coordinates of (0.162, 0.188). The $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ reached $7 . 5 \%$ at the high luminance of $\sim 1 8 0 0$ cd $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and the EQE remained at $6 . 2 \%$ at the luminance of $1 0 0 0 0 { \mathrm { c d } } { \mathrm { m } } ^ { - 2 }$ with a slight roll-off of $1 7 \%$ , indicating that this device could work at high brightness. The best EL efficiency and color purity of blue light achieved in the PAPT-based non-doped device could be attributed to the combination of the promising hot exciton channel and high PLQY due to the enhancement of the LE component. The PPPT-based non-doped device showed the most severe red-shifted emission due to the extension of conjugation and a high degree of CT component. Besides, all the non-doped devices showed high maximum luminance $\left( L _ { \mathrm { m a x } } \right)$ higher than 30 000 cd $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ ; in particular, the $L _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of the PPT-based device reached 45 600 cd $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ .

As the doped films of three emitters exhibited higher PLQYs with shorter emission wavelengths, the doped devices of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were also fabricated for approaching deep blue OLEDs with higher EL efficiencies. The doped devices adopted the same device structure as the non-doped ones, except that the emitting layers were changed to $2 0 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ emitters doped in host matrix CBP. As expected, deep blue OLEDs were achieved with superior EL efficiencies, and the related details are summarized in Table 2. There was no additional peak in the EL spectra, suggesting the energy transfer from the host CBP to the emitters was complete. The PPT and PPPT-based devices both exhibited deep blue emission at $4 3 6 \mathrm { n m }$ but the $\mathrm { C I E _ { y } }$ coordinate of the PPT-based device was 0.073, which was smaller than that of PPPT (0.100) as a result of a smaller FWHM of $5 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ for PPT than PPPT $( 7 2 \mathrm { n m } )$ ). Compared with PPT and PPPT, the electroluminescent peak of PAPT was red-shifted to $4 4 4 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ due to the further extended conjugation from the $\pi$ -bridge of anthracene. Thanks to the high proportion of the LE component, the FWHM of the PAPT-based doped device was restricted to $5 3 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ , and the CIE coordinates of the device were (0.151, 0.085) which was very close to the NTSC standard blue index (0.14, 0.08). Moreover, the PAPT-based doped device realized a higher $\operatorname { E Q E } _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ of $1 0 . 2 \%$ than PPT $\left( 9 . 0 \% \right)$ and PPPT $\left( 8 . 3 \% \right)$ . These EL results were competitive as compared with the deepblue OLEDs based on HLCT materials reported in the literature (Table S4, $\mathbf { E S I \dagger }$ ). Assuming that the optical out-coupling efficiency was $2 0 \%$ , the exciton utilization efficiencies (EUEs) were calculated to be $6 4 \%$ , $5 4 \%$ and $6 3 \%$ in the PPT, PPPT and PAPTbased OLEDs, respectively, indicating that the EUEs of the devices exceeded the limitation of spin-statistics of $2 5 \%$ . As shown in Fig. S21 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , the luminance exhibited a distinct linear dependence on the current density, which could rule out the TTA channel for harvesting triplet excitons.50 Furthermore, the large energy gaps between $\mathbf { S _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ $( > 0 . 6 0 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ in theoretical calculations, low-temperature phosphorescence spectra in THF and PL lifetime at the nanosecond scale in films were adequate to exclude the RISC channel via TADF mechanism. Taking into account the small energy gaps and the significant values of SOC constants between $\mathbf { S _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ , the hot exciton theory should be the most appropriate mechanism for explaining the high EL efficiency of these devices. These results indicate that phthalide could serve as a good electron acceptor for constructing highly efficient deep blue emitters and achieving highperformance OLEDs based on the hot exciton mechanism.

# Conclusions

In conclusion, three blue light-emitting materials PPT, PPPT and PAPT, with $\mathbf { D } { - } \mathbf { A } / \mathbf { D } { - } \pi { - } \mathbf { A }$ architectures have been synthesized and characterized. Phthalide was selected to construct blue fluorescence emitters as the electron acceptor due to its electron-withdrawing property. PPT, PPPT and PAPT all showed excellent thermal stability. Theoretical simulation and experimental investigations indicated their HLCT characteristics, which enabled the hRISC process via hot exciton channels. By regulating the $\pi$ bridge between D and A moieties from benzene in PPPT to anthracene in PAPT, the LE component was gradually increased. The phthalide unit also induced abundant supramolecular interactions to lock the molecular motion and reduce the aggregation effect. All these factors endowed PAPT with the highest PLQY. The PLQYs of doped films of PPT, PPPT and PAPT were measured as high as $7 0 . 2 \%$ , $7 7 . 0 \%$ and $8 0 . 8 \%$ , respectively. The PAPT-based non-doped device reached the $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of $7 . 5 \%$ at a high luminance of 1800 cd $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ with CIE coordinates of (0.162, 0.188). The PAPT-based doped device also achieved the best performance among the three compounds with $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of $1 0 . 2 \%$ and CIE coordinates of (0.151, 0.085). In addition, the FWHM was determined to be only $5 3 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , showing a high color purity. The high efficiency observed could be attributed to the efficient triplet exciton harvesting through hot exciton channels, verified by theoretical calculations combined with experimental results. This work provides insights into developing a new phthalide acceptor suitable for blue emitters and offers valuable investigations on modulating $\pi$ bridges between D and A for regulating the excited state properties of organic light-emitting materials.
