# Constructing High-Efficiency Orange-Red Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescence Polymers by Excited State Energy Levels Regulation via Backbone Engineering

Lei Hua, Yuchao Liu, Haisong Zhao, Shangyun Chen, Yuzhuo Zhang, Shouke Yan,\* and Zhongjie Ren\*

Thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) materials have attracted extensive attention because of their $100 \%$ theoretical exciton utilization. Solution-processable orange-red TADF polymers are one of indispensable participants. Herein, a series of orange-red TADF polymers with dibenzothiophene (DBT) and carbazole (Cz) units as joint backbones are synthesized. Their performance can be successfully optimized by regulating the connection positions of DBT units through backbone engineering. It is found that the pNAI37 series with DBT units embedded in the polymeric backbones at the 3, 7 sites display a better performance than those connected at the 2, 8 sites. The optimal polymer, pNAl3705, exhibits a better excited state nature, leading to the photoluminescence quantum yield of $60 \%$ -. Consequently, pNAI3705 based organic light-emitting diodes reach a maximum external quantum efficiency of $2 0 . 1 6 \%$ , and maintain $1 0 . 6 1 \%$ at $5 0 0 \ c d \ m ^ { - 2 }$ , which is in first tier among orange-red polymers. These results unambiguously suggest the potential application of the combined DBT and $\texttt { c z }$ backbones in TADF polymers. This design strategy may provide a versatile approach for optimizing the properties of TADF polymers through backbone engineering.

# 1. Introduction

Molecules featuring with thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) have extensively been designed and employed as emitters in organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), benefiting from theoretical internal quantum efficiency of $100 \%$ [1] TADF molecules usually contain a highly twisted donor-acceptor structure to construct charge transfer (CT) states with a small energy gap $\mathrm { ( E _ { g } ) }$ between the lowest singlet state $( \mathsf { S } _ { 1 } )$ and lowest triplet state $( \mathrm { T } _ { 1 } )$ $( \Delta \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { S T } } )$ which can thus provide high-efficiency reverse intersystem crossing (RISC) from triplet to singlet state[?] and high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) .3] OLEDs employing TADF materials as emitting layer have impressively attained maximum external quantum efficiency $\left( \mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } } \right)$ of over $30 \%$ so far, covering the entire spectral regions from blue to red emission.[4] However, the construction of most highefficiency OLEDs still relies on vacuum evaporation techniques characterizing with high material loss and extensive energy consumption.5l Due to the characteristic of low-cost solution-processing, polymers with TADF properties have attracted great attention. The photophysical properties of polymers can be easily regulated by tuning polymeric chain structures. Up to date, an $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of $2 5 . 4 \%$ for green polymer-based OLEDs has been achieved.[6] Thus, TADF polymers are indispensable in the application of OLEDs.7l Compared with blue- and green-emitting TADF polymers, relatively few reports focus on orange-red-emitting ones.[8] This is possibly caused by the significant non-radiative transitions and consequently the lower luminous efficiency brought by narrow band gap of red polymers.[9] Meanwhile, since polymeric backbones are unable to ensure sufficient energy transfer to red-emitting TADF units, the exciton loss via backbone luminescence in the high energy region also hinders the development of orange-red light-emitting polymers.[10] Consequently, backbone design is vital for preparing orange-red polymers.

Introducing functional units into polymeric backbones is a common design strategy for orange-red TADF polymers.[8,11] Generally, functional backbones consisting mainly of hosts can effectively suppress the concentration quenching effect of TADF units and improve carrier injection or transport.[12] Furthermore, it is demonstrated that the backbones of conjugated TADF polymers have also a significant impact on the natures of their excited states and thus the photophysical processes.[13] Therefore, it is an important strategy to improve spin orbit coupling (SOC) between singlet and triplet states, intersystem transition process and luminous efficiency via modifying the polymeric backbones.[14] The carbazole unit is one of the most prevalent backbone units for blue or green polymers because of its superior hole transport capability.[15] In this case, it usually requires TADF units having strong absorption at blue area to ensure the effective Forster energy transfer and thus keep monochromatic light emission. As a result, there are few reports on utilizing polycarbazole as the backbones of orange-red polymers due to the inefficient energy transfer from polycarbazole to red TADF units.[10] Diverse backbone units are already selected, including fluorene, dibenzothiophene sulfone, and combined two-unit backbones.[8b,11,13c] The addition of fluorene or dibenzothiophene sulfone units can effectively increase the energy levels of backbones, and thus weaken emission from itself. However, the increased energy levels of backbones generally block the expansion of molecular orbitals, which has certainly adverse effects on carrier transport of backbones.

Dibenzothiophene (DBT) with high triplet energy level has commonly been used as co-hosts in OLEDs.[16] Similar to carbazole, DBT possesses good hole transport ability.[17] Thus, good exciton transmission ability can readily be maintained when forming the polymeric backbones together with carbazole units. It consequently is a potential excellent backbone unit for orangered polymers.18] Herein, we have designed a backbone for orange-red conjugated polymers, which contains both carbazole and DBT units. High-efficiency naphthalimide-dimethylacridine (NAI-DMAc)[19] are chosen as TADF units, in which the electron donors dimethylacridine are embedded into the polymeric backbones. Meanwhile, polymers with different ratios of TADF units are prepared to optimize polymeric properties. Two series of polymers have elaborately been synthesized to regulate the excited state natures by controlling the connection positions between

DBT and TADF units. All polymers exhibit an orange-red emission at ca. $6 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ with TADF characteristic. As expected, the connecting positions of DBT units have significant effect on the excited state natures and photophysical properties of the TADF polymers. The pNAI37 series with DBT units embedded into polymeric backbones at the 3, 7 sites display a faster rate of reverse intersystem crossing $( k _ { \mathrm { R I S C } } )$ and a higher PLQY than those connected at the 2, 8 sites. Among them, pNAI3705 with $5 \%$ TADF units presents the outstanding PLQY of $60 \%$ and $k _ { \mathrm { R I S C } }$ of 7  105 s-1. OLEDs based on pNAI3705 achieve an EQEmax of $2 0 . 1 6 \%$ , and exhibit the better performance at high voltages, demonstrating that the mixed backbone of DBT and carbazole is well fit for orange-red TADF polymers. It is also provably effective to optimize the excited state natures by controlling polymeric chain structures to improve the device performance, which provides an idea for designing high-performance TADF polymers.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Molecular Design and Synthesis

Two series of orange-red TADF polymers consisting of TADF units and backbones of carbazole and dibenzothiophene units with different chain configurations were constructed by simple Suzuki polymerization, in which the dibrominated TADF units and carbazole units reacted with the dibenzothiophene bisborate at different sites. As shown in Figure 1, the pNAI37 and pNAI28 series were obtained using 3,7-bisbroate DBT and 2,8- bisbroate DBT, respectively. In order to obtain the optimized photophysical properties, three kinds of TADF polymers with $2 \%$ $5 \%$ and $10 \%$ TADF units in molar ratios were prepared considering that the good properties of NAI-DMAc depends on the lower doping concentration,[19] named as pNAI3702, pNAI3705, pNAI3710 and pNAI2802, pNAI2805, pNAI2810 for pNAI37 and pNAI28 series, respectively. The synthetic routes of all monomers are shown in Scheme S1 (Supporting Information), and chemical structures of the monomers were confirmed by $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR, $^ { 1 3 } \mathrm { C }$ NMR and high-resolution mass spectra (Figures S1-S15, Supporting Information). The molar fractions of TADF units in all polymers were determined by H-atom integral proportions of the characteristic peaks of acridine groups and alkyl chains of carbazole in $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra, as shown in Figures S16 and S17 (Supporting Information). The proportions of TADF units are very close to the feed ratios for all polymers. The molecular weight and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined by gel permeation chromatography, exhibiting typical weight-average molecular weights $( \mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } } )$ in range of $6 . 8 \mathrm { - } 1 2 \mathrm { k D a }$ and PDI in range of 1.37-1.60, respectively (Figure S18 and Table S1, Supporting Information). The medium $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ can reduce the negative impact of the polymeric long-chains on the device performance.[10] The thermal properties were evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry and thermogravimetric analysis. The glass transition temperatures $( \mathrm { T _ { g } s } )$ of these polymers are in the range of $9 0 { - } 1 5 6 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { ~ C ~ }$ (Figure S19, Supporting Information). And $\mathrm { T _ { g } s }$ of polymers are drastically increased with the improved ratios of TADF units due to the rigid structure of TADF units. The decomposition temperatures are over $4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Figure S20, Supporting Information), indicating a good thermal stability that meets the requirement of device fabrication. Because of the long alkyl chains introduced into the carbazole units, all polymers present good solubility in common organic solvents.

# 2.2. Frontier Molecular Orbital Simulation

In order to better understand the effect of backbone structure on the photophysical properties of the TADF polymers, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out. According to the content of TADF unit in polymers, the fragments with one TADF unit, two DBT units and two expanded carbazole units were selected. The distributions of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOmO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were calculated by Gaussian 16 with the B3LYP 6-31 G(d,p) level. Taking pNAI37 and pNAI28 as examples, the selected fragments obviously contain the entire extension of HOMOs, indicating the choice of fragments is reasonable as shown in Figure 2a.

The two kinds of polymers display the similar distributions of LUMO, which are located on the electron-accepting naphthalimide groups of TADF units. The LUMO levels of pNAI37 and pNAI28 are $- 2 . 5 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 2 . 5 9 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively. However, due to the different connection positions of DBT units in the backbones, obvious differences in the distributions of HOMO can be observed for the two kinds of polymers. Their HOMOs extend from the dimethylacridine group of the donors to the neighboring backbones. Moreover, due to the more twisted configuration, HOMO of pNAI28 is hardly distributed on the carbazole units. By contrast, HOMO of pNAI37 is more widely stretched on the backbones, which passes through the DBT units and distributes on the carbazole units in a small amount. Meanwhile, as shown in Figure 2a and Table S2 (Supporting Information), the HOMO of pNAI37 does not locate on the sulfur atom of DBT, while the sulfur atom has a significant contribution to HOMO for pNA128. Due to the greater extension of HOMO orbitals, the energy gap $\mathrm { ( E _ { g } ) }$ of pNAI37 is slightly smaller than that of pNAI28. In addition, the relatively isolated frontier molecular orbits (FMOs) distributions for both polymers can reserve small $\Delta \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ of only 8 meV and thus accelerate the triplet-to-singlet spin flip processes.

From total self-consistent field (SCF) density performed by using the electrostatic potential method, it can be obtained that relatively electron-rich regions are continuously distributed on the polymeric backbones as shown in Figure 2b, which enables rapid migration of carriers and excitons.[20] Due to the different connection sites of DBT units, the chain structures of two polymers are also significantly disparate. The backbones of pNAI37 are more stretched, so the distribution of electron-rich regions in the backbones is more uniform. The electron-rich regions of pNA128 are somewhat scattered due to its twisted backbones. This reveals that pNA137 may possess a better carrier transport ability.

# 2.3. Electrochemical Properties

The electrochemical behaviors of six polymers were investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) in degassed anhydrous acetonitrile solution. As shown in Figure S21 (Supporting Information), the CV curves show quasi-reversible oxidation and reduction processes for all polymers. Consistent with FMO simulations, the LUMO energy levels of all polymers are similar, at ca. $- 3 . 4 0 \mathrm { e V } ,$ because of the contribution of reduction potential is mainly from the naphthalimide groups. While the oxidation sites primarily come from electron-donating dimethylacridine groups and polymeric backbones. Within the same series, HOmO energy level is gradually declined with the decreased proportion of TADF units, accompanied with a higher $\mathrm { E _ { g } }$ . Furthermore, with the same ratio of TADF units, HOMO energy levels of the pNAI37 series are always higher than that of the pNAI28 series. Meanwhile, $\mathrm { E _ { g } s }$ of the pNAI37 series are also relatively small, which is consistent with the FMO simulations. The difference of the connection positions of DBT units in the backbones affects the HOMO distributions, and thus also influences the electrochemical properties of the polymers.

# 2.4. Natural Transition Orbits Calculation

The excited state properties of the TADF polymers were analyzed by calculation of natural transition orbitals (NTOs). The excited states of two typical polymeric fragments were calculated using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) and Multiwfn was utilized to analyze NTOs.[21] SOC matrix element (SOCME) calculation between singlet and triplet states was also carried out using ORCA 4.1.1 package at B3LYP/G TZVP.[22] The complete excited energy level diagrams of two typical polymers are shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), and SOCMEs between $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { { T _ { n } } }$ states are listed in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

As shown in Figure 3, the blue and pink areas represent hole and electron parts of the excited states, respectively. ${ < } \Psi _ { \mathrm { h } } \Psi _ { \mathrm { e } } { > }$ is the overlap integral of hole and electron orbitals, which reveals the fraction of localized excitations in excited states. Obviously, both $\mathsf { S } _ { 1 } \mathsf { s }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } \mathrm { s }$ of two series of polymers behave as charge transfer excited states $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { C T }$ for charge transfer singlet excited state, $^ 3 { \mathrm { C T } }$ for charge transfer excited triplet state), and $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 } \mathrm { s }$ show localized excited triplet state $( ^ { 3 } \mathrm { L E } )$ . Similar to the FMO simulations, the electron areas for two series of polymers are alike but the hole areas are slightly different. The difference is also resulted from the contribution of sulfur atoms. That is, the hole area of the pNAI37 series doesn't contain sulfur atoms, whereas the hole area of the pNA128 series does. Consequently, the localized proportion of ${ } ^ { 3 } { \mathrm { C T } }$ state is 0.151 for the pNAI37 series, which is slightly higher than that of the pNA128 series (0.142). Meanwhile, $^ 1 { \cal C } \mathrm { T }$ and $^ 3 { \mathrm { C T } }$ energy levels of the pNAI37 series are both $1 7 \mathrm { m e V }$ higher than those of the pNA128 series due to the different contributions of sulfur atoms. Because of the almost same $^ 3 \mathrm { L E }$ states of two series of polymers, the pNAI37 series demonstrate a smaller difference between $^ 1 \mathrm { C T }$ and $^ { 3 } \mathrm { L E }$ energy levels $( \Delta E _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 C T \cdot 3 L E } )$ than the pNA128 series. According to the second-order oscillator coupling mechanism, the smaller energy level difference leads to the higher RISC efficiency.[23] Because $^ 3 \mathrm { L E }$ state can act as a mediator, which can help excitons to spin flip from triplet to singlet state. The diverse excited states also reflect the influence of DBT attachment positions on the photophysical properties of polymers. Thus, the pNAI37 series theoretically have the better photophysical properties than the pNA128 series.

# 2.5. Photophysical Properties

A series photophysical characterizations of polymers were carried out to elucidate the effect of backbone structure on the polymer properties. First, as shown in Figure 4a, the pNAI37 and pNAI28 series exhibit similar UV absorption spectra in pure films with obvious bimodal absorption of $\pi \cdot \pi$ transition in the range of $2 0 0 { - } 4 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . As mentioned above, the different connection positions of DBT units result in the various expansion of HOMO on the polymeric backbones. So, the absorption of the pNAI37 series with the wider HOMO expansion shows a significant redshift compared with the pNAI28 series, which is consistent with the results of CV measurements. At the same time, the weak absorption assigned to the charge transfer of TADF units at $4 0 0 { - } 6 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ can also be obviously observed. In the photoluminescence (PL) spectra, all polymers exhibit a distinct red emission at ${ \approx } 6 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . At ${ \approx } 4 2 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , there exists a weak emission peak from the polymeric backbones, especially for pNA12802 and pNAI3702. In addition, with the same proportion of the TADF units, the PL emission of pNAI3702 demonstrates $9 \ \mathrm { n m }$ redshift $( 6 1 2 \mathrm { n m } )$ compared with pNA12802 due to the expansion of HOMO, which is corresponding to the results of FMO simulations and CV measurements. Furthermore, the red-shifted emissions of two series of polymers can be found when increasing the proportion of TADF units. From the perspective of CV measurements, the higher proportion of TADF units, the lower $\mathrm { E _ { g } } { \mathrm { : } }$ which is consistent with the red-shifted PL spectra. However, PL spectra of the pNAI37 series display only $1 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ redshift from pNAI3702 to pNAI3710, while the spectra of the pNAI28 series obviously increase by $2 4 \mathrm { n m }$ from pNAI2802 to pNAI2810. This may be caused by different aggregation states of the polymers with different attachment positions of DBT units in the backbones. The more stretched backbones of the pNAI37 series with a larger space occupation volume can inhibit the aggregation between polymer chains, and thus a correspondingly smaller redshift can be found. Dispersing in toluene solution, UV absorption spectra of the polymers are nearly consistent with that in film states, as shown in Figure S23a,b (Supporting Information). Moreover, the absorption edge in toluene solution is correspondingly blue-shifted as the proportion of TADF groups decreases, reflecting a higher $\mathrm { E _ { g } }$ , which is in full accord with the results of

CV measurements. In terms of PL emissions in solution, they mainly exhibit the backbone emission at ${ \approx } 4 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , with only a weak red emission at ${ \approx } 6 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$

Furthermore, $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ energy level of polymers were determined by the onset positions of the fluorescence spectra at $3 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ and the phosphorescence spectra at $7 7 \ \mathrm { K } ,$ respectively. As shown in Table 1 and Figure S24 (Supporting Information), their $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ energy levels well correspond to the results of fluorescence spectra. In the same series, the polymers with the higher TADF unit ratios show the lower $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ levels. Meanwhile, $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ of pNAI3702 is lower than that of pNA12802 due to the different HOMO orbital expansion. The connection site of the DBT units in the backbone has no obvious effect on $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ . All polymers show similarly tiny $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { S T } }$ of $0 . 0 4 { - } 0 . 0 7 \ \mathrm { e V } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ which can ensure the efficient RISC processes.

PLQYs of the doped films with $1 0 \ \mathrm { \ w t } \%$ polymers in 9Hcarbazole-3-carbonitrile (mCP-CN) were characterized. mCP-CN is the optimized host, which is selected according to the PLQY of the $1 0 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ pNAI3705 doped films (Figure S23c, Supporting Information). The PLQYs in vacuum conditions were converted from the PLQYs in air and the integration ratio of steady-state spectra in vacuum and air. The vacuum and air steady-state spectra of all polymers are shown in Figure S25 (Supporting Information). Obviously, under the same excitation conditions, the intensity of steady-state spectra of all polymers in vacuum is enhanced compare to the counterpart in air. This indicates that their photoluminescence processes involve the participation of triplet excitons. Due to the quenching effect of oxygen on the triplet exciton in air, the fluorescence intensity in air is obviously reduced, which is consistent with the characteristics of TADF materials. It is distinct that with the same fraction of TADF units, the pNA137 series represent a higher PLQY, in which pNAI3705 possesses the highest PLQY of $60 \%$ , as shown in Figure 3c. Possibly, the stretched backbones of the pNAI37 series can effectively inhibit the aggregation-induced quenching of triplet excitons.[25] In the same series, the polymers with $5 \%$ TADF units perform the maximum PLQY. pNAI2802 and pNAI3702 display the low PLQYs due to the insufficient energy transfer from the polymeric backbones to the TADF units, which form obvious exciton traps. On the other hand, pNAI3710 and pNAI2810 with more TADF units and higher exciton concentration may bring quenching of aggregation and thus the low PLQYs.

The fluorescence decay characteristics also reflect the influence of different DBT attachment positions on the photophysical properties of polymers, as shown in Figures 4d,e. The effect of percentage of the TADF units on polymeric lifetimes is consistent with PLQY as well. pNAI2810 and pNAI3710 display a shorter delayed fluorescence lifetime of ${ \approx } 1 . 2 – 1 . 3 ~ { \mu } { \mathrm { s } }$ and a lower delayed fluorescence ratio of $5 6 . 3 \%$ due to the quenching effect of high-concentration TADF units. Although pNAI2802 and pNA13702 have similar fluorescence lifetimes and delay ratios with pNAI2805 and pNAI3705, they show the lower radiative transition rates due to the existence of exciton traps. Both pNA12805 and pNAI3705 show the balanced decay rate of prompt fuorescence $( k _ { p } )$ and $k _ { R I S C }$ among the same series, which can reduce the non-radiative transition of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and improve the utilization of all excitons. Furthermore, the $k _ { R I S C }$ of pNAI3705 is relatively faster, $7 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , due to its shorter fluorescence lifetime, which possibly benefits from its backbone architecture. Therefore, more efficient RISC channels can be established by introducing the DBT units and controlling its connection positions through backbone engineering. This design strategy is effective for optimizing the performance of TADF polymers.

Taking pNA13705 as an example, the kRISC reduces from $7 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ to $1 . 4 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ as the temperature decreases from 300 to 77 K. Furthermore, the experimental activation energies $\big ( E _ { \mathrm { a } } ^ { \mathrm { { \ R I S C } } } \big )$ can be obtained by fitting the temperature-dependent $k _ { R I S C }$ with Arrhenius equation. In this case, the $E _ { \mathrm { a } } ^ { \mathrm { \ R I S C } }$ value is equal to the adiabatic energy splitting between excited singlet and triplet states . All polymers exhibit similar slopes, with $E _ { \mathrm { a } } ^ { \mathrm { { \ R I S C } } }$ ranging from 12.1 to $1 7 . 3 \mathrm { m e V } ,$ which is apparently small enough to ensure the efficient RISC processes. These results are consistent with the FMO simulations.

Meanwhile, under the luminance of $5 0 0 \ \mathrm { c d } \ \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ its EQE can still maintain at $1 0 . 6 1 \%$ , which is in first tier of the reported orange-red TADF polymers,[8,10,11,13c,26] as summarized in Figure 5f and Table S5 (Supporting Information). Although the electroluminescent performances presented herein are champion data, the EL performances for each polymer are measured in at least two same devices, over eight pixels with errors less than $2 \%$ . This study reflects EQE and efficiency roll-off of orange-red TADF polymeric OLEDs can significantly be optimized through the simple control of polymeric backbones.

# 3. Conclusion

In this study, the performance of orange-red TADF polymers is fully optimized by adjusting the connection positions of DBT units in the polymeric backbones. Small structural change of polymeric backbones has a significant impact on the aggregation states, photophysical properties and excited state properties of TADF polymers. Accordingly, the pNAI37 series polymers exhibit the optimized excited state properties, consequently reach a PLQY of $60 \%$ and a faster $k _ { R I S C }$ of $7 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ . The corresponding orange-red OLEDs show an $\mathrm { E Q E } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ of $2 0 . 1 6 \%$ with a relatively lower efficiency roll-off, demonstrating the backbone composed of DBT and carbazole units has a good application prospect in orange-red TADF polymers. Therefore, similar design strategy via backbone engineering can be extended to much more TADF polymers for improving their photophysical properties and thus the corresponding device performance.
