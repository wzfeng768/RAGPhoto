# Enhancing the Photovoltaic Performance of LadderType Heteroheptacene-based Nonfullerene Acceptors by Incorporating Halogen Atoms on Their Ending Groups

Shuo Wan, Yunlong Ma, Dongdong Cai, Wenyuan Lin, Pengsong Wang, Jinyun Wang, and Qingdong Zheng\*

Ending group halogenation is an effective strategy for modulating the energy levels, bandgaps, and intermolecular interactions of nonfullerene acceptors. Understanding the influence of different halogen atoms on the acceptor properties is of great importance for designing high-performance nonfullerene acceptors. Here, three acceptor-donor-acceptor (A-D-A) type nonfullerene acceptors (M5, M6, and M7), which are constructed by using a Iadder-type heteroheptacene core without the traditional $\mathsf { \mathsf { s p } } ^ { 3 }$ carbon-bonded side chains as the electron-rich core, and 2-(3-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-inden-1- ylidene)malononitrile without or with halogen atoms as the ending groups. The nonfullerene acceptors with chlorinated (M6) and brominated (M7) ending groups exhibit broadened absorption spectra, down-shifted energy levels, and enhanced molecular ordering compared to the counterpart without any halogenated ending groups (M5). Among the nonfullerene acceptors, M6 has the strongest intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ interaction with its shortest $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance and the longest coherent length which are beneficial for enhancing the charge transport and therefore boosting the photovoltaic performance. An excellent power conversion efficiency of $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ is achieved for the best-performing polymer solar cell based on M6. These results suggest that the halogenated ending groups are essential for high-performance heteroheptacene-based nonfullerene acceptors considering their simultaneous enhancements in both the light-harvesting and the charge transport.

# 1. Introduction

Nonfullerene polymer solar cells (PSCs) have attracted increasing attention in the past 5 years due to the significantly enhanced power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) by the replacement of the traditional fullerene derivatives with the nonfullerene counterparts.[1-6] Compared to the fullerene derivatives, nonfullerene acceptors possess easily modifiable molecular structures as well as wide-range tunable energy levels and absorption bands, which are advantageous for further increasing the short-circuit current density $( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } )$ and the open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ values of PSCs. With extensive efforts by many research groups, PCEs of the nonfullerene PSCs have far exceeded those of state-of-the-art fullerene-based solar cells by now.[7-13]

Among several different types of nonfullerene acceptors, those with rod-like acceptor-donor-acceptor (A-D-A) scaffolds are the most intensively researched with their rapidly increased PCEs.[14-16] The A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors generally contain an electron-rich ladder-type fused-ring core bonded with soluble side chains, and two strong electron-withdrawing groups at both ends of the conjugated backbone. This unique structural feature offers great convenience and flexibility to tailor the properties of the acceptor materials. For a nonfullerene acceptor, both the donor (electron-rich) core and the ending groups are essential because either of them can greatly affect the properties of the resulting acceptor materials such as bandgaps, energy levels, and intermolecular interactions.7,18] So far, ladder-type heteroarenes are regarded as ideal donor cores for building high-performance A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors with their coplanar geometry and excellent $\pi$ electron delocalization feature.[1419] To further enhance the charge transport and photovoltaic properties of the nonfullerene acceptors based on a given electron-rich core, modification on their ending groups is an important strategy.

Ending group halogenation has proven to be an effective and simple method to optimize performance of the nonfullerene acceptors. As the most electronegative atom, fuorine atoms have been widely used for constructing nonfullerene acceptors with improved optoelectronic properties.[2,7] It has been found that the introduction of fuorine atoms can reduce the optical bandgap of the resulting acceptor, accompanied by both enhancements in the absorption coefficient and the intermolecular interaction.[20,21] Thus, the photovoltaic properties of nonfullerene acceptors with fluorinated ending groups can be greatly improved. So far, most of the reported high-performance acceptors, such as IEICO-4F, IT-4F, and Y6, contain fluorinated ending groups.7,223] At the same time, trifluoromethyl groups have also been successfully implanted in the nonfullerene acceptors for achieving PSCs with improved PCEs.[24,25] As the other two important halogen atoms, chlorine and bromine also exhibit strong electron-withdrawing ability, and they can also induce strong intermolecular interactions.[26-28] However, compared to the fluorinated ending groups, few studies have been focused on the use of chlorinated or brominated ending groups for constructing efficient nonfullerene acceptors.2] Furthermore, chlorinated and brominated ending groups are of relatively low cost because they can be synthesized more easily from the commercial raw materials.[29-31]

Very recently, our group reported a novel type of ladder-type heteroheptacene cores which are free of the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 }$ -hybridized bridging carbons.[32-35] The aggregation and crystalline properties of these heteroheptacene-based nonfullerene acceptors can be controlled by modulating the size of the neighboring sidechains as well as the aromaticity (aromatic stabilization energy) of the heteroheptacene core. Through reducing the $\pi - \pi$ interaction (or stacking) distance, the resulting nonfullerene acceptor (M36) showed enhanced charge transport and therefore a greatly boosted PCE of $16 \%$ , demonstrating the great potential of these ladder-type heteroheptacene cores for efficient nonfullerene acceptors.133] However, the effects of ending groups on the photovoltaic properties of these heteroheptacene-based nonfullerene acceptors have not been systematically investigated, and it shall also be important for developing high-performance nonfullerene acceptors.

Based on the above considerations, we designed and synthesized three A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors (M5, M6, and M7 in Figure 1a) based on benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']bis(4-H-dithieno[3,2- ${ \tt b } ; 2 ^ { \prime } , 3 ^ { \prime }$ d]pyrrole) (BDTPT) end-capped with 1,1-dicyanomethylene-3-indanone (INCN), dichlorinated INCN (INCN2Cl), or dibrominated INCN (INCN2Br). We systematically compared the differences among the three nonfullerene acceptors in terms of the optical and electronic properties, molecular orientation behaviors, crystallinity, film morphology and photovoltaic performance. It is found that M6 and M7 with the chlorinated and brominated ending groups, respectively, exhibit narrower bandgaps, down-shifted energy levels, and enhanced intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ interaction in comparison with the non-halogenated counterpart (M5), thereby leading to the notable improvements in the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and fill factor (FF) values of the corresponding PSCs. Furthermore, the chlorinated acceptor (M6) has a closer intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance and an improved molecular ordering than the brominated acceptor (M7) thus leading to a higher electron mobility as well as improved photovoltaic performance of the former one. As a result, when blended with a widebandgap polymer donor of PM6, the M6-based device (with Ag electrode) achieved a high PCE of $1 4 . 2 8 \%$ .In contrast, the devices based on M5 and M7 showed lower PCEs of $3 . 0 3 \%$ and $1 2 . 8 2 \%$ respectively. More encouragingly, when the top $\mathrm { A g }$ cathode was replaced with Al, the best-performance PM6:M6- based device showed an outstanding PCE of $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ , which is the best value for binary PSCs based on all A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors with the chlorinated ending groups. At the same time, when paired with the polymer donor of PBDB-T, the optimal M6-based device delivered a PCE of $1 0 . 6 3 \%$ , which is also higher than those of the PSCs based on M5 $( 9 . 2 0 \% )$ and M7 $( 1 0 . 2 4 \% )$

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Synthesis and Characterization

The synthetic routes for M5, M6, and M7 are displayed in Figure S1, Supporting Information. The dialdehyde intermediate BDTPT-CHO was synthesized according to the procedure reported by us previously.[32] Condensation reactions between BDTPT-CHO and INCN, INCN2Cl, and INCN2Br in chloroform afforded the corresponding acceptors M5, M6, and M7 in $82 \%$ $8 5 \%$ , and $84 \%$ yields, respectively. All the acceptors were characterized by $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR, $^ { 1 3 } \mathrm { C }$ NMR, elemental analysis and high-resolution mass spectrometry (Supporting Information). All the three acceptors are soluble in the common solvents, such as chloroform, chlorobenzene, and ortho-dichlorobenzene.

In order to study the effect of the halogen atoms on the molecular geometries and frontier orbital energy levels of M5, M6, and M7, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed at the $8 3 \mathrm { L Y P } / 6 { \cdot } 3 1 1 \mathrm { G } ^ { \ast \ast }$ level. To simplify the calculations, all the long alkyl chains were replaced by isobutyl groups. As shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information, the backbones of the three acceptors are of a nearly planar and linear configuration, which can promote the intermolecular interaction and therefore enhance the carrier transport. In addition, for all the three acceptors, their highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMOs) are mainly distributed along the conjugated electron-rich cores and the malononitrile groups, while lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMOs) are delocalized over the whole $\pi$ conjugated systems. These results indicate that the ending group halogenation has little effect on the geometries and orbital spatial distributions of the acceptors. The calculated LUMO/HOMO levels for M5, M6, and M7 are $- 3 . 5 0 / - 5 . 5 7$ $- 3 . 7 4 / - 5 . 7 6$ and $- 3 . 7 2 / - 5 . 7 4 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively. Due to the electron-withdrawing properties of the halogen atoms, both the HOMO and LUMO levels for M6 and M7 decreased in com parison with those for M5. The electrostatic potential (ESP) distributions of the three acceptors were then estimated,[36,37] and the calculation results are shown in Figure 1b. Generally, the ESP values on the most part of the electron-rich units are negative, whereas those of the electron-deficient units are positive. As shown in Figure 1b, the positive areas of ESP distributions in the conjugated surfaces of M6 and M7 are larger than that of M5. Therefore, when blending these acceptors with a donor which has a negative ESP (Figure S6, Supporting Information), the intermolecular electric field (IEF) values for the halogenated nonfullerene systems (M6 and M7) would be higher than that for the non-halogenated system (M5). For a PSC device, increasing the IEF value between the polymer donor and the nonfullerene acceptor is beneficial for exciton dissociation thereby facilitating the electron transfer from the polymer donor to the nonfullerene acceptor.[36]

Absorption spectra for M5, M6, and M7 in dilute chloroform solutions and in thin films were measured to investigate the halogenation effect on their optical properties. The absorption spectra are shown in Figure 1c and Figure S3a, Supporting Information, and the related optical parameters are summarized in Table 1. As shown in Figure S3a, Supporting Information, each acceptor in solution has a strong absorption band ranging from $6 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and the absorption peaks $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { m a x } } \mathrm { s } )$ for M5, M6, and M7 are 727, 756, $7 5 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , in that order. However, M6 and M7 show obviously red-shifted absorption bands compared to M5, which is due to the enhanced intramolecular charge transfer from the electron-rich core to the electron-deficient ending groups with the introduction of more electronegative halogen atoms.[38,39] Moreover, M6 and M7 in solutions have similar absorption coefficients of $2 . 1 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ and $2 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ c m ^ { - 1 } }$ respectively, both of which are higher than that for M5 $( 1 . 8 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ c m ^ { - 1 } ) }$ . This can be ascribed to the hyperchromic effect of the incorporated halogen atoms for M6 and M7. Similar phenomena were also reported in some other acceptor systems.40] Compared with those in solutions, absorption spectra of the three acceptors in thin films are conspicuously red-shifted (Figure 1c) with a more pronounced 0-1 transition, demonstrating the enhanced intermolecular interactions in the thin film state. The nonfullerene acceptors with halogen atoms show red-shifted absorption bands in thin films compared to the acceptor without the halogen atoms. At the same time, M6 and M7 in thin film show red-shifted $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } \mathrm { s }$ by 58 and $5 9 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively, compared to those in solution. Both the red-shifts for M6 and M7, in going from solution to thin film, are larger than the M5-based counterpart $( 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ . These results indicate that the introduction of halogen atoms not only enhances the intramolecular charge transfer transition, but also improves the intermolecular interaction of the nonfullerene acceptors. The optical bandgaps $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { \mathrm { o p t } } )$ calculated from the absorption onsets for M5, M6, and M7 are 1.47, 1.38, and $1 . 3 8 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively. As shown in Figure 1c, the two halogenated acceptors (M6 and M7) have more complementary absorption spectra with those of the wide-bandgap polymer donors (PM6 and PBDB-T) in comparison with the non-halogenated counterpart (M5), which is beneficial for the enhanced light harvesting of the corresponding PSCs.

To understand the differences in the molecular orientation and crystallinity of the three acceptors, grazing incidence wideangle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) experiments were conducted. The 2D GIWAXS patterns as well as the extracted 1D profiles of the pure acceptor films are shown in Figure 2, and the related parameters are listed in Table S4, Supporting Information. As shown in the figure, all the pure films (M5, M6, and M7) show a well-defined (010) diffraction peak along the $q _ { z }$ axis (out-of-plane direction) and a clear (100) peak in the $q _ { x \gamma }$ axis (in-plane direction), suggesting a preference of face-on orientation for all the acceptor molecules. For M5, the (100) and $\pi - \pi$ interaction (O10) diffraction peaks are located at around 0.33 and $1 . 7 3 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ , corresponding to a lamellar stacking distance $( d _ { \mathrm { L } } )$ of $1 9 . 0 9 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ and a $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance $( d _ { \pi } )$ of $3 . 6 3 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ , respectively. Based on the Scherrer equation $( \mathrm { C L } = 2 \pi / \mathrm { F W H M }$ , where FWHM is the full width at half-maximum of the diffraction peak), the coherence lengths (CLs) for (100) and (010) diffraction peaks were calculated to be 12.67 and $6 \dot { 2 } . 1 0 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ , respectively. With the halogenated ending groups, the (010) diffraction peaks in the out-of-plane direction (OOP) of M6 and M7 shift toward 1.80 and $1 . 7 6 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively, but the positions of their (100) diffraction peaks remain almost unchanged. From their (010) peak positions, the $\pi - \pi$ interaction distances for M6 and M7 films were estimated to be 3.49 and $3 . 5 7 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ ,respectively, and both of them are shorter than that of M5. Meanwhile, com. pared to M5, pure M6 and M7 films not only have larger CLs for their $\pi - \pi$ interaction peaks (28.61 and $1 5 . 9 2 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ for M6 and M7, respectively), but also exhibit longer CLs for the lamellar stacking peaks (97.48 and $8 2 . 2 2 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ for M6 and M7, respectively). These results suggest that the ending group halogenation not only can enhance the molecular ordering but also can decrease the $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance of the resulting nonfullerene acceptor, both of which are beneficial for improving charge transport of the resulting active layer.[42] It should be noted that a shorter $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance will increase the charge transfer integral that represents the electronic wavefunction overlap between the adjacent molecules thereby increasing the charge transport.[43]

# 2.2. Photovoltaic Performance and Charge Dynamics

To assess the photovoltaic properties of the three acceptors, PsCs with a conventional device architecture of $\mathrm { I T O } /$ PEDOT:PSS/active layer/PDIN/Ag were fabricated first. Here, a wide-bandgap polymer of PM6 was selected as the donor polymer and 2,9-bis(3-(dimethylamino)propyl) anthra[2,1,9-def:6,5,10-d'e'f'ldiisoquinoline-1,3,8,10(2H,9H)- tetraone (PDIN) was chosen as the cathode interlayer. The optimal donor:acceptor ratio for the three blends was fixed at 1:1 by weight and the total concentration was $1 6 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ in chloroform with $0 . 5 \%$ $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { v o l \% } } )$ 1-chloronaphthalene as the solvent additive. To optimize the photovoltaic performance, all the three active layers were thermally annealed at $9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The detailed photovoltaic parameters for the device optimization are described in Tables S1-s3, Supporting Information. The best-performance M7-based device delivered a lower $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ due to the lower LUMO level of M7 than that of M5. However, an obviously enhanced $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $2 3 . 0 1 \mathrm { m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and an increased FF of $6 2 . 5 2 \%$ were achieved for the M7-based device, contributing to a significantly enhanced PCE of $1 2 . 8 2 \%$ The enhancements in both the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF values are mainly ascribed to the extended absorption range and enhanced charge transport induced by improved intermolecular interaction. Among devices based on the three nonfullerene acceptors, the highest PCE of $1 4 . 2 8 \%$ was achieved for the PM6:M6-based device, along with a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } ,$ a $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $2 5 . 2 1 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ and a FF of $6 3 . 6 1 \%$ . Furthermore, when the top cathode of the PM6:M6-based device was changed from Ag to Al, the best-performance device exhibited a further enhanced PCE of $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ with an increased FF of $7 0 . 3 0 \%$ a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } ,$ and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $2 4 . 7 0 \ m \mathrm { A \ c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . It is noteworthy that the efficiency of $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ is the best result for single-junction PSCs based on all the A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors with chlorinated ending groups. The enhanced performance with Al as the top electrode could be due to the low work function of PDIN/Al, allowing them to form better ohmic contact with the nonfullerene acceptor. To confirm the accuracy of the measured $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values for the PSCs, external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were then carried out. As shown in Figure 3b, in comparison with the M5-based device, M6- and M7-based devices exhibited broader EQE spectra with higher average EQEs in the range of $3 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ which is consistent with their absorption results (see Figure 1c). It is worth noting that in comparison with the M6-based device, the M7-based PSC showed a slightly decreased average EQE thereby resulting in a lower $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ value. The integrated $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values from the EQE spectra were listed in Table 2, and the values agree well with the $J { - } V$ measurements within $5 \%$ errors.

We note that there is a mismatch in HOmO energy levels between PM6 and M5 (Figure 1d), which can be one of the factors leading to the small $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF values of the device based on PM6:M5. To investigate the exciton dissociation and charge transfer behavior in PM6:M5 blend film as well as PM6:M6 and PM6:M7 blend films, photoluminescence (PL) quenching experiment was carried out. Figure S4a, Supporting Information shows the PL spectra of M5, M6, and M7 neat films and their blend films with PM6. All the three acceptors showed strong PL emissions in the range of $7 5 0 \mathrm { - } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . When blended with PM6, the PL intensities of neat films (excited at $7 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ ) were quenched by $7 3 \%$ for M5, $96 \%$ for M6, and $9 6 \%$ for M7. These results suggest that the hole transfer from M5 to PM6 was less efficient compared to that from M6 or M7 to PM6. Meanwhile, we also examined the possibility of electron transfer from the donor to the acceptors. As shown in Figure S4b, Supporting Information, when blended with the acceptors, the PL intensity of PM6 was obviously quenched in all three blend films, indicating the efficient electron transfer from PM6 to all the three acceptors.

To provide more sufficient driving force for charge transfer, PBDB-T, which possesses an upshifted LUMO energy level of $- 3 . 4 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and HOMO energy level of $- 5 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ in comparison with PM6, was selected as the donor material to pair with the three nonfullerene acceptors. The $J { - } V$ and corresponding EQE curves for the devices are displayed in Figure 3c,d. In comparison with the device based on PM6:M5, PBDB-T:M5- based device afforded an enhanced $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of $1 9 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { m A ~ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ and an increased FF of $5 2 . 0 7 \%$ , leading to a higher PCE of $9 . 2 0 \%$ However, this photovoltaic performance enhancement did not occur in the devices based on the two halogenated acceptors (M6 and M7). As listed in Table 2, the PCEs of PBDB-T:M6- and PBDB-T:M7-based devices are reduced to $1 0 . 6 3 \%$ and $1 0 . 2 4 \%$ , respectively, but they are still higher than that of the PBDB-T:M5-based device. In any case, all the PBDB-T-based PSCs showed decreased $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ values in comparison with the PM6-based devices due to the upshifted HOMO energy level of PBDB-T. These results indicate that it is important to finely tune the energy levels of both the donor and acceptor materials thus to minimize the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ loss.

In order to understand the effect of the ending group halogenation on the charge transport properties of the bulkheterojunction (BHJ) blend films, hole and electron mobilities of the optimized PM6:M5, PM6:M6, and PM6:M7 blends were measured using the space charge limited current (sCLC) model. Hole- and electron-only diodes with device structures of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer/ $\mathrm { { ' M o O } _ { 3 } / A g }$ and $\mathrm { I T O } / \mathrm { Z n O } /$ active layer/Ca/Al, respectively, were fabricated to get the dark current density. The $J { - } V$ characteristics are shown in Figure 4a,b and the charge carrier mobilities, estimated from the $\overset { \cdot } { J } ^ { 1 / 2 } - \overset { \cdot } { V }$ curves, are summarized in Table S5, Supporting Information. Hole mobilities of PM6:M6, and PM6:M7 blends were $6 . 1 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and $4 . 5 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively. However, PM6:M5 blend showed a significantly decreased hole mobility of $1 . 6 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ which is more than 2 order of magnitude lower than those for the blends based on PM6:M6 and PM6:M7. The electron mobility for PM6:M5 is $1 . 0 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ which is also much lower than those of PM6:M7-based $( 5 . 4 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , and PM6:M6-based $( 1 . 4 5 { \times } 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ counterparts. Clearly, the PM6:M6 blend showed the highest and most balanced hole and electron transport $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } = 4 . 2 7 )$ among the three blend films, which could be related to its optimized morphology, improved intermolecular interaction (reduced $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance) and enhanced molecular ordering. The higher and more balanced $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ values are beneficial to suppressing the accumulation and recombination of charges, and therefore the enhanced $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and FF values.

To further understand the photovoltaic performance difference among the three sets of PSCs, the exciton dissociation and charge collection properties of the devices were investigated and estimated from the photocurrent density $\left( J _ { \mathrm { p h } } \right)$ versus the effective voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ plots. The probabilities $( P _ { \mathrm { E , T } } )$ of exciton dissociation and charge collection can be reflected by the ratio of $J _ { \mathrm { p h } } / J _ { \mathrm { s a t } } ,$ where $J _ { \mathrm { s a t } }$ is defined as the saturation photocurrent density.[44] As shown in Figure $4 \mathsf { c }$ under the short-circuit condition, the $P _ { \mathrm { E , T } }$ values were determined to be $9 0 . 1 \%$ $9 7 . 2 \%$ and $9 6 . 5 \%$ for the M5, M6, and M7-based devices, respectively. The devices based on the halogenated acceptors (M6 and M7) showed higher $P _ { \mathrm { E , T } }$ values than that based on M5, indicating that the ending group halogenation could promote the exciton dissociation and charge collection efficiencies. These results are consistent with the higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values in the M6 and M7-based devices.

To get insights into the charge recombination process in the three devices, the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values were measured and plotted against the incident light intensity $( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ (Figure 4d). The relationship between $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } }$ can be described by the power-law equation of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \propto \ P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } ^ { \mathrm { } } \alpha$ . The slopes $( \alpha )$ of linear fitted $\log ( J _ { \mathrm { S C } } )$ versus $\log ( P _ { \mathrm { l i g h t } } )$ plots for the devices based on the three acceptors are 0.898, 0.952, and 0.918, respectively. In principle, $\alpha$ is close to 1 if the bimolecular recombination is suppressed in the device.[45] Therefore, the M6 and M7-based devices, especially for the M6-based device, showed limited bimolecular recombination which contributed to their higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ values.

# 2.3. Morphology

To study the influence of ending group halogenation on the blend morphology, tapping mode atomic force microscopy (AFM) was carried out. The AFM height and phase images are displayed in Figure 5. From the AFM height images in Figure 5a-c, the root-mean-square roughnesses for PM6:M5, PM6:M6, and PM6:M7 blend films are 1.05, 1.27, and $1 . 2 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively. The slightly increased roughness values for the PM6:M6 and PM6:M7 blend films may be correlated with their increased crystallinity, as confirmed by the GIWAXS studies in the following section. As shown in Figure 5d-f, phase separation with a nanofribillar structure is more obvious for the M6-based blend film in comparison with the other two blend films, which is favorable for the efficient exciton dissociation and charge transportation.

Further, the molecular packing behaviors and crystallinities of the three blend films were measured by GIWAXs. The 2D GIWAXS patterns and 1D line cuts of the BHJ films are shown in Figure 6 and the detailed GIWAXS parameters are summarized in Table S4, Supporting Information. As shown in Figure S5, Supporting Information, PM6 showed a (010) peak at $\overset { \cdot } { 1 . 6 6 } \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ $( d _ { \pi } ^ { \mathrm { ~ \bar { ~ } { ~ } ~ } } \approx 3 . 7 8 \mathrm { ~ \bar { ~ } { ~ } ~ }$ along the $q _ { z }$ axis direction with a CL of $1 . 7 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and a (100) peak at $0 . 2 9 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ $( d _ { \mathrm { L } } \approx 2 1 . 6 5 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } )$ in the $q _ { x \gamma }$ axis direction with a CL of $5 . 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , which suggests a dominant face-on orientation for PM6. When the acceptors were blended with PM6, all the three BHJ films still prefer face-on orientations in relation to the substrate with intense (O1o) diffraction peaks that originate from both PM6 and the acceptors in the OOP direction. Incorporation of chlorine or bromine atoms barely changes the location of (1o0) peak in the IP direction but it shortens the $\pi - \pi$ interaction distances of the blend films $( d _ { \pi } \approx 3 . 6 9$ A for $\mathrm { P M } 6 { : } \mathrm { M } 5$ $d _ { \pi } \approx 3 . 5 9 { \mathrm { ~ \AA } }$ for PM6:M6, and $d _ { \pi } \approx 3 . 6 1$ A for $\mathrm { P M } 6 { : } \mathrm { M } 7 )$ . In the OOP direction, the calculated $\pi - \pi$ interaction CLs for PM6:M6 and PM6:M7 blends are 17.04 and $1 6 . 6 8 \mathring \mathrm { \mathrm { A } } .$ respectively, which are slightly longer than that of the PM6:M5 blend (15.41 A). Similar trend was also observed in the CL of lamellar stacking for the three blend films (Table S4, Supporting Information). The results implied that the blended films based on the nonfullerene acceptors with halogenated ending groups show decreased $\pi - \pi$ interaction distances with enhanced molecular ordering which is in agreement with their higher charge carrier mobilities and better device performances.[46]

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, three A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors, namely, M5, M6, and M7, were designed and synthesized by using a ladder-type benzobis(4-H-dithienopyrrole) core in combination with three different ending groups. The influences of ending group halogenation on the optical properties, energy levels, crystallinities, BHJ morphologies, and photovoltaic performance of the resulting nonfullerene acceptors were systematically investigated. In general, the nonfullerene acceptors with halogenated ending groups showed reduced optical bandgaps with downshifted energy levels, reduced $\pi - \pi$ interaction distances with enhanced molecular ordering, thus leading to the improved light-harvesting, charge transport, and photovoltaic performance of the corresponding PsCs. Especially, the acceptor (M6) with chlorinated ending groups exhibited the smallest $\pi - \pi$ interaction distance with the longest CL among the three nonfullerene acceptors. When paired with PM6, the M6-based devices showed a PCE of $1 4 . 2 8 \%$ which is higher than the control devices based on M5 $( 3 . 0 3 \% )$ and M7 $( 1 2 . 8 2 \% )$ . When the Ag electrode of the PM6:M6-based device was replaced with an Al electrode, a further improved PCE of $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ was achieved for the best-performance device. To the best of our knowledge, the $1 5 . 4 5 \%$ efficiency is the highest among all A-D-A type nonfullerene acceptors with chlorinated ending groups. When another wide-bandgap donor polymer of PBDB-T was used, the best-performing M6-based device also showed a higher PCE compared to the control devices based on M5 and M7. These results demonstrate that incorporating halogen atoms on the ending groups of nonfullerene acceptors is effective to improve their photovoltaic performance, and the nonfullerene acceptors with chlorinated ending groups are promising for high-performance PSCs considering their reduced bandgaps, decreased $\pi - \pi$ interaction distances, and enhanced molecular ordering.
