# Recent progress in the development of n-type organic semiconductors for organic field effect transistors

Jesse T. E. Quinn, $\textcircled{1}$ a Jiaxin Zhu, $\textcircled{1}$ a Xu Li,b Jinliang Wangb and Yuning Li $\textcircled{1}$ \*a

This review highlights recent major progress in the development of organic semiconductors as electron transport n-channel materials in organic field effect transistors (OFETs). Three types of materials are discussed: (1) small molecules, (2) polymers, and (3) n-doped small molecules and polymers. Much effort has been made in the modification of known building blocks, development of novel building blocks, and optimization of materials processing and device structures. These efforts have resulted in the achievement of record high electron mobilities for both small molecules $( 1 2 . 6 \ c m ^ { 2 } \lor ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } )$ and polymers $( 1 4 . 9 \mathsf { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , which are approaching the highest hole mobilities achieved by p-type small molecules and polymers so far. In addition, n-doping of ambipolar and p-type organic semiconductors has proven to be an efficient approach to obtaining a greater number of n-type organic semiconductors. However, it is found that n-type organic semiconductors, in general, still lag behind p-type organic semiconductors in terms of carrier mobility and air stability. Further exploration of new building blocks for making novel materials and optimization of processing conditions and device structures are needed to improve the performance, particularly air stability.

# 1. Introduction

Organic field effect transistors (OFETs) or organic thin film transistors (OTFTs) employ organic semiconductors, either small molecules or polymers, as channel layers, which can be more conveniently fabricated at much lower temperature, lower cost, and higher throughput in comparison to the conventional devices using inorganic semiconductors such as silicon.1–3 In addition, organic semiconductors can be made soluble in solvents, enabling mass production of OFET-driven electronics by printing techniques. Organic semiconductors, particularly polymers, are much more mechanically robust and lighter than inorganic semiconductors. These appealing features offered by OFETs have opened up opportunities for the development of next generation electronic products such as flexible displays, ubiquitous radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags, wearable electronics, sensors, etc.4–8 Charge carrier mobility of OFETs is the most important device parameter for many of the target applications of OFETs. Intrinsically organic semiconductors are inferior in terms of charge transport due to the nature of the weak secondary bonds (e.g., the van der Waals force) that hold the molecules together in comparison to inorganic semiconductors where ions and/or atoms are connected by the much stronger and shorter primary bonds such as ionic and covalent bonds.1–3 The weaker intermolecular interaction results in much larger intermolecular distances in organic semiconductors. Furthermore, the more complicated geometry of organic molecules compared with the simple, spherical atoms and ions that constitute the inorganic semiconductors renders most of the organic semiconductors rather disordered. These factors make the charge transport within the organic semiconductors take place dominantly in a hopping mode, which requires much higher energy compared with the band-like charge transport mechanism for inorganic semiconductors. Despite these structural disadvantages, over the past few decades and especially in the past few years, remarkable improvements have been made in enhancing the charge carrier mobility of organic semiconductors, which are enabled by the development of new materials, improved materials processing, and device architecture optimization.3,9–13,14,15 High mobilities of up to ${ \sim } 4 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \bf V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \bf \Phi } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for both single crystal16,17 and aligned18 small molecules and $\sim 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for well-aligned polymers19 have been achieved in OFETs. For solution processed (spin coated) OFET devices, high mobilities exceeding $1 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were reported for several polymer semiconductors.20–23 These mobility values are far better than that of amorphous silicon $\left( 0 . 1 - 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ and have entered the mobility regime of multi-crystalline silicon. However, most of these high mobility values have been achieved for p-type organic semiconductors, which conduct holes in the OFET channel. For many electronic applications, n-channel OFETs using n-type organic semiconductors, which conduct electrons, are required to make complementary metal oxide (CMOS)-like logic circuits together with p-channel OFETs. Unfortunately, much less n-type organic semiconductors have been reported and their mobility values are still lower compared with p-type organic semiconductors.24–29

One key reason for the lack of high performance n-type organic semiconductors is the much smaller number of available electron withdrawing building blocks that can be used for making n-type semiconductors with stable electron transport characteristics. When an electron is injected into the organic semiconductor in an OFET, it is equivalent to a reduction process in an electrochemical reaction. If the organic semiconductor has a rather high LUMO energy (or low electron affinity, EA), the injected electron residing in the LUMO of the semiconductor is at a high-energy state, which is prone to interact or react with some species such as the Si–OH groups on $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ dielectric surfaces and oxygen and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ in ambient air and thus the injected electrons are trapped or annihilated. Therefore, electron transport is weakened or inhibited (Fig. 1). It has been theoretically proven and empirically demonstrated that a LUMO energy of ca. $- 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ or lower is required to realize a stable electron transport performance for an organic semiconductor.29–31 A low LUMO energy would also reduce the electron injection barrier height $\left( \phi _ { \mathrm { e } } \right)$ , which is the difference between the LUMO energy of the organic semiconductor and the Fermi energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { F } } \right)$ of the source electrode (Fig. 1). Al, Ca and Ba, which have low work functions (or high Fermi energies), are favoured for electron injection; however, their thin films are prone to rapid oxidation and thus are difficult to handle. Instead, a more stable conductor, Au, is frequently used as contacts for OFETs, although the work function ( $\sim 5 . 1 \mathrm { e V }$ , which is equivalent to a Fermi energy of $- 5 . 1 \mathrm { e V }$ ) of Au is not favourable for electron injection, but rather more favourable for hole injection for p-channel OFETs. Therefore, large contact resistances are often observed for n-channel OFETs with Au contacts, which is another reason for the observed low electron motilities of many n-type organic semiconductors.

Most of the high mobility p-type and n-type polymer semiconductors reported so far have a D–A type structure, i.e., the repeat unit of these polymers is composed of an electron donor (D) unit and an electron acceptor (A) unit. Polymer main chains with such an alternating D–A arrangement would have a stronger intermolecular interaction, which shortens the $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance and thus promotes charge carrier hopping along the $\pi$ -stacks. Some $\mathbf { D - A }$ polymers with strong electron accepting building blocks show very high electron mobilities; however, they also possess rather high HOMO energies due to the existence of the donor units, which creates insufficient hole injection barrier heights $\left( \phi _ { \mathrm { h } } \right)$ to block hole injections and allows stable hole transport in these semiconductors (Fig. 1). As a result, these polymers exhibit ambipolar (electron and hole) charge transport characteristics, which are not desirable for CMOS-like logic circuit applications because ambipolar OFETs normally have a high off current $\left( I _ { \mathrm { o f f } } \right)$ and a low current on-to-off ratio $\left( I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } } \right)$ .32 Furthermore, the OFET device architecture adopted also has an impact on the observed field transistor performance. Fig. 2 shows the three types of OFET architectures often used, bottom-gate bottom-contact (BGBC), bottom-gate top-contact (BGTC), and top-gate bottom-contact (TGBC). TGBC n-channel OFETs exhibit better air stability and often higher electron mobility because the semiconductor layer is protected by dielectric and gate electrode layers from exposure to oxygen and moisture in ambient air. Lastly, the mobility values obtained by using the MOSFET (metal oxide semiconductor field effect transistor) model may be inaccurate since the mobility of organic semiconductors is often heavily dependent on the gate bias.33–36 If the behaviour of an OFET obeys the MOSFET model, the field effect mobility $( \mu )$ in the saturation regime can be extracted using eqn (1):

$$
I _ { \mathrm { D } } = \frac { \mu C _ { \mathrm { d i e l } } W } { 2 L } \left( V _ { \mathrm { G S } } - V _ { \mathrm { t h } } \right) ^ { 2 }
$$

where $I _ { \mathbf { D } }$ is the drain current, $W$ and $L$ are the channel width and length, $C _ { \mathrm { d i e l } }$ is the capacitance per unit area of the dielectric, $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ is the gate voltage, and $V _ { \mathrm { t h } }$ is the threshold voltage. $\big | I _ { \mathrm { D } } \big | ^ { 1 / 2 }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ would follow a linear relationship. However, many OFETs show a nonlinear $\big | I _ { \mathrm { D } } \big | ^ { 1 / 2 } { - } V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ plot with an increasing slope with $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ (Fig. 3A), resulting in an increase of the mobility against the gate voltage with the mobility in the higher $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region significantly higher than that in the lower $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region. $^ { 1 , 2 9 , 3 7 , 3 8 }$ The cause of this nonlinearity has been accounted for by the existence of charge carrier traps residing at the disordered molecular packing sites and structural defects in the semiconductor, in the dielectric layer close to the surface, and at the interface of the semiconductor and the dielectric, evidenced by the large $\lvert V _ { \mathrm { t h } } \rvert$ . Recently, several studies reported ultra-high mobility OFETs that showed a kinked $\big | I _ { \mathrm { D } } \big | ^ { 1 / 2 } { - } V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curve (Fig. 3B). These devices showed a much faster increase in the drain current $\left( I _ { \mathrm { D } } \right)$ over a very small gate voltage range just above the threshold $\left( V _ { \mathrm { t h } } \right)$ compared to the high $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region, or so-called an ‘‘abrupt turn-on’’.16,34–36 A recent study on the non-ideal behaviour of single crystal rubrene OFETs revealed that the contact resistance $\left( R _ { \mathrm { c } } \right)$ drops abruptly in the small $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region just above the $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } ,$ , which resulted in a higher mobility in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region than in the high $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region extracted using the MOSFET model by more than one order of magnitude. The mobility obtained from the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region was considered to be ‘‘overestimated’’ by some researchers.35,36 The root cause of this ‘‘kinked’’ non-linear transfer curves is still not very clear. Takeya et al. explained this non-ideal behaviour observed for the single crystal rubrene OFETs by the charge carrier trapping and scattering at the semiconductor and dielectric interface.16 Specifically, the mobility at a lower $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ represents that of the in-crystal carriers, while the mobility at a higher $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ becomes lower because more charge carriers are confined to the less ordered semiconductor/dielectric interface, where charge carrier trapping and scattering occur. Recently, Pei et al.23 found that the non-ideal characteristic of a high mobility n-type polymer is correlated with the microstructures of the polymer films, i.e., higher crystallinity films having larger crystallite domains and better stacking order led to more frequent occurrence of non-ideal kinked transfer curves. Nonetheless, overly inaccurate mobility values, which are either overestimated or underestimated from the non-ideal OFET behavior, may provide erroneous structure– property relationships of organic semiconductors and mislead the chemists when designing organic semiconductors.36 Therefore, caution should be applied when correlating the reported carrier mobilities with the chemical and nano-/microscale structures of the materials. In addition to a high charge carrier mobility, a high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ ratio, a small $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } ,$ and a small subthreshold swing are other desirable OFET parameters, which are critically required for many applications of OFETs.24

This article provides a review on the major advancement in the development of new n-type organic semiconductors, including small molecules and polymers, with high electron mobilities of about $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ (the lower limit for amorphous silicon) or higher, in the past five years. Some relevant materials reported earlier are also included for the convenience of discussion. Through this review, we aim to provide new insight into the relationship between the structure (types of acceptor building blocks, crystallinity, morphology, etc.) and properties (the LUMO and HOMO energies, air stability, as well charge carrier mobilities, etc.) of n-type organic semiconductors. We hope that the newly acquired knowledge and findings summarized in this review will benefit chemists and materials scientists in this field for the development of better organic semiconductors for OFETs and other applications.

# 2. Small molecule n-type organic semiconductors

# 2.1. Rylene diimide-based derivatives

Naphthalene tetracarboxylic diimides (NDIs) are among the first small molecule n-type organic semiconductors that demonstrated great potential for OFETs (Fig. 4 and Table 1).39–41 For example, the single crystal $N \cdot$ -cyclohexyl substituted NDI, S1, exhibited a high electron mobility of $7 . 5 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . 42 However, the OFET devices with $N \cdot$ -alkyl substituted NDIs degrade readily under ambient conditions due to their inadequately low LUMO energies to prevent the detrimental reactions with moisture and oxygen. Introduction of fluorinated side chains was found to be able to achieve a denser molecular packing to prevent the diffusion of oxygen and moisture43 as well as to lower the LUMO energy from $- 3 . 6 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the alkylated NDI to $- 3 . 7 1$ to $- 3 . 8 2 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ for the semifluoroalkylated NDIs.44 Improved air stability of the OFETs with these semifluoroalkylated NDIs was observed. $N { - } p$ -(Trifluoromethoxy) benzyl $\left( \mathbf { B O C F } _ { 3 } \right)$ ) substituted NDI, S2 (NDI$\mathbf { B O C F } _ { 3 } ]$ , also showed improved air stability in OFETs, but the electron mobilities reported previously were quite low $\left( \sim 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ .45,46 Recently, Zhang et al. revisited this compound and found that S2 has a surprisingly low LUMO energy of $- 4 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { \ e V } ,$ , which was accounted for by the strong electron accepting effect of the $\mathrm { B O C F } _ { 3 }$ side chains. OFETs with this compound showed an electron mobility of $0 . 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ in ambient air.47 Recent endeavors have been made to obtain air-stable NDIs by incorporating electronwithdrawing groups such as cyano and halogen groups into the naphthalene core in order to lower their LUMO energy levels. $^ { 3 9 , 4 7 , 4 8 }$ Lee et al. demonstrated that chlorination of the NDI core with two or four electron-withdrawing chlorine atoms could enhance the air stability of the resulting materials.49 The dichloro and tetrachloro NDIs showed LUMO energies of $- 4 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 4 . 1 3 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively, which are much lower than that of the non-chlorinated NDI compound $\left( - 3 . 7 2 \ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ . The dichlorinated S3a $\left( \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 } { \cdot } \mathrm { N D I } \right)$ with $N \cdot$ -fluoroalkyl substitution showed the highest electron mobility of $0 . 5 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for OFETs processed by solution-shearing deposition and $0 . 8 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for OFETs processed by vacuum deposition. He et al. achieved electron mobilities as high as $8 . 6 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $\sim 1 0 ^ { 7 }$ for the single crystal S3a under ambient conditions.50 The molecules were packed in a face-to-edge herringbone structure with a very short $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance of $0 . 3 2 7 { \mathrm { n m } }$ . It was found that the $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { H } { \cdot } \cdot \cdot \mathrm { O }$ interactions existed, which might facilitate electron tunneling between the $\pi$ -stacks. The mobility decreased only by ${ \bf 1 3 \% }$ after the device was kept in ambient air for 82 days (Fig. 5). Yuan et al. recently performed a systematic study on core-fluorinated NDIs.48 The di- or tetrafluorinated NDIs have slightly lower LUMO energies, which are in a range between $- 3 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 3 . 9 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ depending on the side chains and the number of F, than that of the nonsubstituted one $\left( - 3 . 6 3 \mathrm { e V } \right)$ . However, they exhibited lower mobilities than their core-chlorinated NDI counterparts. The best mobility of $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained for the difluoro core-substituted NDI S3b.

Fusion of $\pi$ -conjugated moieties to the NDI core (coreexpansion) is another effective approach to lowering the LUMO energy of NDIs.52,54,81 The dithiazole-fused NDI, S4 (NDITz),51 has a LUMO energy of $- 3 . 9 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , about $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ lower than that of the non-core fused NDIs. Electron mobilities of up to $0 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and a high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $\sim 1 0 ^ { 7 }$ were obtained under ambient conditions. Gao et al. fused two 2-(1,3-dithiol-2- ylidene)malonitrile groups to the NDI core by a simple nucleophilic aromatic substitution reaction.52 The resulting core-expanded NDI (S5a) exhibited a LUMO energy of $- 4 . 3 \mathrm { e V }$ , which is much lower than that of the unsubstituted NDI and warrants the air stability of S5a in n-channel OTFTs. Under ambient conditions electron mobilities as high as $0 . 5 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } } { - 1 0 } ^ { 7 }$ were achieved for the spin coated S5a thin films (annealed at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ ) in BGTC devices with Ag contacts. By moving the branching point of the sidechains one $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 }$ farther from the NDI core, the mobility of the resulting S5b was greatly improved to $3 . 5 0 ~ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in the BGBC device architecture with Au contacts.53 The position of the branching point was found to cause critical changes in molecular packing and thus the electron mobility. The high mobility of S5b was attributed to its dense in-plane molecular packing and large crystal domains $\sim 1 \ \mu \mathrm { m } \times$ $3 \ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ . The devices showed comparable performance in ambient air and nitrogen, indicating the good air stability of this compound. The same group developed a mild and versatile one-pot synthetic route to a variety of core-expanded naphthalene diimides with symmetric and unsymmetrical substituents.54 One unsymmetrically substituted product, S5c, achieved good electron mobility of $0 . 7 0 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $1 0 ^ { 7 }$ under ambient conditions. More recently, an NDI-based dimer bridged by a (1,3-dithiol-2-ylidene)acetonitrile moiety (S6) was synthesized. The as-spun S6 thin films showed electron mobilities as high as $0 . 4 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with a high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of up to $\sim { 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ .55 Fukutomi et al. incorporated two thiophenes into the NDI core forming naphthodithiophenediimides (NDTIs).56 The $N \cdot$ -octyl substituted NDITI, S7a (C8-NDTI), has a lower LUMO energy of $- 4 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ than that of its NDI counterpart $\left( - 3 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ due to the more extended $\pi \cdot$ -conjugation enabled by the additional two thiophene moieties. Vacuum deposited S7a films exhibited electron mobilities as high as $0 . 0 5 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } }$ under ambient conditions. By adding chloro substituents at the $\propto$ -positions of the thiophene moieties, the new derivative S7b has a further lower LUMO energy of $- 4 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ .57 The two chloro atoms enabled Cl  O–C contacts, which played a role in the formation of a two-dimensional (2D) brick layer structure. In contrast, S7a without chloro substitution formed a herringbonelike one-dimensional (1D) electronic structure. The 2D-like electronic structure of S7b helped charge transport, achieving a much higher electron mobility of $0 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ for the vacuum deposited films. Fan et al. reported an $N \cdot$ -fluoroalkyl NDTI, S8, which has very low LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 1 6 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 6 . 3 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively.58 The molecular packing of a single crystal S8 belongs to the $P 2 _ { 1 } / c$ space group with a short $\pi - \pi$ distance of $3 . 4 0 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ . OFETs with single crystal S8 achieved high electron mobilities of up to $1 . 5 9 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $1 0 ^ { 4 }$ in BGTC devices in ambient air. Lately, Wang et al. fused two naphthalene imide units to NDI, forming S9 (NDINI), which has very low LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 2 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 6 . 6 8 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively.59 Spin coated S9 films showed a rather low electron mobility of ${ 7 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } } \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ due to the poor film quality (poor morphology). However, single-crystalline microfibers exhibited electron mobility as high as $1 . 7 5 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ in OFETs measured in nitrogen. When measured in air, the mobility was only slightly lower (up to $1 . 5 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ .

Similar to NDIs, perylene diimides (PDIs) are also a class of initially well-studied n-type semiconductor materials.40,41 They have particularly received increasing interest as very promising non-fullerene acceptors for low-cost OPVs.82,83 For the OFET application, a noteworthy perylene derivative, diperylene bisimide S10 (C12-4CldiPBI), was reported by Wang et al.60 S10 has deep LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 2 2$ eV and $- 6 . 0 4$ eV, respectively. An ‘‘Au stripe mask’’ technique was used to fabricate OFETs with small single crystal ribbons of S10. Very high electron mobilities of up to $4 . 6 5 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ were achieved under ambient conditions. The devices showed no obvious degradation after being kept in air for 50 days.

Substitution of the imide oxygen for sulfur (thionation) has recently been reported to be a simple and efficient way to lower the LUMO energy and promote the electron transport of $\mathrm { N D I } ^ { 6 1 , 8 4 }$ and $\mathrm { P D I } ^ { 6 2 }$ derivatives. Kozycz et al. found that as the degree of thionation increases the LUMO energy decreases gradually from $- 3 . 7 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the non-thionated NDI to $- 4 . 3 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ for the terthionated NDI, S11d. 61 On the other hand, the HOMO energies of the thionated NDIs became higher at ca. $^ { - 6 . 4 }$ to $- 6 . 5 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ than that $\left( - 6 . 9 7 \ : \mathrm { e V } \right)$ of the non-thionated NDI, leading to smaller band gaps of the thionated NDIs. These band energy changes are considered due to the involvement of the large sulphur atoms in the core conjugation. All the thionated NDIs showed dramatic increases in electron mobilities to $\sim 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ c m ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ (the best mobility of $7 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for S11b) with respect to electron mobilities of $\sim 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for the non-thionated NDI compound. Similar trends were observed for the thionated PDI compounds S12s.62 The LUMO energy decreases with the degree of thionation from $- 3 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the parent PDI compound to $- 4 . 1 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for the tetrathionated PDI S12d. Among this thionated PDIs, S12d showed the best n-type semiconductor performance with the highest mobility of $0 . 1 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ in OFETs owing to the combined effects of its lowest LUMO energy, favoured crystal structure, and improved grain boundaries.

A bithiophene linked by an alkylimide group at the $^ { 3 , 3 ^ { \prime } }$ -positions, $N \cdot$ -alkyl- $^ { \cdot 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -bithiophene- $^ { 3 , 3 ^ { \prime } }$ -dicarboximide unit (BTI), was used by Marks et al.85 as an electron deficient building block to make air-stable n-type, ambipolar, and p-type polymers in OFETs with mobilities on the order of $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Guo et al. very recently reported a series of well-defined oligomers with 2 to 5 fused BTI units.63 These ladder-type BTI derivatives showed promising electron-transporting characteristics with electron mobilities of $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in TGBC OFETs. The largest molecule in this series, S13 (BTI5), has a LUMO energy of $- 3 . 5 5 \ \mathrm { ~ e V }$ and exhibited the highest electron mobility of $0 . 0 4 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ . Higher mobility may be achieved if the bulky 2-octyldodecyl side chains are replaced with smaller side chains to improve the molecular packing.

# 2.2. N-Heteroacenes

N-Heteroacenes, where some of the carbon atoms of acenes are replaced with nitrogen atoms that render these compounds more electron deficient, represent a class of promising n-type semiconductors for OFETs.86 Among them, the triphenodioxazine (TPDO) structure has been of particular interest because this five-ringed structure resembles pentacene that is one of the mostly studied small molecule p-type semiconductors. Early in 2008 Di et al. reported a trifluoromethyl-substituted TPDO S14a $\left( \mathbf { C F } _ { 3 } – \mathbf { T P D O } \right)$ .64 The electron withdrawing TPDO core and $\mathrm { C F } _ { 3 }$ substituents at the para-positions to the nitrogen atoms helped lowering the LUMO energy to $- 3 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ . OFET devices with vacuum deposited S14a films showed good air stability and an electron mobility of $\sim 0 . 0 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with a high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ . S14b with $\mathrm { C F } _ { 3 }$ substituents at the para-positions to the oxygen atoms has an identical LUMO energy to that of S14a, but showed lower mobilities around $0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Both S14a and S14b showed good air stability with minimal changes in electron mobility after 30 days. The LUMO energy $\left( - 3 . 6 7 \ : \mathrm { e V } \right)$ of these compounds seems insufficient for achieving air stable electron transport. It was suggested that the close crystal packing enabled by the introduction of $\mathrm { C F } _ { 3 }$ substituents could prevent the diffusion of oxygen, which accounted for the good air stability of these compounds. Triisopropylsilyl (TIPS) has been used for the substitution of pentacene to improve the solubility and obtain favourable solid state packing of the resulting TIPS-pentacene (Fig. 6), which is a high performing p-type semiconductor.87 Nicolas et al. reported TIPS-TPDO,88 S15a, which is a direct analogue of TIPS-pentacene. S15a showed n-type semiconductor performance. However, the electron mobilities of the S15a based OFETs are quite low on the order of $\sim 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ . The same research group later synthesized a series of TIPS-TPDOs with two or four CN substituents on the TPDO core.65 Products S15b and S15c, which have two CN substituents, have LUMO energies of $- 3 . 7 9 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ and $- 3 . 8 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, measured by cyclic voltammetry (CV). S15d (TIPS-TPDO-4CN) with four CN substituents has the lowest LUMO energy of $- 4 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ . Among these compounds, S15d achieved the best performance with average electron mobilities of 0.014 and $0 . 1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (with a maximum of $0 . 1 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ for the spin coated and vacuum evaporated films in OFETs using $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ contacts under ambient conditions. The devices retained $8 0 \%$ of the initial mobility after 30 days of storage in air, demonstrating the excellent air stability of this material. More recently, the same group used S15d as the semiconductor and cross-linked poly(methyl methacrylate) (c-PMMA) as the dielectric to fabricate OFETs on polyethylene terephthalate substrates.89 The devices demonstrated a maximum electron mobility of $1 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ and an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $1 0 ^ { 5 }$ , which are promising for flexible applications.

Wang et al. reported a large 9-ring N-heteroacene, S16 (OANQ).66 The single-crystal structure of S16 reveals a slightly twisted motif, which may play a role in enhancing the stability by reducing the strain generated from the $\pi$ interactions and steric effects of the side chains. The 2D ‘‘brick layer’’ face-toface stacking structure and the strong $\pi - \pi$ interactions are considered favourable for large transfer integrals and 2D electron transport. A very low LUMO energy of $- 4 . 3 2 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ was measured. Single crystals of S16 were tested in OFETs with an Ag source and drain contacts. The highest electron mobility of $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of up to ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } }$ was obtained under ambient air. Devices showed no obvious degradation in performance after storage for one month under ambient conditions, demonstrating excellent air stability of this material.

# 2.3. Azulene derivatives

Azulene, a resonance-stabilized aromatic isomer of naphthalene, consists of a seven-membered ring and a five-membered ring, which displays a large dipole moment of $1 . 0 8 \mathrm { ~ D ~ }$ (Fig. 7).69,90 Application of azulene in construction of organic optoelectronic materials was recently reviewed by Gao91 and Zhang et al.92 and OFET characteristics of two azulene-based derivatives $^ { 5 , 5 ^ { \prime } }$ -di(2-azulenyl)- $^ { \cdot 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -bithiophene S17a (DAzBT) and 2,5-di(2- azulenyl)-thieno[3,2-b]thiophene S17b (DAzTT).67 These compounds showed p-type semiconductor performance with hole mobilities of $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ and $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } } { - 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ in OFETs. No reduction peaks were observed in the CV diagrams of these compounds and therefore the LUMO energies were not reported in this paper. However, based on the reported HOMO energies and optical band gaps, the LUMO energies of S17a and S17b can be estimated to be $- 3 . 6 9 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ and $- 3 . 7 1 \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively. An extension of this work was reported by the same group in the following year.68 Three azulene units were connected linearly in the same direction to form $2 , 6 ^ { \prime } { : } 2 ^ { \prime } , 6 ^ { \prime \prime } { : }$ terazulene S18a (TAz1). S18a has a narrow $E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { \mathrm { o p t } }$ of $1 . 6 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ with a HOMO energy of $- 5 . 5 6 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and a low LUMO level at $- 3 . 9 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ . Bottom-gate top-contact (Au or Al) OFETs with $6 0 \ \mathrm { \ n m }$ -thick vacuum deposited S18a thin films on octadecyltrichlorosilane (ODTS)-treated $\mathrm { S i } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ substrates at different substrate temperatures (room temperature, $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , and $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ were fabricated. All devices showed unipolar n-channel performance. A maximum electron mobility of $0 . 2 9 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ and an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $1 0 ^ { 7 }$ were achieved at a substrate temperature of $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . It is interesting to note that S18a as a hydrocarbon molecule showed electron transport, not hole transport, performance. The authors conducted DFT calculations on this molecule and found that the LUMO is quite evenly distributed, while the HOMO is localized at one end of the molecule. Therefore, for two molecules packed with an antiparallel orientation, the LUMOs can overlap, but HOMOs cannot. This explains the observed electron-only charge transport performance of this molecule. The single crystal XRD data confirmed the coexistence of both parallel and antiparallel orientations at a 1 : 1 ratio. Very recently, Yamaguchi et al. thoroughly studied a series of terazulenes, S18b, S18c, and S18d, which are regioisomers of S18a. 69 The LUMO/HOMO energies decrease in the order of S18b, S18c, and S18d with values of $- 3 . 8 3 ~ \mathrm { e V } / - 5 . 4 5 ~ \mathrm { e V } .$ , $- 3 . 9 0 ~ \mathrm { e V } / - 5 . 4 7 ~ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , and $- 3 . 9 3 \ \mathrm { e V } / - 5 . 5 9 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively. S18b showed unipolar p-type transistor performance for all devices with Au contacts, achieving the highest hole mobility of $1 . 3 2 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . When the low work function Al contacts were used, hole transport only performance was observed for the devices fabricated on substrates heated below $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , while ambipolar charge transport was observed for devices fabricated at a substrate temperature of $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . S18c showed unipolar electron charge transport at most annealing temperatures with either Au or Al contacts except for the ones with films deposited at room temperature and Au as contacts, which showed ambipolar performance. The highest electron mobility of $0 . 3 1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ for S18c was obtained for the device with the film deposited at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and Al as contacts. S18d showed unipolar n-type semiconductor performance in all devices with an electron mobility of up to $0 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Based on the device data, it seems that the 2,2-biazulene unit (as in S18b and S18c) accounted for the p-type characteristics. This was explained through the molecular orbital distribution supported by the B3LYP/6-31G calculations. S19 (BAzDI-2), an azulene-based aromatic diimide, which comprises a $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -biazulene moiety and two seven-membered imide groups, has a LUMO energy level of $- 3 . 7 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and showed a moderate electron mobility of $1 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 ^ { 4 } } { - 1 0 ^ { 5 } }$ in BGTC devices using Au contacts in nitrogen.70 Judging the relatively high LUMO energy of this compound, the devices are probably not stable in air, although it was not mentioned in the paper. Recently, Gao et al. reported an ethynylazulene end-capped NDI S20, 71 which showed a LUMO energy of $- 3 . 9 9 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ that is similar to those of the unsubstituted NDIs. The highest electron mobility of $0 . 1 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } } { - 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ were achieved under nitrogen conditions in BGTC devices.

# 2.4. Quinoidal oligothiophenes

Dicyanomethylene-terminated quinoidal oligothiophenes (QOTs) are a class of well-studied n-type semiconductors.93–95 However, the electron mobilities of QOTs have been much lower than $1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , a bottleneck that has been attributed to the smaller $\pi$ -surface of QOTs, which negatively affects the transfer integral magnitude that relates to the charge transport in organic semiconductors. $^ { 7 3 } \mathrm { ~ I n ~ } 2 0 1 1$ , Zhu et al.72 synthesized a solution processable dicyanomethylene-substituted fused tetrathienoquinoid, S21 (Fig. 8), which enhances the intermolecular interactions and eliminates the problem of cis/trans-isomerism that can occur in their QOT counterparts. S21 has a deep LUMO energy of $- 4 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ . BGTC OFET devices were all fabricated and tested under ambient conditions. Unipolar electron mobilities as high as $0 . 9 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } }$ were obtained. A device with an original electron mobility of $\sim 0 . 3 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ still showed a mobility of $0 . 1 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ after storage in ambient air for 87 days.

In 2014 Zhang et al. expanded the $\pi$ -surface of linear QOTs by using thieno[3,4-b]thiophene and 5-alkyl-4H-thieno[3,4- c]pyrrole- $4 , 6 ( 5 H )$ -dione moieties to replace thiophenes.73 The obtained 2D $\pi$ -expanded QOTs with distal (S22a, 2DQTT- $^ o$ ) and proximal (S23a, 2DQTT-i) regiochemistry (Fig. 8) showed very small $E _ { \mathrm { g } } ^ { \mathrm { o p t } }$ of 1.11 and $1 . 1 6 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively. The HOMO energies are $- 5 . 9 3$ and $- 5 . 6 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and the LUMO energies are very low at $- 4 . 5 1$ and $- 4 . 7 7 \ \mathrm { { ~ e V } }$ for S22a and S23a, respectively. S22a underwent a dramatic redshift in its $\lambda _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ by $2 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ from solution to solid state, suggesting the formation of J-aggregates, which is favored for electron transport. On the other hand, S23a showed only a broader absorption profile with almost no change in its $\lambda _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ from solution to solid state. BGBC OFETs fabricated on octadecyltrichlorosilane (ODTS)-modified $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ substrates with a $3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ gold source and drain contacts were used to evaluate the semiconductors. The spin-coated semiconductor films $( \sim 4 0 { - } 6 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ were annealed at 80, 120, 160, 190, or $2 4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Devices were tested under ambient conditions and all devices showed n-type semiconductor performance. Devices based on S23a showed a maximum mobility of $0 . 4 4 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ { 1 0 } ^ { 3 } }$ at an annealing temperature of $1 9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . S22a based devices displayed the highest electron mobility of $3 . 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ as high as ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ for the films annealed at $1 6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . The overall greater electron mobility observed for these 2D $\pi$ -expanded QOTs compared to the linear QOTs was ascribed to the $\mathrm { o - } s$ and/or S–S intramolecular interactions and the favourable ‘‘brick-layer’’ arrangement in the solid state due to the expanded $\pi$ -surface and steric hindrance that resulted in an exclusive $^ { E , E }$ -configuration. The better performance of S22a than that of S23a was attributed to the formation of J-aggregates, higher crystallinity (verified by XRD), as well as larger grains (visualized by AFM) of the former. The results indicated that the orientation of the thieno[3,4- b]thiophene units has a huge impact on the molecular packing motif and electron transport. It should be noted that the $I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 1 / 2 }  – V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curve of the S22a based devices showed a ‘‘kinked’’ nonlinear pattern and the mobilities were extracted from the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region. OFET devices based on S22a and S23a showed good air stability owing to their very deep LUMO energies.

Later, Zhang et al. reported another series of 2DQTT semiconductors, where the 2-ethylhexyl side chains on the central 4H-thieno[3,4-c]pyrrole- $4 , 6 ( 5 H )$ -dione moiety in S22a and S23a were replaced with either branched 2-octyldodecyl (S22b and S23b) or linear dodecyl (S22c and S23c).74 Despite their different side chains, S22b and S23c have the same HOMO and LUMO energies of -5.59 and $- 4 . 4 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and S23b and S23c have the same HOMO and LUMO levels of $- 5 . 5 7$ and $- 4 . 5 4 \ \mathrm { \ e V } ,$ , respectively. While S23b displayed the lowest electron mobility of $\sim 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ , the linear alkyl substituted S22c and S23c showed much higher electron mobilities of 0.36 and $0 . 4 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. A surprisingly high electron mobility of $5 . 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for S22b, which was attributed to its co-facial molecular packing forming a 2D brickwork motif that promotes $\pi$ -overlap and 2D charge transport. In contrast, S22c formed a herringbone motif with edge-to-face orientation (orthogonal) packing that can only promote 1D charge transport. Similar to S22a, the $I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 1 / 2 }  – V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves for S22b and S22c are also kinked and the mobilities reported were the highest values in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region. It is rather interesting to note that all compounds with the distal regiochemistry (2DQTT-o) showed the kinked $I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 1 / 2 }  – V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves while the compounds with the proximal (2DQTT-i) regiochemistry showed $I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 1 / 2 }  – V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves that fit the MOSFET model better. Further investigation into the influence of the regiochemistry of these series of compounds on the nonideal OFET behaviour may be warranted.

A furan–thiophene quinoidal compound, S24 (TFT-CN), was recently reported by Li et al.75 It was found that TFT-CN existed as a cis–cis structure in the solid state, which is different from the all thiophene analogue (TTT-CN) that was previously reported to have a trans–trans structure in crystals.96 S24 has LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 1 5 \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 7 2 \mathrm { ~ } \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively, which are only very slightly higher than those of TTT-CN $( - 4 . 2 2 ~ \mathrm { \ e V } / - 5 . 7 4 ~ \mathrm { \ e V } )$ . The spin coated S24 films annealed at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ exhibited remarkably high electron mobilities of up to $1 . 1 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , while the corresponding TTT-CN based transistors showed much lower mobilities with a maximum value of $0 . 0 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { \Lambda } } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The significant improvement in the mobility by incorporating a furan unit into S24 was explained by the desirable molecular packing with the $\pi - \pi$ stacking direction parallel to and the $c$ -axis of the unit cell perpendicular to the substrate surface. In contrast, the TTT-CN films were composed of two phase structures. When microribbons of S24 were used as the channel semiconductor to minimize the grain boundaries, electron mobilities as high as $7 . 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were achieved.

# 2.5. Isoindigo derivatives

The building blocks based on isoindigo and its derivatives are electron-deficient, which have recently been extensively studied for constructing high mobility n-type materials. In $2 0 1 5 \mathrm { X u }$ et al. reported two isoindigo derivative S25a and S25b (Fig. 9) based on the fusion of benzothiophene or benzofuran and isoindigo.76 The LUMO energies of S25a and S25b are determined by CV measurements to be $- 3 . 7 2 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 3 . 7 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively. Under ambient conditions the spin coated S25a films exhibited n-channel dominant ambipolar charge transport performance with electron and hole mobilities of up to $0 . 0 1 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ and $5 . 7 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \ s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, in OFETs. On the other hand, S25b showed unipolar n-type semiconductor performance with higher electron mobilities of up to $0 . 0 7 4 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The better performance observed for S25b was explained by the higher crystallinity and better morphology (with less and smaller grain boundaries) than S25a.

Upon further increasing the fluorine substituents to six, the resulting S26e has even lower LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 1 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 6 . 4 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively.77 However, the electron mobilities of this compound dropped with the highest value of $4 . 6 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . # 3. Polymeric n-type semiconductors

# 3.1. Rylene diimide-based polymers

Among various n-type rylene diimide-based polymers reported in the past few years, NDI-based copolymers have been the most studied for various applications including OFETs and OPVs.41,101 The best known n-type polymer, poly{ $[ N , N ^ { \prime }$ -bis(2- octyldodecyl)-naphthalene-1,4,5,8-bis(dicarboximide)-2,6-diyl]- alt- $^ { 5 , 5 ^ { \prime } }$ - $^ { 2 , 2 ^ { \prime } }$ -bithiophene)} (P1a, P(NDI2OD-T2), in Fig. 11), also known as N2200, was first reported by Watson et al.102 in 2008 and was later studied for n-channel OTFTs by Fachetti, Watson, Jenekhe, et al.31,103–105 P1a has been the most extensively investigated n-type polymer for OFETs and all-polymer solar cells to date.106–110 P1a has a low LUMO energy of ca. $- 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , which is sufficient for achieving stable electron transport. P1a showed n-type semiconductor performance with high electron mobilities of up to $0 . 8 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in TGBC devices and $\sim 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in BGTC devices when measured under ambient conditions (Table 2).31 TGBC devices showed almost no change in performance after being stored in air for up to 30 weeks, while the BGTC devices were less stable. Several strategies have been adopted aiming to improve the performance of this polymer in OFETs. Among them, side chain engineering is proven to be a very effective approach. Lee et al.111 synthesized a series of NDI-bithiophene-based copolymers (P(NDIR-T2)) with different alkyl side chains, 2-hexyldecyl (2-HD) (P1b), 2-octyldodecyl (2-OD) (P1a), and 2-decyltetradecyl (2-DT) (P1c), which showed notable differences in their morphology, crystalline structures, and ultimately charge transport performance. They found that the electron mobility of these polymers in BGTC OFETs increases with shortening of the alkyl side chains. The highest mobility of $1 . 9 0 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for P1b, which is much higher than those of P1a $\left( 1 . 2 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ and P1c $\left( 0 . 3 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ . The improved mobility of P1b was explained by the shorter intermolecular $\pi - \pi$ distance of this polymer compared with the other two, which facilitated the interchain charge hopping.

The hybrid siloxane terminated side chains were used for p-type isoindigo polymers and an enhancement of the hole mobility was observed.144 Kim et al. incorporated the hybrid siloxane-terminated hexyl chain $\left( \mathrm { S i C _ { 6 } } \right)$ into NDI to form P(NDI2SiC6-T2) (P1d).112 P1d showed much better mobility of $1 . 0 4 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ than that of the benchmark P1a $\left( 0 . 3 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 } \right)$ in OFETs fabricated and tested under the same conditions. The XRD results revealed that in P1d the polymer chains formed quite balanced face-on and edge-on orientations rather than one preferred orientation. In addition, P1d exhibited much stronger $\pi - \pi$ stacking interactions evidenced by its stronger (010) diffraction peak. These factors might have contributed to its enhanced charge transport performance compared to P1a.

Kang et al. synthesized another NDI-T2 polymer, P1e, with semifluoroalkyl $\left( - ( \mathbf { C H } _ { 2 } ) _ { 5 } ( \mathbf { C F } _ { 2 } ) _ { 7 } \mathbf { C F } _ { 3 } \right)$ side chains to replace hydrocarbon side chains.113 BGTC OFETs using spin coated polymer films with chloroform as a solvent exhibited a high electron mobility of $3 . 9 3 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Devices showed no obvious degradation in performance after exposure to air for three months. With the addition of a higher boiling point additive, 1-chloronaphthalene, to the solvent, the obtained polymer films showed even high electron mobilities of up to $6 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , owing to the improved chain packing. Twodimensional grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (2D GIXD) data (Fig. 12) indicated that the P1e film showed intense (00l) reflections along the in-plane direction, while P1a with branched alkyl side chains showed no corresponding reflections. It was believed that the introduction of the semifluoroalkyl side chains into P1e can make the polymer backbone more rigid and strengthen the self-organization of the side chains themselves, forming a superstructure composed of ‘‘backbone crystals’’ and ‘‘side-chain crystals’’. The rigid backbone organization was considered to be mainly responsible for the enhanced charge transport of P1e. The AFM images of these two polymers also showed distinct differences. The P1a film had small irregular grains, while the P1e film was consisted of much larger, elongated grains with better connectivity (shown in Fig. 12).113 However, this polymer showed slight hole transport behaviour with hole mobilities two orders of magnitude lower than the electron mobilities.

Efforts have also been made to alter the bithiophene donor unit in NDI-T2 to thiophene–vinylene–thiophene (TVT) since the use of TVT has met successes in other types of polymers. TVT has a highly coplanar structure, which can facilitate the polymer main chain packing and reduce the $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance.145 An NDI–TVT polymer with 2-decyltetradecyl side chains, P2a, was reported by Kim et al.114 Incorporation of TVT raised the HOMO energy to $- 5 . 4 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , but the LUMO energy remained similar at $- 4 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ compared to the NDI–T2 polymer, P1a. As a result, ambipolar charge transport was observed for P2a in OFETs with Au as contacts. When $\mathrm { C s } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 }$ was used to modify the Au contact surface to reduce the work function, unipolar electron transport was achieved due to the increased hole injection barrier. A high electron mobility of up to

$1 . 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained. The improved charge transport was attributed to the improved $\pi - \pi$ intermolecular interaction. The devices also showed good air and bias stress stabilities. Side chain engineering was also conducted on NDI–TVT polymers. With the hybrid siloxane terminated hexyl $\left( \mathrm { S i C _ { 6 } } \right)$ side chains, P2b showed the highest electron mobility of $0 . 9 3 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ ,112 which is better than that of P1a, but lower than that of its corresponding NDI–TVT polymer P2a. Kang et al. prepared an NDI–TVT polymer P2c with semifluoroalkyl $\left( - ( \mathbf { C H } _ { 2 } ) _ { 5 } ( \mathbf { C F } _ { 2 } ) _ { 7 } \mathbf { C F } _ { 3 } \right)$ hexyl side chains on NDI to make P2c. 113 P2c showed good solubility in some common organic solvents such as chloroform and chlorobenzene. As with P1e, it was also found that these side chains improved the packing of the rigid polymer backbone evidenced by the 2D-GIXD and AFM data (Fig. 12). Very high electron mobility of up to $5 . 6 4 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained in TGBC devices with polymer films processed using chloroform as the solvent with the aid of a solvent additive, 1-chloronaphthalene. The transistor performance was almost unchanged in ambient air after three months.

Selenophene–vinylene–selenophene (SVS) has been used as a donor unit and produced some of the best performing p-type polymer semiconductors such as a DPP–SVS polymer, which demonstrated an ultrahigh mobility of $\sim 1 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \thinspace s } ^ { - 1 }$ . 21 Very recently, SVS was used as the donor unit to form an NDI–SVS polymer, P3. 115 The HOMO energy increased to $- 5 . 2 9 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , while the LUMO energy remained similar at $- 3 . 9 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ in comparison to P2a. High electron mobilities of up to $2 . 4 ~ { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { \Lambda } } { \mathrm { s } } ^ { - 1 }$ were achieved in TGBC OFETs with P3 films spin coated and annealed at $2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , better than that of its NDI–TVT counterpart P2a ( $\left\lfloor . 8 \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { s } } ^ { - 1 } \right\rfloor$ . However, P3 also showed hole transport performance due to its high HOMO energy, which facilitates hole injection and transport. By introducing a thin $\mathrm { C s } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 }$ layer between the Au source/drain and the semiconductor layer, unipolar n-type OFET performance with an electron mobility of up to $0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ was realized for the as-cast polymer films.

Recently, Zhao et al.116 used the comonomer blocks, selenophene–benzothiadiazole–selenophene and thiophene– benzothiadiazole–thiophene, to make a series of NDI polymers,

P4a–d (Fig. 11). The incorporation of the weak acceptor benzothiadiazole aimed to fine-tune the electronic structure and thus the charge transport properties of the polymer. P4a and P4b with selenophene as the donor units exhibited much higher electron mobilities than P4c and P4d that have thiophene donors, which was explained by the much better film forming ability of P4a and P4b due to the presence of selenophene. The highest electron mobility of $8 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ among these compounds was achieved for P4a in a TGBC device. P4a and P4c, which have LUMO/HOMO energies of $- 3 . 7 7 \ \mathrm { e V } / - 5 . 8 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 3 . 8 1 ~ \mathrm { e V } / - 5 . 9 0 ~ \mathrm { e V } ,$ showed ambipolar charge transport performance. By incorporating two fluoro substituents into benzothiadiazole, the resulting P4b and P4d showed slightly lower LUMO energies of $- 3 . 8 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $3 . 8 8 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ as well as much lower HOMO energies of $- 6 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 6 . 2 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively. The fluoro substitution on the benzothiadiazole seems to have mainly contributed to the lowering of the HOMO energies, which would build up higher energy barriers for hole injection. As a result, these two polymers showed unipolar n-type semiconductor performance with maximum electron mobilities of 3.5 and $2 . 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, which are lower than their corresponding non-fluoro counterparts, P4a and P4c. The lower solubility and thus poor film quality of P4b and P4d might have contributed to their poor performance. The TGBC devices of P4a and P4c showed excellent stability in air. After 100 days in air, the devices showed negligible changes in performance. In the BGBC devices, all the polymers showed much lower performance with electron mobilities in the range of $0 . 1 5 { - } 0 . 2 9 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ s ^ { - 1 }$ when tested in a $\mathbf { N } _ { 2 }$ -filled glove box. Significant decreases in hole and electron mobilities were observed after the devices were exposed to ambient air for two hours. The same group reported a similar polymer, P4e, which contains a selenophene–benzooxadiazole– selenophene.117 This polymer has a lower LUMO energy of $- 3 . 9 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ together with a low HOMO energy of $- 5 . 9 0 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , which enabled unipolar electron-transport characteristics of this polymer in OFETs. The TGBC devices showed no obvious decay in performance after being stored in air for 70 days. The air stability of this polymer in bottom gate devices was not reported.

A core-extended NDI moiety has also been incorporated into the polymer backbone of P5, which has low HOMO and LUMO energies of $- 5 . 8 2 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 4 . 2 5 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively.118 At an optimized temperature of $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , an electron mobility of $0 . 3 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with a high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ was achieved in BGBC devices.

Another rylene diimide building block, PDI, has also gained considerable attention as it has demonstrated gradual improvement in performance in both OFETs and OPVs.146 The initially reported PDI-based polymer, P6a, exhibited modest unipolar electron mobilities of up to $1 . 3 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { - 2 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ .119,120 Marked improvements were later reported with the inclusion of a phenothiazine comonomer, P6b, with electron mobilities approaching $0 . 0 5 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $1 0 ^ { 5 }$ for devices measured in nitrogen.121 By introducing acetylene linkages, the resulting polymer P6c has a lower LUMO energy and a higher HOMO energy than those of P6a without the acetylene linkages, due to the more coplanar structure and thus more extended conjugation of the former.122 Increased electron mobility of up to $0 . 0 7 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ with an $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ was achieved. More importantly, due to the lowered LUMO energy of P6c, the devices showed good stability in ambient air with $4 5 \%$ retention of the mobility after being stored in air for 6 days.

A tetraazabenzodifluoranthene diimide based polymer, P7, reported by Jenekhe et al. has two-dimensional $\pi$ -conjugation in parallel and normal to the main chain.123 XRD analysis revealed that the polymer chains in the drop cast films adopted a lamellar packing motif with a very short $\pi - \pi$ distance of $0 . 3 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which would facilitate the charge transport in the third dimension (the $\pi$ -stacking direction). High electron mobilities of up to $0 . 3 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were achieved in BGTC devices with Ag contacts in nitrogen for the films annealed at $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The LUMO energy of this polymer, $- 3 . 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , seems insufficient for achieving air stability of the operating devices with this polymer.

# 3.2. DPP polymers

Diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP) is a strong electron acceptor building block and some DPP based polymers represent the best performing polymer semiconductors for OFETs,11,21 although the high mobility values obtained for some DPP polymers were thought to be overestimated36 because their $\big | I _ { \mathrm { D } } \big | ^ { 1 / 2 } { - } V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves exhibited nonlinear kinks (as illustrated in Fig. 3B). A DPP compound prepared by the common synthetic routes is accompanied by two flanking aromatic units. Thiophene is the mostly used flanking unit to DPP because of its small steric effect that can keep the DPP2T (or DBT) structure highly coplanar, a feature that is desirable for achieving high carrier mobility. However, the electron donating effect of the flanking thiophene units raises the HOMO energy. Therefore, the polymers with the DBT backbone, e.g., $\mathbf { P 8 a } ^ { 1 2 4 }$ and $\mathbf { P 8 b } ^ { 1 2 5 }$ (Fig. 13), have a rather high HOMO energy of $- 5 . 4 \mathrm { e V } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ , although their LUMO energy of $- 3 . 7 \mathrm { e V }$ is sufficiently low for stable electron transport. Thus, P8a and P8b exhibited ambipolar charge transport performance with quite balanced electron and hole mobilities on the order of $1 0 ^ { - 2 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in BGTC or BGBC devices when Au was used as the source and drain contacts. When a TGBC structure with unsymmetrical contacts, Al as the source and Au as the drain, was used, unipolar n-type semiconductor performance with high electron mobilities of up to $3 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was obtained for P8b.125 This is mainly due to the low work function of Al, which would block the hole injection and facilitate the electron injection. The fluorine polymer Cytop used as a dielectric material might also be helpful for reducing the electron traps compared to the $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ dielectric used in the bottom gate devices where the residue of Si–OH groups might serve as electron traps. Combining DPP2T with an electron acceptor comonomer benzothiadiazole unit led to a copolymer P9, 126 which showed a lower LUMO energy of $- 4 . 0 \mathrm { e V }$ but a higher HOMO energy of $- 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ compared to P8. This polymer showed ambipolar charge transport with high and balanced electron and hole mobilities of up to $0 . 4 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 3 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. The large fused benzothiadiazole spacer might strengthen the $\pi - \pi$ interaction and increase the $\pi - \pi$ overlapping, which are beneficial for the interchain charge transport along the $\pi$ -stacks.

In order to promote the electron transport, Hong et al. used an electron deficient azine $( \mathbf { C } { = } \mathbf { N } { \mathbf { - } } \mathbf { N } { = } \mathbf { C } )$ unit to link DPP2T units through a convenient catalyst-free Schiff base type polymerization reaction.127 The formed polymer P10 (PDBTAZ) showed a very low LUMO energy of $- 4 . 2 4 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ and a slightly lower HOMO energy of $- 5 . 3 4 \ \mathrm { e V }$ compared to most DPP2Tdonor type copolymers. P10 showed good electron transport properties with the highest electron mobility of $0 . 4 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . However, similar hole mobilities of up to $0 . 3 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were also observed. Since Au was used as the contact material, the HOMO energy of this polymer would be inadequately low to block the hole injection.

Another electron withdrawing comonomer unit, dicyacypenylene, was used by Xiong et al.130 to form a polymer P11. This polymer has a very low HOMO energy of $- 5 . 6 3 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and yet a relatively low LUMO energy of $- 3 . 7 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ . The OFET devices showed good electron transport performance with electron mobilities of up to $0 . 1 8 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The lowered HOMO energy could build a larger hole injection barrier; however, weak hole transport (with a hole mobility of ${ \bf 8 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } c m ^ { 2 } V ^ { - 1 } } { \bf s } ^ { - 1 } )$ was still observed.

Reichmanis et al. used the electron deficient bithiazole as the comonomer unit to DPP2T.131 The polymer P12a showed the highest electron mobility of $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in OFETs, where low work function Ca and polyethylenimine ethoxylated (PEIE)-modified Ag were used as contact electrodes in BGTC and TGBC devices, respectively, in order to facilitate the electron injection. The use of these low work function contacts also had an effect on the blocking of hole injection, thus realizing the observed unipolar n-type semiconductor performance of this polymer. A similar polymer P12b reported by Guo et al.132 showed n-type-dominant ambipolar charge transport performance in TGBC OFETs with the high work function Au as contacts. P12b showed better electron mobilities of up to $0 . 5 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ along with hole mobilities of up to $4 . 8 \ \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The improved electron mobility of P12b compared to P12a might be due to their different monomers and polymerization methods, direct arylation for P12b and Stille coupling for P12a, which led to different types and amounts of structural defects in these two polymers. With a replacement of the flanking thiophenes to the DPP unit with thiazoles, polymer P13 exhibited unipolar electron mobilities as high as $0 . 0 6 7 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ with modest $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of ${ 1 0 ^ { 4 } } { - 1 0 ^ { 5 } }$ in BGBC devices with Au contacts when measured in nitrogen.133 The electron affinity (EA) of this polymer was estimated to be between $3 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $4 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , using the ionization potential $( \mathrm { I P } = 5 . 7 1 $ eV) measured by UPS and the band gap $( 1 . 3 4 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ measured by UV-Vis. The high IP or low HOMO energy of this polymer would block the hole injection. To evaluate air stability, devices were stored in ambient air $2 5 \mathrm { ~ \ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $5 0 – 6 0 \%$ RH) and characterized periodically in nitrogen by removing the absorbed air in a vacuum before the measurement. Devices showed no appreciable degradation in performance after 4 months, indicating the good air stability of the devices owing to the high $\mathrm { I P / E A }$ values of this polymer.

Sun et al. replaced the electron rich thiophene units in DPP2T with the electron deficient 2-pyridine units.134 The connection of DPP to the 2-positions of the pyridine units is crucial to maintain the high coplanarity of the DPP2Py (or DBPy) building block. The copolymer of DPP2Py and bithiophene, P14, has LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 3 3 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 6 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, which are much lower than those of the corresponding polymer using DPP2T, PDQT.147 P14 showed a very high electron mobility of $6 . 3 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in TGBC OFETs. The very high electron mobility achieved by P14 was mainly accounted for by the extremely high crystallinity, which was verified by the intense X-ray diffraction peaks as well as the large crystalline domains shown in the AFM image of the spin coated polymer film (Fig. 14). While the very low LUMO energy of P14 is very beneficial for the electron injection and transport, the HOMO energy of $- 5 . 6 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ seems insufficient to block the hole injection when Au is used as the contact material, which has a work function of $\mathrm { \sim } 5 . 1 \mathrm { e V }$ . Therefore, high hole mobilities of up to $2 . 7 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were also observed. Another copolymer of DPP2Py with thieno[3.2-b]thiophene, P15,135 showed similar LUMO and HOMO energies to those of P14. With pure Au contacts, the TGBC OFETs with P15 showed high electron and hole mobilities of $3 . 3 6 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $2 . 0 4 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \nabla \mathrm { V } } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. To block the hole injection, a thin layer of polyethylenimine (PEI) was used to modify the Au contact surface, which led to a reduction of the work function of the electrode down to $3 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ . With PEI modified Au contacts, unipolar n-type semiconductor performance was realized. A high electron mobility of up to $2 . 3 8 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved. Surface modification of the contact electrodes by PEI was also proven to be a simple and versatile approach to converting ambipolar OFETs to unipolar n-channel OFETs for several other ambipolar polymers.128

# 3.3. Isoindigo derivative based polymers

Isoindigo (IID) is an electron acceptor building block and the D–A polymers based on IID have achieved high hole mobilities in OFETs.148 However, the electron affinity of IID is insufficient to bring down the LUMO energy low enough to achieve stable electron transport characteristics. Huang et al. replaced the benzene ring in IID with the more electron-withdrawing pyridine to form 7,7-diazaisoindigo (AIID).136 A copolymer of AIID and bithiophene, P16 (Fig. 15), possesses LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 3 . 6 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, determined by the CV measurements. In BGBC OFET devices, unipolar p-type semiconductor performance with a maximum hole mobility of $7 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved. On the other hand, in the TGBC devices, ambipolar charge transport with maximum hole and electron mobilities of 2.33 and $0 . 7 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, was achieved. The observation of electron transport behavior in the TGBC devices is due to the encapsulation effect of the dielectric and gate electrode layers above the polymer semiconductor, which could prevent the exposure of the semiconductor to the oxygen and moisture in the ambient environment. These results again demonstrated that a polymer with a LUMO energy above ca. $- 3 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ cannot achieve electron transport in ambient air.

Recently, McCulloch et al. reported a copolymer of AIID and selenophene (a weaker electron donor than thiophene), P17, which has a rather low LUMO energy of $- 4 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ .137 This low LUMO energy facilitated electron injection and enabled electron transport. Electron mobilities as high as $0 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were obtained in TGBC OFETs. However, the HOMO energy was at $- 5 . 6 \mathrm { e V }$ , which seemed not low enough to suppress hole injection and transport and thus this polymer showed ambipolar charge transport performance although the electron transport was more favored. When the electron accepting benzothiadiazole was used as a comonomer unit, the resulting polymer P18 exhibited both lower LUMO and HOMO energies at $- 4 . 1 \ \mathrm { { \ e V } }$ and $- 5 . 8 \ \mathrm { \ e V } ,$ respectively. Unipolar n-type semiconductor performance was observed thanks to the reduced HOMO energy of this polymer. High electron mobilities of up to $1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ were achieved. DFT simulations showed that the AIID–selenophene or AIID– benzothiazole linkage is quite coplanar with a small dihedral angle of $5 ^ { \circ }$ due to the less sterically demanding nitrogen atoms at the 7-positions of AIID, in sharp contrast to the isoindigo analogues, which are much more twisted. This might explain the high carrier mobility achieved by P17 and P18.

As aforementioned, IBDF is an excellent electron acceptor building block for small molecule n-type semiconductors with high electron mobility. The first IBDF containing polymer, P19 (Fig. 16) that consists of an IBDF and a thiophene in its repeat unit, was reported by Yan et al.97 This polymer has a very low LUMO energy of $- 4 . 4 3 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ and also a relatively low HOMO energy of $- 5 . 7 9 \mathrm { ~ \ e V } .$ . Unipolar n-type semiconductor performance was observed when the BGBC OFET devices were encapsulated with PMMA. The highest electron mobility is $5 . 4 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { - 3 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Interestingly the devices showed enhanced electron mobility of up to ${ 8 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \ { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } \ { \mathrm { V } } ^ { - 1 } \ { \mathrm { s } } ^ { - 1 } }$ and hole transport characteristics with mobility of up to $9 . 0 ~ \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 3 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ when the semiconductor layer was exposed to ambient air. The enhanced electron mobility and appearance of hole transport behavior were explained by doping of the semiconductor layer by oxygen and/or moisture in the air. The synthesis of an IBDF–bithiophene copolymer, P20a, was also reported in the same paper. However, this polymer was found to be insoluble in any solvents due to the strong intermolecular interactions. Later, Lei et al.138 used a much large branched alkyl substituent containing 40 carbon atoms on the IBDF unit and the resulting polymer, P20b, became readily soluble. Unipolar n-type semiconductor performance was observed in both TGBC and BGBC OFETs. The highest electron mobility of $1 . 7 4 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved for TGBC devices when the devices were fabricated in nitrogen. The BGBC devices showed electron mobilities of up to $0 . 1 3 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ , significantly lower than those of the TGBC devices. Similar to the phenomenon observed by Yan et al.,97 the devices fabricated in ambient air showed ambipolar charge transport performance. He et al. reported another IBDF–BT copolymer, P20c, using the newly developed ester-type side chains, which is much easier to synthesize than the C40 side chain precursor in P20b. 139 In BGBC OFETs, quite balanced electron and hole mobilities of $0 . 3 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, were obtained. The electron mobilities are similar to those of its alkyl side chain counterpart, P20b, in the same BGBC device structure.

To improve the electron affinity of the IBDF core, Zheng et al. introduced four fluorine substituents.23 The resulting polymer P21 has lower LUMO and HOMO energies of $- 4 . 3 2$ and $- 5 . 9 6 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ than those of P20b $( - 4 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $- 5 . 7 2 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ ). The TGBC OFET devices with P21 exhibited typical $\mathbf { n }$ -type semiconductor performance. A record high electron mobility of $1 4 . 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved in the TGBC OFETs. The devices based on P21 showed good air stability with only a $4 0 \%$ drop in mobility (from $1 0 . 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to $6 . 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ after 30 days of exposure to air, much better than the devices based on P20b, which showed a $78 \%$ drop in mobility (from $0 . 9 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 2 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ .

However, kinked $I _ { \mathrm { D } } ^ { 1 / 2 } { - } V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves were observed for the P21-based OFETs with high mobilities (Fig. 17). While a high mobility of $1 4 . 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was calculated in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region, a much lower mobility of $\mathbf { 1 . 2 4 \ c m ^ { 2 } \ V ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } }$ in the high $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region was obtained using the MOSFET model (eqn (1)). As mentioned earlier, the non-ideal kinked transfer curves might be accounted for by an abrupt drop in the gated contact resistance in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region.34,35 For P21, the authors ruled out the solvent to be the cause of the non-linear behaviour because similar trends were observed for the films deposited by using either $^ o$ -dichlorobenzene (ODCB) or 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane (TCE).23 Since a fluorine polymer Cytop, which is known to have few electron traps, was used as the dielectric, dielectric trapping is also unlikely to be the cause of the non-linear behaviour. The authors found that the percentage of devices with non-ideal transfer curves decreases with the spin coating speed used for fabricating the polymer films. At a low spin speed of $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ , all devices showed non-ideal transfer characteristics, while at a high spin speed of $2 5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ , only $1 9 \%$ of the devices showed non-ideal behavior (Fig. 18). Through 2DGIXD analysis, it was found that the crystallinity or the coherence length in the lamellar stacking of the polymer films increases with decreasing spin speed. Therefore, it seems that larger crystallite domains and better polymer stacking order have led to the nonideal transfer characteristics of the OFETs. Previously, we used different routes to synthesize a series of p-type DPP-based polymers (PDQT with 2-octyldodecyl (C20) side chains), which contained varied amounts of structural defects (homo-coupled dimer units).149 We also observed serious deviations of the transfer characteristics from the ideal MOSFET model for the OFETs with high mobilities. Since all the devices adopted the same device structure and were fabricated under the same conditions, the difference in the non-linear behavior between the high mobility and low mobility devices highly likely originated from their semiconductor layers with different polymers. Similar to the observation by Pei et al., we found that the non-ideal behavior became more apparent with increasing crystallinity (or chain packing order) of the polymer films. On the other hand, the number of structural defects, molecular weight, and thin film morphology do not have obvious correlations with the appearance of non-ideal transfer characteristics. Pei’s results and ours might support the explanation by Takeya et al.16 that the mobility at a lower $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ represents the in-crystal charge carriers, while the reduction in mobility at a higher $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ is contributed by the less ordered semiconductor/dielectric interface, where charge carrier trapping and scattering occur. If that is the case, the mobility extracted in the lower $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region of the transfer curve may reflect the highly ordered, crystalline domains of the semiconductor film, rather than being ‘‘overestimated’’. Hence, instead of saying that the contact resistance reduced abruptly in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region, it might be more appropriate to say that the contact resistance increased abruptly in the high $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region, which might be due to the participation of the more disordered fractions of the polymer chains in the charge transport as the $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ increases. The transfer characteristics severely deviated from the ideal MOSFET model may be problematic or not useful for practical applications. However, the mobilities extracted in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region using the ideal model may still be helpful for the understanding of the structure–charge transport relationship, particularly the potential/intrinsic charge transport properties of the organic semiconductor, to design materials with enhanced charge transport performance. Further studies are needed to clarify the root cause of the ‘‘kinked’’ non-linear transfer characteristics and address this issue. For instance, the approach of decreasing crystallinity used by Pei et al. to eliminate the non-ideal transfer behaviour comes along with a significant reduction of carrier mobility. If the opposite approach of increasing crystallinity of the polymer films works, it would shed more light on the cause of the non-ideal transfer characteristics and would be more beneficial for improving the OFET performance.

# 4. n-Doped organic semiconductors

Doping is a well-known process to tailor the electronic properties of a semiconductor by intentionally adding a small amount of foreign substance (impurity). Controlled doping is the foundation of the modern electronic industry where $\mathrm { p - }$ and n-type silicon semiconductors are made and used for MOSFET based logic circuits.151 The pioneering work on doping of $\pi$ -conjugated polymers by Heeger, MacDiarmid, and Shirakawa led to the discovery of highly electrically conductive polymers.152–155 In the presence of an oxidant or acid, electron rich (p-type) polymer semiconductors such as polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, and polythiophene can be $\mathrm { p }$ -doped to show a high electrical conductivity of up to $\sim 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathrm { S \ c m } ^ { - 1 }$ . On the other hand, electron deficient conjugated polymers can be n-doped with a reducing agent or base to increase electrical conductivity. In recent years, research into controlled doping of organic semiconductors with a small amount of dopants has intensified156–159 since many doped organic semiconductors show much improved performances in OLEDs, OPV, OTFTs,160,161 and thermoelectrics (TEs).162 Molecular doping of an organic semiconductor may affect the electronic properties through three mechanisms, (A) charge transfer,156,159 (B) charge carrier trapping,163,164 and (C) charge carrier scattering,163,164 depending on the relative frontier LUMO and HOMO energies between the organic semiconductor and the dopant compound (Fig. 19). n-Doping is discussed below and the mechanisms apply to p-doping similarly, but in an opposite manner. In scenario (A) shown in Fig. 19, when the HOMO of the dopant is higher than the LUMO of the organic semiconductor, electrons readily transfer from the HOMO of the dopant to the LUMO of the organic semiconductor. As a result, the electrical conductivity increases accompanied by a redshift of the absorption spectrum of the doped organic semiconductor. In scenario (B), when the HOMO of the dopant is lower than the LUMO of the organic semiconductor, but higher than the HOMO of the organic semiconductor, electron transfer from the dopant to the organic semiconductor is energetically prohibited; however, the mobile holes (injected from the source electrode in an OFET) may be trapped at the energetically more favoured HOMO of the dopant in comparison to the HOMO of the organic semiconductor. The HOMO energy difference $( \Delta E )$ between the dopant and the organic semiconductor is termed as the trap depth or trap energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { T } } \right)$ .165,166 When $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ is $< c a . \ 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { \ e V } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ , the dopant forms shallow hole traps,166–168 where holes can easily enter and can also exit with a small amount of energy. The shallow traps would slow down the hole transport or cause a decrease in hole mobility.166 When $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ is $> c a . \ 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , the dopant forms deep hole traps, where holes can readily enter, but cannot exit or require a very high energy to exit. As a result, hole transport may be completely suppressed due to a dramatic decrease in the number of mobile holes. Some recent studies by the time-of-flight (TOF) measurement show that the presence of deep traps does not significantly reduce the mobility of charge carriers that are not trapped.166,168,169 In scenario (C), when the HOMO of the organic semiconductor is higher than the HOMO of the dopant, but lower than the LUMO of the dopant, neither electron transfer from the organic semiconductor to the dopant nor hole trapping at the dopant sites would occur. In this case, the low-lying HOMO of the dopant would form an energy barrier and cause scattering of mobile holes. Similar to the shallow and deep traps, low and high scatters are divided by the HOMO energy difference between the dopant and the organic semiconductor (or the so-called height of the scatters) with a value smaller or greater than ca. 0.2 eV, respectively.168 It was found that scatters influence the carrier mobility in a similar manner to shallow and deep traps, i.e., low scatters reduce the carrier mobility, while high scatters do not significantly affect the carrier mobility.168 In the cases of (B and C), since no charge transfer occurs between the semiconductor and the dopant, there would be no changes in the UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra and no increase in the electrical conductivity of the doped organic semiconductor if no charge carriers are injected into the doped organic semiconductor. Theoretical and experimental evidence shows that doping in scenarios (A) or (B) causes a shift of the Fermi energy of the semiconductor: a decrease for p-doping and a lift for n-doping.159 A decreased Fermi energy or electron density of the semiconductor via p-doping would help fill the hole traps, while an increased Fermi energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { F } } \right)$ or electron density via n-doping would help fill the electron traps. In addition, if the same electrode material is used, an increased or decreased $E _ { \mathrm { F } }$ would also be beneficial for the electron or hole injection, respectively. Therefore, it is often observed that hole transport is enhanced for p-doped organic semiconductors, while in contrast electron transport is enhanced for n-doped organic semiconductors.

![](images/064419bfacdb752e2fdd5494d78b2527eb717f4ced4a7cb052913dc35e14c93e.jpg)  
Fig. 19 The energy level scheme of three doping mechanisms of an organic semiconductor (OSC) depending on the relative energy levels of the OSC and the dopant. (A) Electron transfer occurs when the HOMO energy of the dopant (or OSC) is higher than the LUMO energy of the OSC (or dopant), leading to n-doping (or p-doping) or an OSC; (B) for n-doping, trapping of the injected holes occurs when the HOMO energy of the dopant is lower than the LUMO energy of the OSC, but higher than the HOMO energy of the OSC, leading to the suppression of hole transport and enhancement of electron transport. Likewise, trapping of injected electrons occurs for p-doping. The HOMO or LUMO energy difference between the dopant and the OSC is termed as trap energy $( E _ { \top } )$ , where deep traps form when $E _ { \top } > c a$ . $0 . 2 \mathsf { e V }$ , while shallow traps form when $E _ { \top } < c a$ . $0 . 2 \ : \mathrm { e V } .$ . (C) Scattering of injected holes (or electrons) occurs when the HOMO (or LUMO) energy of the dopant is lower (or higher) than that of the OSC, which either decreases or enhances the hole (or electron) transport. The Fermi energy $( E _ { \mathsf { F } } )$ , which is positioned at the middle of the band gap of a pristine OSC, may increase or decrease with the addition of an electron-rich or -deficient dopant, which affects the charge injection and transport for the doped OSC in (B) and (C).

Compared to p-doping, n-doping is less studied because of the unavailability of stable n-dopants with high HOMO energy and organic semiconductors with very low LUMO energy. Nonetheless, n-doping of an organic semiconductor has a number of benefits for OTFT application. First, the electron transport can be improved by the increased electron density through trap filling. Second, the hole transport can be suppressed, which can convert an ambipolar and even a p-type organic semiconductor into an n-type semiconductor. Therefore, a great number of n-type organic semiconductors can potentially be obtained by n-doping known materials. In particular, a large number of $\mathbf { D - A }$ polymers have shown very high charge carrier mobilities. However, due to their rather high HOMO energy and low LUMO energy contributed mainly by the donor and acceptor units, respectively, many D–A polymers have shown ambipolar charge transport performance. Although the use of ambipolar polymers as single component semiconductors can greatly simplify the fabrication of CMOS-like complementary circuits, the devices suffer from high off currents and low $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ ratios, which results in high power consumption.32 Therefore, converting ambipolar polymers into unipolar n-type or p-type polymers by doping to fabricate unipolar n- or p-channel OFETs with improved $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ ratios would open up new applications for ambipolar polymers. Third, n-doping (as well p-doping) can readily tune the threshold voltage $\left( V _ { \mathrm { t h } } \right)$ of an OTFT by controlling the filling of electron traps with an appropriate amount of dopant. A precise control of the threshold voltage of transistors is very important for fabricating integrated logic circuits.

In 2010 Bao et al. synthesized and used stable (4-(1,3- dimethyl-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzoimidazol-2-yl)phenyl)dimethylamine (N-DMBI) (Fig. 20) as an n-dopant to [6,6]-phenyl $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 1 }$ butyric acid methyl ester $\mathbf { \left( P C _ { 6 1 } B M \right) }$ , which is an n-type semiconductor.170 The electron mobility of doped $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ markedly increased to $1 . 2 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \ s } ^ { - 1 }$ from $1 . 6 8 ~ \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ of undoped $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ in OTFTs. It was also concentration, indicating that mobile electron traps were filled by the DMBI molecules. The devices with doped $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ demonstrated much improved air stability, which was attributed to the compensatory effect of the dopant on the ${ \bf O } _ { 2 }$ electron traps. However, the off current increased significantly once the dopant concentration was greater than $2 \ \mathrm { m o l \% }$ , resulting in a smaller $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ ratio. The UV-Vis-NIR spectrum of the N-DMBI doped $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ showed a new broad peak in the $8 0 0 { - } 1 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ region, which suggested that strong doping, which might have involved charge transfer, indeed occurred. However, the n-doping effect of N-DMBI on $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ could not be interpreted by the energy levels shown in Fig. 19 since the HOMO of N-DMBI is $- 4 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { \ e V } ,$ , which is too low to allow electron transfer to the LUMO of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ $\left( - 4 . 3 0 \ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ as shown in scenario (A) and there should be no electron transfer in scenario (B). The authors initially proposed that the neutral radical formed at N-DMBI upon removal of hydrogen would allow the electron transfer.170 Later on they further studied the doping mechanism of N-DMBI and several of its analogues and found that a reaction between the dopant and the host semiconductor occurred with either hydride or hydrogen atom transfer, forming host radical anions, which are taken into account for the doping effect.171 Interestingly, the typical p-type semiconductor, TIPS-pentacene, could be n-doped with 2-(2-methoxyphenyl)-1,3-dimethyl-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzoimidazole $\scriptstyle \mathbf { \partial } \cdot \mathbf { \partial }$ -MeO-DMBI), which is an analogue of N-DMBI.172 Pristine TIPSpentacene showed p-channel performance with a hole mobility of $0 . 2 1 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ in OFETs fabricated by solution shearing, while TIPS-pentacene : $o$ -MeO-DMBI $\left( 1 : 1 \right)$ blend films exhibited electron transport dominant ambipolar transistor performance with a very high electron mobility of $6 . 8 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and a rather low hole mobility of $3 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Unlike the n-doping of fullerene derivatives, where OTFTs displayed reduced $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ at higher dopant concentrations, the OFETs of the TIPS-pentacene: $o \mathrm { \cdot }$ -MeO-DMBI blend showed very high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $\sim { 1 0 } ^ { 5 } – { 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ (and low off current) even with an equivalent amount of dopant. The electrical conductivity of the organic semiconductors doped through electron transfer increases since the charge carrier density increases after doping, which generally results in an undesirable reduction in the $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of OFETs. Therefore, this characteristic of the TIPS-pentacene:o-MeO-DMBI blend is particularly useful for OTFTs. The UV-Vis-NIR and photothermal deflection spectroscopies showed no evidence for the formation of radical anions, suggesting that the doping of TIPS-pentacene by $o$ -MeO-DMBI might have a different mechanism from that of the fullerene-DMBI systems. Very recently, the same group used this compound to n-dope a high mobility p-type donor–acceptor polymer poly(tetrathienacene-diketopyrrolopyrrole) (PTDPPTFT4) to induce a strong memory behavior in OFETs. 173 Hole trap density was found to increase with dopant concentration, showing a strong suppression effect of this compound on hole transport. Again, no differences in the UV-Vis-NIR spectra between the pristine and the doped polymer films were observed. The authors proposed that (1) electron transfer may partially take place with surface defects, which allowed electron transport, and (2) holes may also transfer from the HOMO of the semiconductor to and be trapped at the dopant sites. $o$ -MeO-DMBI has a HOMO energy of $- 4 . 6 7$ eV, while TIPS-pentacene174 and PTDPPTFT4173 have HOMO/LUMO energies of $- 5 . 4 ~ \mathrm { \ e V } / - 3 . 5 3 ~ \mathrm { \ e V }$ and $- 5 . 3$ eV/ $- 3 . 9 5 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively. Therefore, we believe the trapping mechanism illustrated in Fig. 19B may apply to these systems, where $o$ -MeO-DMBI would form deep hole traps $( E _ { \mathrm { T } } > - 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ .

Recently, our group used an amine-containing polymer, polyethylenimine (PEI), as an n-dopant for ambipolar (such as P10 and P14) and p-type polymers.129 Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements showed that the Fermi energies of the doped polymers increased by $0 . 0 4 \substack { - 0 . 2 1 } \ \mathrm { e V }$ compared to the corresponding pristine polymers, indicating an increase in the electron density. Most of the doped polymers showed improved electron mobilities at a lower dopant concentration of ${ < } 2 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ , which could be explained by the filling of mobile electron traps. On the other hand, all the polymers showed a disappearance of hole transport at a dopant concentration of $2 \mathrm { w t } \%$ or higher. For example, $2 \%$ PEI doped P10 showed no hole transport performance, while the electron mobility significantly improved from $\sim 0 . 4 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to as high as $0 . 8 8 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (Fig. 21). This is a very convenient approach to obtaining n-type polymers by simply doping ambipolar and even p-type polymers with PEI. The suppression of hole transport was explained by the increased Fermi energy, which increased the hole injection barrier, and the strong interaction of the injected holes with the electron lone pairs of the nitrogen atoms in PEI, which slowed the movement of the holes. The UV-Vis-NIR spectra of the PEI-doped polymers displayed no noticeable changes compared to their corresponding pristine polymers, indicating that charge transfer between PEI and the polymer semiconductor is unlikely. Similar to the TIPS-pentacene:o-MeO-DMBI blend reported by Bao et al., 172 the PEI-doped polymers showed very low off current (the electrical conductivity of the blend remains as low as that of the pristine polymers) and high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } } ,$ which are very different from many other n-doped semiconductor systems that involve charge transfer and show decreased $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ due to their increased conductivity.

We also used several small molecule aromatic amine compounds (Fig. 22), which are known organic semiconductors, as n-dopants for the ambipolar polymer P10 in order to suppress hole transport and transform this polymer into a unipolar n-type semiconductor.175 The rationale is that if the HOMO energy of a dopant is sufficiently higher than that of the host semiconductor to create an $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ greater than ca. $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , the holes in the channel may be trapped at the HOMO of the dopant (see Fig. 19B). The common feature of these aromatic amine compounds is that they have higher HOMO energies than that of the host polymer semiconductor $\left( - 5 . 6 3 \mathrm { e V } \right)$ . The $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ values of these amines against the polymer are in the range between $0 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $0 . 6 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ . It was found that when the $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ of an aromatic amine is $\geq 0 . 2 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , a complete suppression of hole transport of the polymer and only electron transport behaviour were observed (Fig. 23). As observed for the PEI-doped blends, the OTFTs with P10 doped by these small amines showed a low off current and high $I _ { \mathrm { o n } } / I _ { \mathrm { o f f } }$ of $\sim { 1 0 } ^ { 4 } – { 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ . There were no changes in the UV-Vis-NIR spectra of doped P10, which indicated that no electron transfer occurred between the dopant and the polymer, and the doping mechanism of Fig. 19B was applicable in this case.

To solve the issues of the high moisture sensitivity of PEI observed for the $\mathbf { n }$ -doped polymer blends, He et al. recently synthesized a novel aromatic amine based polymer, PDPAT-12 (Fig. 20), as an n-dopant to P20c. 176 It was found that with addition of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ PDPAT-12, the doped P20c blend exhibited unipolar n-type semiconductor performance with electron mobilities of up to $0 . 4 2 \ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { \bf ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ , while pristine P20c showed ambipolar charge transport behaviour with electron and hole mobilities of up to $0 . 3 5 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 2 0 \thinspace { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { V } } ^ { - 1 } { \mathrm { s } } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Again the doped polymer films showed no changes in their UV-Vis-NIR spectra compared to the pristine polymer.

The observed suppression of hole transport was interpreted by the hole trapping effects of PDPAT-12 as illustrated in Fig. 19B, since this compound has a high HOMO energy of $- 5 . 2 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ that forms deep hole traps with an $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ of $0 . 4 5 \mathrm { e V }$ relative to the HOMO energy of P20c, but a higher LUMO energy of $- 2 . 6 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ than that of P20c $\left( - 3 . 8 7 \ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ . The P20c film with $1 0 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ PDPAT-12 has much smoother surface morphology forming a well-connected network compared to the pristine P20c film, which is ascribed to the improved electron mobility of the former. Poor dispersion and phase segregation over time are the common problems for small molecule/polymer blends. To tackle these issues, Quinn et al. designed and synthesized a series of polymers (P20c-TPA) having varied amounts of triphenylamine (TPA) terminal groups (Fig. 24).177 It was expected that the aromatic amine groups would be able to function as n-dopants to self-dope the polymer chain, while the covalent bonding of the TPA end group to the polymer would improve the uniform and stable dispersion of the dopants and solve the phase separation issue mentioned above. The chosen pristine polymer P20c is an ambipolar semiconductor with quite balanced electron and hole mobilities.139 The estimated trap energy $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ of TPA and the polymer using computer simulation is $0 . 4 6 \mathrm { e V }$ , which is sufficient for trapping holes (Fig. 19B). Therefore, the hole transport is expected to be suppressed. Indeed, as the number of TPA groups increases, the hole mobility decreases, while the electron mobility remains similar. An electron and hole mobility ratio $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } \right)$ of 25.6 was achieved, which is much higher than that of the pristine polymer without TPA end-capping $\left( \mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } = 0 . 9 6 \right)$ , i.e., the polymers were transformed from ambipolar to n-type dominant by the simple TPA end-capping. These self-n-doped polymers showed much better stability in terms of the $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } / \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ ratio when thermally annealed at temperatures up to $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ compared to the small amine/polymer blends,175 which showed a recovery of hole transport at much lower annealing temperatures of $8 0 - 1 0 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ caused by phase separation.

# 5. Conclusions

In the past five years or so, numerous novel n-type small molecule and polymer semiconductors have been reported. One approach frequently used has been on the optimization and modification of existing popular electron acceptor building blocks such as NDI, PDI, DPP, etc. Much improved electron mobilities of up to $8 . 6 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (S3a) for small molecule n-type semiconductors and $6 . 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (P1e) for n-type polymer semiconductors were achieved.

Much effort has also been made to develop new electron acceptor building blocks. Several classes of materials with these new building blocks, such as small molecules based on azulene derivatives, dicyanomethylene-terminated quinoidal oligothiophenes, and isoindigo derivatives, and polymers based on DPP and isoindigo-derived building blocks, have shown very promising electron transport properties. For example, IBDF small molecules showed extremely high electron mobilities of up to $1 2 . 6 ~ \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (S26d) in single crystals, while solution processed IBDF polymers also demonstrated the highest electron mobility of $1 4 . 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (P21) reported so far. It should be mentioned that some of the high electron mobilities (such as the highest mobility reported for P21) were extracted from the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region of ‘‘kinked’’ non-ideal transfer curves, while the mobilities extracted from the high $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ of the same transfer curves are lower by more than one order of magnitude. Recently, there have been some debates on whether the high mobility values in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region are overestimated. Based on some studies including ours, we believe that at least in some cases the mobility extracted in the low $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ region might reflect the highly ordered crystalline domains of the organic semiconductors, rather than being overestimated. These mobilities may still be helpful for gaining insight into the structure–charge transport relationship, which is critical for the design of new organic semiconductors or the optimization of processing conditions for the known materials to further enhance charge transport performance in OFETs.

To address the undesirable hole transport characteristics and maintain or enhance the electron transport performance of ambipolar semiconductors, two approaches have been adopted. The first one is to modify the source contact to raise the Fermi energy of the conductor in order to block the hole injection as well as to facilitate the electron transport. Several ambipolar polymers have been successfully converted into unipolar n-type polymers. The second approach is to simply blend a small amount of n-dopant to n-unipolarize ambipolar organic semiconductors or even p-type semiconductors. Amine containing compounds such as $o$ -MeO-DMBI, aromatic amines, and PEI were found to be very effective in suppressing hole transport and promoting electron transport without increasing the off current of the OFETs. It is believed that the Fermi energy of the organic semiconductors is raised by the electron rich n-dopant to effectively fill electron traps thus enhancing the electron transport. On the other hand, the electron lone pairs of the nitrogen atoms in these n-dopants would probably act as trapping sites to suppress hole transport. It was also found that the charge carrier trapping of a dopant can be readily applied to convert ambipolar or p-type polymers into unipolar n-type polymers. When the trap energy, the difference between the HOMO energies of the n-dopant and the matrix semiconductor, is greater than ca. $0 . 2 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , complete hole transport suppression is observed. An n-dopant with such a high $E _ { \mathrm { T } }$ would act as deep traps for the injected holes. n-Doping seems to be a very promising strategy to utilize high mobility ambipolar organic semiconductors for unipolar n-channel OFETs.

In summary, impressive progress has been made in the development of n-type organic semiconductors with electron mobilities approaching the values of the best hole mobilities achieved by p-type organic semiconductors. However, the number of available suitable acceptor building blocks for n-type organic semiconductors is still very limited compared to electron donating building blocks. Furthermore, there are still very few truly unipolar pristine n-type polymers since many electron transport polymers also exhibit hole transport characteristics. More efforts are needed to explore new acceptor building blocks for unipolar n-type polymers. The choice of a comonomer building block for the acceptor is also very important. Excessively strong electron donating ability or very large size of the donor comonomer would result in the appearance of hole transport characteristics.

Air stability is very important for device fabrication and longterm device operation. The stable n-channel OFETs based on small molecules reported so far have been mostly the ones using single crystals, which prevented diffusion of oxygen and moisture into the active charge transport layer. On the other hand, almost all the stable n-channel OFETs based on polymers have been achieved in the TGBC device architecture, where the semiconductor layer was protected by the top dielectric and gate electrode layers from exposure to ambient air. Further attention should be paid to study and further improve the air stability of the n-type semiconductors.

It should be noted that n-type organic semiconductors (and their doped form) are also indispensable materials for several other types of organic electronics such as organic photovoltaics,82,83,107,191–194 thermoelectrics,181,195,196 and batteries.197,198 New materials and knowledge gained through the development of n-type organic semiconductors will be very helpful for the advancement of these closely connected applications.
