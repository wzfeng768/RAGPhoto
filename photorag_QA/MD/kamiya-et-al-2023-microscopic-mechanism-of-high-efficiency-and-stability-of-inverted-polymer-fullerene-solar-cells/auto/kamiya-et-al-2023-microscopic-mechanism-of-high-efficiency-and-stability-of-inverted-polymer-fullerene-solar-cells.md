# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic solar cells have been identified as one of the nextgeneration solar cells because they are lightweight, low-cost, and flexible.!-9 Recently, their power conversion efficiency. (PCE) has been improved remarkably to over $1 9 \%$ 7-9 However, their PCE and stability are still lower than those of other conventional solar cells fabricated from inorganic materials, including crystalline silicon, gallium arsenide, and Other inorganic compound materials.l- Thus i is necessary not only to develop their unique applications taking advantage of the organic solar cells but also to further improve their PCE and stability.24s,14,1s The PCE has increased year by year due to the development of novel materials used for the active layer, electron and hole transport layers, and the optimization of the device structure of the cells using techniques such as inverted device structure and sequential-deposition strategy.7-9,16-19

The influence of the cell structure on the efficiency and stability of organic solar cells is an important issue because the device performance depends on the cell structures even though the same photoactive layers with fullerene acceptors are used. For example, PCEs of approximately $7 - 8 \%$ and of approximately $9 \%$ have been, respectively, achieved for conventional and inverted polymer fullerene solar cells with poly({4,8-bis[(2-ethylhexyl)oxy]benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b']- dithiophene-2,6-diyl}{3-fluoro-2-[(2-ethylhexyl)carbonyl]- thieno[3,4-b]thiophenediyl}) (PTB7), which is one of the representative materials for organic solar cells, and [6,6]- phenyl- $\mathrm { \cdot C } _ { 7 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester $\left( \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } \right)$ for the active ayer.161720his rult inica th thtr impacts the efficiency of organic solar cells.1,17,20 Additionally, the cell structure reportedly affects the stability of polymer fullerene solar cells.1321-23 For example, Hau et al. have investigated the effect of the cell structure on the stability of polymer fullerene solar cells that have poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) and [6,6]-phenyl- $C _ { 6 1 }$ -butyric acid methyl ester $\left( \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } \right)$ as the active layer.2' As a result, cells with the conventional structure became inactive within 4 days; on the other hand, inverted cells continued working for 40 days with only a slight decrease in their performance.

As mentioned above, the PCEs and stability of inverted cells are higher than those of conventional cells in many cases with fullerene acceptors. For another example, a previous study has reported that the PCE of polymer fullerene solar cells with active layers of poly(4,9-bis(4-(2-decyltetradecyl)-5-(thiophen2-yl)thiophen-2-yl)-naphtha[1,2-c:5,6- $\cdot c ^ { \prime }$ ]bis[1,2,5]thiadiazole) (PNTz4T) (Figure 1a) and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ (Figure 1b) is improved by replacing the conventional structure (Figure 1c) with an inverted structure (Figure 1d).24 PNTz4T is a donneraccepter polymer (D-A polymer) composed of quaterthiophene (4T) and naphtho[1,2-c:5,6- $\cdot c ^ { \prime }$ ]bis[1,2,5]thiadiazole (NTz) as the donner and acceptor units, respectively,24,25 which has attracted attention as one of the promising standard p-type semiconducting polymers for absorption layers of polymer fullerene solar cells (Figure 1a).25 Polymer fullerene solar cells using PNTz4T and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as the active layer have achieved the maximum PCE of ${ > } 1 0 \%$ with a single-junction inverted device structure, recently, the maximum PCE of ${ > } 1 3 \%$ has been obtained when a non-fullerene acceptor is used.26 This improvement has been suggested to be caused by the effects of the molecular orientation of $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T }$ 24 That is, in the active layer, $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T }$ molecules tend to be aligned with edge-on orientation near the indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate; however, the molecules show face-on orientation in the bulk and upper regions of the active layer. The face-on orientation is more favorable for hole transport than the edge-on orientation. Thus, one possible explanation is that the improved device performance of the inverted cells is due to improved hole transport toward the upper region of the active layer in the cells. On the basis of the above results, not only the type of organic semiconducting materials used as the active layers but also the cell structures of the polymer fullerene solar cells are critical for improving the efficiency and stability of organic solar cells. However, the microscopic mechanism of the improvement by applying the inverted structure has not been completely clarified.

Here, we report the microscopic investigation of conventional and inverted polymer fullerene solar cells with the same photoactive layers of $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ blend films. For this purpose, we used polymer solar cells with fullerene acceptors because the studies of polymer fullerene solar cells have provided many examples reporting the dependence of device performance on cell structures. Thereby, we microscopically clarify how the different cell structures affect the cells' efficiency and stability. We find a significant charge formation under the dark condition in the as-prepared conventional cells compared to the as-prepared inverted cells, which contributes to a decrease in its initial efficiency. The results of operando ESR spectroscopy under device operation demonstrate that the long-lived charge accumulation and performance deterioration coincide in both types of cells at the initial stage of device performance. Although the number of long-lived accumulated charges is similar in both types of cells under device operation, the stability of the inverted cells is better than that of the conventional cells. This can be explained by the different spatial distributions of the long-lived charge accumulation in the cells depending on the cell structure. These findings provide a striking advance in fundamental understanding, which can explain the origin of the improved efficiency and stability of the inverted cells compared to the conventional ones.

# 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

2.1. Solar Cell Fabrication and Characterization. In this study, we investigate the charge formation in the asprepared conventional and inverted polymer fullerene solar cells under the dark condition and long-lived charge accumulation in these cells during device operation to microscopically clarify how the device structure affects the efficiency and the stability of the cells. For this purpose, we performed ESR measurements under dark conditions and operando ESR measurements on the cells under short-circuit and open-circuit conditions at room temperature. We used a lock-in detection method for the ESR measurements with a modulation frequency of $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { k H z }$ for the external magnetic field. This enables the observation of long-lived (accumulated) charges (spins) with a lifetme of $2 1 0 \mu s$ 57-35 This also means that detectable signals are not related to charge carriers with a short lifetime $\left( < 1 0 \mu s \right)$ contributing to normal device operation under light irradiation. Under the short-circuit and open-circuit conditions, we also monitored changes in shortcircuit current densities, $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } s ,$ and open-circuit voltages, $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } s ,$ respectively. From these simultaneous measurements, we evaluated a correlation between ESR signals and device performances.

We used polymer fullerene solar cells of two types: conventional and inverted cells. The conventional cells were composed of ITO-coated quartz glass/poly(3,4- ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(4-styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) $\left( 3 0 \mathrm { \ n m } \right) / \mathrm { P N T z 4 T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ $3 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ )/LiF (0.8 nm)/Al ( $\cdot 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ (Figure 1c).24 On the other hand, the inverted cells were composed of ITO-coated quartz glass/ $_ \mathrm { Z n O }$ $\left( 3 0 \mathrm { \ n m } \right) / \mathrm { P N T z 4 T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ $\left( 3 0 0 \mathrm { \ n m } \right) / \mathrm { M o O } _ { x }$ $( 7 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m } ) / \mathrm { A g }$ $\left( 1 0 0 \mathrm { n m } \right)$ (Figure 1d).24 Energy diagrams of the materials used in the conventional and inverted cells are shown in Figure le,f. \begin{array} { l }  \displaystyle { { J _ { \mathrm { { S C , p } } } } = - e { D _ { \mathrm { p } } } \frac { \mathrm { { d } } \left( { \Delta p } \right) } { { \mathrm { d } } z } + e { \mu _ { \mathrm { { p } } } } { E _ { \mathrm { b i } } } \Delta p } \\ { \displaystyle = { \mu _ { \mathrm { p } } } \left\{ - k T \frac { { \mathrm { d } } \left( { \Delta p } \right) } { { \mathrm { d } } z } + e { E _ { \mathrm { b i } } } \Delta p \right\} } \end{array}
$$

where $e$ is the elementary charge, $D _ { \mathfrak { n } }$ and $D _ { \mathfrak { p } }$ are the diffusion coefficients defined by the Einstein relation for electrons and holes, respectively, $\mu _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } }$ are the electron and hole mobilities, respectively, $\Delta n$ and $\Delta p$ are the excess free electron and hole densities, respectively, $E _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ is the electric field due to the built-in potential, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $T$ is the temperature. The equations indicate that $J _ { \mathrm { S C , n } }$ and $J _ { \mathrm { S C , p } }$ are proportional to the carrier mobilities $\mu _ { \mathfrak { n } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } } ,$ respectively. Here, we assume that $\mu _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } }$ are independent of the position and equal $\mu _ { { \mathrm n } , 0 }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 }$ at the initial stage, respectively. When the scattering due to accumulated charges occurs and reduces the mobilities, $\mu _ { \mathfrak { n } }$ is written as follows on the basis of Matthiesen's rule31-35,40

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \displaystyle \frac { 1 } { \mu _ { \mathrm { n } } } = \frac { 1 } { \mu _ { \mathrm { n , 0 } } } + \frac { 1 } { ( c _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } / n _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } ) \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } } } } \\ { \displaystyle \therefore \mu _ { \mathrm { n } } = \frac { \mu _ { \mathrm { n , 0 } } } { 1 + ( \mu _ { \mathrm { n , 0 } } / ( \mu _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } \bullet c _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } ) ) n _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } } } \end{array}
$$

$$
\therefore J _ { \mathrm { S C , n } } = \frac { \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n , 0 } } } \left\{ \frac { k T \mathrm { d } ( \Delta n ) } { \mathrm { d } z } + e E _ { \mathrm { b i } } \Delta n \right\} } { 1 + ( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n , 0 } } } / ( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } } { \bullet } c _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } ) ) n _ { \mathrm { n , C A } } }
$$

where $\mu _ { \mathrm { n } , 0 }$ is the initial $\mu _ { \mathrm { n } } , \mu _ { \mathrm { n , C A } }$ is the electron mobility with charge accumulation, $n _ { \mathrm { n , C A } }$ is the density of long-lived accumulated charges in the acceptor material, and $c _ { \mathrm { n , C A } }$ is the proportional constant. $n _ { \mathrm { n , C A } }$ is related to the $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } }$ observed in ESR measurements and, in this case, corresponds to the density of electrons additionally accumulated in the acceptor material, $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } _ { , }$ due to light irradiation. The $J _ { \mathrm { S C , p } }$ is similarly obtained as follows

$$
J _ { \mathrm { S C , p } } = \frac { \mu _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 } \Bigl \{ - \frac { k T \mathrm { d } ( \Delta p ) } { \mathrm { d } z } + e E _ { \mathrm { b i } } \Delta p \Bigr \} } { 1 + ( \mu _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 } / ( \mu _ { \mathrm { p } , \mathrm { C A } } \bullet c _ { \mathrm { p } , \mathrm { C A } } ) ) n _ { \mathrm { p , C A } } }
$$

where $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 }$ is the initial $\mu _ { \mathrm { p } } , \ \mu _ { \mathrm { p , C A } }$ is the hole mobility with charge accumulation, $n _ { \mathrm { p , C A } }$ is the density of long-lived accumulated charges in the donor material, and $c _ { \mathrm { p , C A } }$ is the proportional constant. In this case, the $n _ { \mathrm { p , C A } }$ corresponds to the density of holes additionally accumulated in the donor material, PNTz4T, due to light irradiation. In the end, $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ is

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { J _ { \mathrm { S C } } = \frac { \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n } , 0 } } \{ k T | \nabla \left( \Delta n \right) | + e E _ { \mathrm { b i } } \Delta n \} } { 1 + \left( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n } , 0 } } / \left( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { n } , \mathrm { C A } } } \bullet c _ { \mathrm { n } , \mathrm { C A } } \right) \right) n _ { \mathrm { n } , \mathrm { C A } } } } \\ & { \qquad + \frac { \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 } } \{ - k T | \nabla \left( \Delta p \right) | + e E _ { \mathrm { b i } } \Delta p \} } { 1 + \left( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { p } , 0 } } / \left( \mu _ { _ { \mathrm { p } , \mathrm { C A } } } \bullet c _ { \mathrm { p } , \mathrm { C A } } \right) \right) n _ { \mathrm { p } , \mathrm { C A } } } } \end{array}
$$

This equation clearly shows that $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ decreases with the increased densities of long-lived accumulated charges. This correlation explains that the hole accumulation in $\mathrm { \bar { P N T z 4 T } }$ and the electron accumulation in $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ^ { - }$ in the complexes with ${ \mathrm { L i } ^ { + } }$ decrease the $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ in conventional cells. In other words, this correlation indicates that the charge formation and accumulation are significantly related to the device-performance stability of conventional cells. Therefore, operando ESR spectroscopy, which gives insights into the charge formation and accumulation of complete devices in operation, is a powerful tool for the investigation of the performance stability of solar cells.

Another possible explanation for the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ decrease in this study may be that the long-lived accumulated charges at donor-acceptor interfaces weaken the built-in electric field. This weakened built-in electric field may cause a reduction in the exciton dissociation yield and an enhancement in carrier recombination at donor-acceptor interfaces; thereby, the $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ possibly decreases. This possibility, however, may be excluded by comparing the difference between the spatial distribution of charge accumulation in the conventional and inverted cells, as discussed later.

Figure $_ { 4 c }$ shows the evolution of the open-circuit voltage, $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ,$ of the conventional cell under device operation. Here, normalized $V _ { \mathrm { O C } } , \ V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ( t ) / V _ { \mathrm { O C } } ( 0 )$ is shown. In conventional polymer solar cells with P3HT or PTB7 as active layers, the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ decreases because of the hole and electron accumulation at the interface between the active layer and PEDOT:PSS during light irradiation.28,29 These cells show band bending due to the charge transfer between the active layer and PEDOT:PSS just by stacking these films under dark conditions.28,29 For this phenomenon, it has been discussed that when the interface charge transfer due to stacking active layers and hole transport materials occurs significantly under dark conditions, deep trapping levels are formed and the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ tends to more significantly decrease during device operation.2,29,31 To examine the charge transfer under dark conditions, we measured ESR signals of an ITO/PE$\mathrm { D O T } { : } \mathrm { P S S } / \mathrm { P N T z } 4 \mathrm { T } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ stacked film and a PEDOT:PSS single-layered film under dark conditions. One possible explanation for the reason why such electron accumulation does not reduce the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ is that such long-lived accumulated electrons are neutralized by attracting long-lived PNTz4T cations rather than creating electric dipole layers at the interface with the LiF/Al electrodes. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that the absolute $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } }$ value of PNTz4T cations is almost identical to that of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ anions (see Figure 4a,b).

2.8. Origins of Performance Difference between the Conventional and Inverted Cells. As described above, we find the different charge formations in the as-prepared conventional and inverted solar cells and investigate the correlation in the variation of $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } }$ and the decrease in $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ of these cells during device operation. As discussed in the previous ESR study on pentacene/ $\mathrm { { ' C } } _ { 6 0 }$ heterojunction solar cells, the charge formation in the as-prepared cells is crucial because such charge formation decreases the efficiency of solar cells.27-3s The charge formation at the organic/electrode interface in the as-prepared conventional cells seems to have similar effects as that caused by the accumulation of space charges because the charge formation prevents current flow and creates additional potential in the cells, which decreases the efficiency, as mentioned above.27-35 Therefore, the efficiency can be further improved by reducing the charge formation in as-prepared cells. Such improvement can be attempted effectively by observing microscopic properties by means of ESR spectroscopy.

Here, we consider the reasons behind the higher stability of the inverted cells than that of the conventional cells, in terms of the effects of charge formation and accumulation on the device performance. (See detailed discussion on the influence of charge formation and accumulation on the device performance and stability) From the viewpoint of $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } } ,$ there was no significant difference between the conventional and inverted cells (see Figures 4 and 7). However, it is found that the decrease in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of the conventional cell is more significant than that of the inverted cell (see Figures 4 and 7). This difference can be explained by a difference in the spatial distribution of long-lived accumulated charges as discussed below. Figure 8 shows schematic diagrams of the spatial distribution of long-lived accumulated charges in the conventional and inverted cells. As shown in Figure 8a, charge accumulation occurs in the active layer in the vicinity of the LiF/Al electrode in the conventional cell because of the electron trapping levels due to the $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ^ { - } \mathrm { - } \mathrm { L i } ^ { + }$ complexes mentioned above. This causes a high density of long-lived accumulated charges, and $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ is strongly affected by chargecarrier scattering. On the other hand, as shown in Figure 8b, the density of accumulated charges in the inverted cell seems lower than that in the conventional cell case, given that accumulated charges are distributed relatively homogeneously in the active layers. In this case, the influence of charge-carrier scattering becomes smaller than that in the conventional cell, leading to a more stable $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ If a similar high-density state of long-lived accumulated charges occurred in the inverted cells, a similar $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ decrease would occur for the inverted cell. On the basis of eq 10, the higher the densities of long-lived accumulated charges, the more significant the reduction in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ The conventional cell has a higher density of long-lived accumulated charges than that in the inverted cell, as hypothesized in Figure 8 since long-lived accumulated charges tend to concentrate near the LiF/Al electrode. For this reason, $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ decreases significantly near the active layer in the vicinity of the LiF/Al electrode in the conventional cell, and the significantly reduced local $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ limits the whole $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ . Thereby, $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ of the conventional cell is decreased more significantly than that of the inverted cell. On the basis of the above discussion, one may generally say that conventional cells with LiF/Al electron-collecting electrodes show a greater decrease in $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ than inverted cells without LiF/Al electron-collecting electrodes during device operation even when almost the same $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } }$ is obtained. For the complete understanding of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ reduction mechanisms, measurements of the charge mobilities before and after deterioration need to be performed. Additionally, we will have to perform additional experiments to understand the effect of Al/Li electrodes on the stability of cells.

# 3. CONCLUSIONS

We performed the ESR measurements under dark conditions and the operando ESR measurements on the conventional polymer fullerene solar cells $\left( \mathrm { I T O / P E D O T { : } P S S / } \right.$ $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T { : } P C _ { 6 1 } B M / L i F / A l ) }$ and the inverted polymer fullerene solar cells $\left( \mathrm { I T O / Z n O / P N T z 4 T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M / M o O _ { \it x } / A g } \right)$ and clarify the charge states in the as-prepared cells and in these cells under device operation that influence the efficiency and stability from a microscopic viewpoint. We find that the asprepared conventional cells show a significantly large ESR signal intensity due to the charge formation compared to the as-prepared inverted cells under the dark condition. In other words, the ESR signals of the as-prepared inverted cell are negligibly small compared to those of the conventional cell, meaning negligibly smaller charge formation in the as-prepared inverted cell than that in the as-prepared conventional cell under the dark condition. This negligibly small charge formation may contribute to the higher PCE of the inverted cells $\left( 9 . 8 0 - 1 0 . 1 \% \right)$ than that of the conventional cells $( 8 . 7 0 -$ $8 . 9 2 \% ) ^ { 2 4 }$ as well as the favorable molecular orientation for higher PCE in the inverted cell.24

For the conventional cells, two-component ESR signals were observed in the dark and during device operation. The origins of these signals are identified as long-lived $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T }$ cations and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { \bar { B } M }$ anions of $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ^ { - } - \mathrm { L i } ^ { + }$ complexes by investigating the ESR signals of the $\mathrm { P N T z 4 T : P C _ { 6 1 } B M }$ films and the DFT calculation. Simultaneous measurements of ESR spectroscopy and the device performance revealed the correlation between the increase in the charge densities of long-lived PNTz4T cations and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ anions and the decrease in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ For the inverted cells, the ESR signals whose origin is the long-lived accumulated holes in PNTz4T were observed during device operation. In addition, the signal originating from long-lived accumulated electrons in $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ was observed in the low-temperature ESR measurements. Also, the correlation between the increase in $N _ { \mathrm { s p i n } }$ of long-lived PNTz4T cations and the decrease in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ is found for the inverted cells.

The observed $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ decreases can be explained by the chargecarrier scattering due to long-lived accumulated charges for both the conventional and inverted cells. However, the decrease in $J _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { S C } }$ of the conventional cells is larger than that of the inverted cells. This difference may be due to the different spatial distributions of long-lived accumulated charges under device operation. In the case of the conventional cells, the charge accumulation seems to occur in the vicinity of the LiF/ Al electrode because of the electron-trapping levels due to the $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M } ^ { - } { - } \mathrm { L i } ^ { + }$ complexes (see Figure 8a), whereas in the case of the inverted cells, the charge accumulation seems to occur relatively with a wide distribution in the active layer (see Figure 8b). As a result, in the conventional cells, the density of the long-lived accumulated charges is increased in the vicinity of the LiF/Al electrode, which is considered to cause enhanced charge scattering (Figure 8a). On the other hand, in the inverted cells, since the charge-accumulation density is lower than that in the conventional cells, the decrease in $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ is smaller than that in the case of conventional cells (Figure 8b). These findings explain one of the reasons behind the higher stability of inverted cells.

The above operando ESR method can be applied to other organic solar cells with not only fullerene acceptors but also non-fullerene acceptors and can be used to investigate the device operation mechanism and the deterioration root cause. This is useful for the development of highly efficient and highly stable organic solar cells.
