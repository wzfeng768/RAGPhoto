# A Flexible, High-Voltage (>100 V) Generating Device Based on Zebra-Like Asymmetrical Photovoltaic Cascade

Hongxian Zheng, Cailing Ou, Xinxin Huang, Baichuan Jiang, Wenbin Li, Jun Li, Xiao Han, Chuanhui Liu, Zixiao Han, Tingyu Ji, Paolo Samori, and Lei Zhang\*

The mutual conversion between light and electricity lies at the heart of optoelectronic and photonic applications. Maximization of the photoelectric conversion is a long-term goal that can be pursued via the fabrication of devices with ad-hoc architectures. In this framework, it is of utter importance to harvest and transform light irradiation into high electric potential in specific area for driving functional dielectrics that respond to pure electric field. Here, a nano-fabrication technology has been devised featuring double self-alignment that is applied to construct zebra-like asymmetric heterojunction arrays. Such nanostructured composite, which covers a surface area of $5 \times 4 ~ \mathsf { m m } ^ { 2 }$ and contains 500 periodic repeating units, is capable of photo generating voltages as high as 140 V on a flexible substrate. This approach represents a leap over the traditional functionalization process based on simply embedding materials into devices by demonstrating the disruptive potential of integrating oriented nanoscale device components into meta-material.

# 1. Introduction

The photovoltaic effect has been exploited during the last few decades to fabricate solar cells[1-4] and self-driven photodetectors.[5-8] In order to maximize the photocurrent generation, the device geometry and material components have been optimized.[9-14] Among the various figures of merits, the magnitude of the output voltage that can be achieved by visible light irradiation can become key for some applications. For example, an external voltage supply can be used to overcome a certain overpotential barrier thereby facilitating the efficient occurrence of electrochemical reactions.15-18] In some (opto-)electronic applications such as piezoelectric actuators and electro-optical modulators, the generation of sufficient electrostatic field (i.e., large operating voltage) is more important than the electrical power supply being produced by the photovoltaic process.[19-21] However, hitherto the development of new photovoltaic devices producing high output voltages has been under-explored.

Being limited by the energy of visible light photons (ranging from $1 . 6 ~ \mathrm { { \ e V } }$ to $3 . 1 \mathrm { e V } _ { I }$ , the potential of electrons that can be harvested by light excitation is typically below $2 . 0 \mathrm { V }$ in single junction photovoltaic devices.22-25] Although various tandem solar cells (i.e., multi-junctions solar cells, MJSCs) have been realized to break the Shockley--Queisser limit by elevating the output voltage, the sum of stacked junctions is generally no more than three or four to avoid inadequate light transmission.126-29] Thus, the open-circuit voltage (Voc) being generated by MJSCs is typically ranging between 2.0 and 2.8 V.[16,30-32] The highest voltage being generated by vertically stacked multi-cells was still below 10 v.[33,34] Inspired by electric eels, which could produce up to 600 volts of electricity by assembling numerous mini-batteries in head-to-tail sequence, it is envisioned that laterally arranged asymmetric bimetallic structures with marked work function difference, fixed orientation, and multiple repeating units could also produce large output voltages when they are combined with appropriate semiconductors. However, the successful construction of such a complex device structure on large area and plastic substrate is challenging, especially at affordable price and high-resolution, because metal patterns, which are successively deposited through independent procedures, must have connected one another with an accuracy as high as possible. Conventional patterning technology employing mask-alignment for photo-lithography[35,36] and the stencil mask method,37,38] can realize high-precision alignment on rigid substrates under the guidance of microscope, despite of expensive equipment being required. However, such an alignment method is not compatible for the controlled patterning on fexible supports due to the inevitable deformation of large-area plastic substrate.

By considering that even a macroscopic object should possess fine edges that can be used to tailor sharp interfaces with a nanoscale precision, here we have devised a top-down strategy consisting in the double self-alignment technology to realize asymmetric bimetallic arrays with spatial resolution better than $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ [39,40] In essence, we used the first layer of metal stripes as shading mask for photoresist (1st self-alignment), then the pristine metal and isomorphic photoresist stripes were exploited to pattern the second type of metal through oblique evaporation (2nd self-alignment). The edge of the stripes in the first layer must be as straight and fine as possible for the successful realization of the subsequent tilted evaporation. Such a method is enabled to precisely pattern two different metals $1 0 0 { - } 2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ close to each other. Various organic semiconductors including poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT), thin films blend of P3HT and [6,6]-phenyl- $C _ { 6 1 } .$ butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), and $\mathrm { N , N ^ { \prime } }$ -dioctyl-3,4,9,10-perylenedicarboximide(PTCDI-C8) supramolecular nanowires (SMNWs)[41] have been explored as photo- and electro-active material by integrating them in such zebra-like bi-metallic arrays. Optimization of the device geometry and heterojunction interface has been achieved to ensure pronounced photovoltage-boosting effect under illumination.

# 2. Results

# 2.1. The Protocol for Double Self-Alignment Technology

As portrayed in Figure 1a, the simple wiring-up of conventional sandwich-like photovoltaic cells into circuit in series enables to obtain large output voltages.42,43] However, the implementation of such a strategy requires monolithic interconnection between different layers in vertically structured cells. Typically, the interconnection is realized through three patterning lines comprising the bottom transparent electrode (contact layer 2), the photovoltaic layer (PV layers), and the top electrode (contact layer 1). Although the high-resolution fabrication of sub cells is easy, the technological complexity obviously increases when interconnecting layers 1 and 2 pass through a via-hole. In contrast, the planar device configuration, enabling the successive deposition of metals and semiconductors, holds greater potential for the realization of large-area patterns comprising numerous lateral repeat units (Figure 1b). Moreover, the open device structure enables simpler access for thorough and multiscale physical chemistry characterization with surface sensitive techniques.

In this work, we selected gold and aluminum to construct periodic and asymmetrical Janus structures (Figure 1b) in view of their large work function difference (e.g., Au $5 . 1 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and Al $4 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { e V } _ { I }$ . The detailed fabrication procedure is shown in the flow chart of Figure S1 (Supporting Information). For the purpose of serving as self-alignment mask, the first layer of metal stripes should have sharp edges and be inert. Therefore, standard photolithography and lift-off processes were employed to engineer gold patterns and ensure the clean fringes. Then positive photoresist was spin-coated onto these gold stripes. The photoresist could be zone selectively exposed to UV light being shed from the back. In this way, the photoresist is prone to form patterns being identical to gold stripes.

Thanks to the diffraction effect, i.e., light could propagate around corners, the photoresist has receded 200-300 nm from the exact edge of gold as characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM) in Figure 1c. This phenomenon is critical for the successful realization of our design because the second metal of Al should directly connect to gold stripe at one of its two sides. By making use of oblique evaporation, Al electrodes were then deposited with a tilt angle of $6 0 ^ { \circ }$ by high-vacuum thermal evaporation to overlap with gold at the side facing the evaporation source, meanwhile forming a channel in the shadow of photoresist (Figure 1d, Figure S1, Supporting Information). Both the thickness of photoresist and the tilting angle during evaporation were tunable, thereby making it possible to adjust the channel length between Al and Au according to the needs.

# 2.2. The Optimization of Device Performance

As preliminary attempt, three solution-processed organic semiconducting layers including a P3HT thin film, PTCDI-C8 nanowires[41] and P3HT/PCBM blends have been chosen to be coupled with the Au/Al bottom electrode array. Under the illumination of Xenon lamp, optoelectronic tests were performed by recording the current density versus voltage $\left( J - W \right)$ , from which the corresponding open-circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ could be obtained. As shown in Figure 1f, the photovoltaic devices incorporating neat P3HT and PTCDI-C8 NWs films showed a collective $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ absolute value of 0.8 and $3 . 2 ~ \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively. Such output voltages were indeed trivial in view of 100 repeating units existing in the periodic Al/Au array. This phenomenon can be easily understood because photovoltaic devices based on single organic semiconductor and asymmetric metal electrodes usually exhibit very low open-circuit voltage.[44] As demonstrated in earlier works, the use of p-n heterojunctions greatly facilitates efficient excitons separation in solar cells and largely improves their PV performance.!45,46] Not surprisingly, when the electron accepting material, i.e., PCBM, was mixed with P3HT, both the short circuit current density and the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ could benefit significantly from the formation of a bulk heterojunction (BHJ). In particular, the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ raised to $^ { 1 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ and the short circuit current increased over 50-folds when the P3HT/PCBM blend was used to replace the neat semiconductors (Figure 1f). The corresponding single junction Au-Al diodes with planar configuration have also been fabricated and tested to study the electrical contact between $\mathrm { { A u / A l } }$ and these three semiconducting films (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

It is worth noting that the typical $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of single junction P3HT/PCBM solar cells could reach ${ \approx } 0 . 5 5 { - } 0 . 6 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ according to the previous reports.47,48] Consequently the collective voltage of $^ { 1 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ is still unsatisfactory because it implies that each repeating unit can only contribute $\approx 1 3 0 \ \mathrm { m V }$ as single junction, giving notable room for further improvement. According to the operation mechanism of organic solar cells, the voltage loss could be explained in terms of the recombination process and the pronounced reverse photogenerated current. Obviously, the spincoated P3HT/PCBM film, which is intrinsically a very good photoconductor, would lead to significant reverse leakage of photo-generated charges through the bulk. In order to minimize the reverse current in between two electrodes under high voltage, we need a semiconductor layer, in which the electrical conductivity markedly decreases with the distance, to promote greater voltage accumulation at the far ends of the device. Such charge transport characteristics can be found in nanofiber films.149] As shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information), both spin-coated P3HT/PCBM film and PTCDI-C8 nanofibers film are incorporated as active layers of organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) with channel length varying from $3 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ to $1 0 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ . For the PTCDI-C8 nanofibers film, the mobility in saturation region of the OFETs with ultralong channel lengths $( 1 , 0 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ would decrease to $1 / 6 7$ of the peak mobility in short channel transistor. As for spin-coated P3HT/PCBM thin film, the hole mobility and electron mobility are only 1.6 and 4.4 times smaller, respectively, when tested in ultra-long channel transistors.

In order to combine the advantages brought into play by heterojunctions and nanofiber morphology, we further optimized the device geometry by zone-selectively modification of the Au surface with thin P3HT layer (Figure 2a). Toward this end, the originally continuous spin-coated P3HT layer was cut by dry-etching into stripes having identical shape with the photoresist patterns being defined through backside lithography. In this way, the P3HT layer would also recede from Au edges due to diffraction effect (Figure 2b). This narrow area of naked Au allows for the intimate contact between Au and the subsequently evaporated Al layer. Finally, PTCDI-C8 NWs, which have been demonstrated to form high performance PV heterojunctions with P3HT in nanophotonic devices,50] were deposited to complete the device fabrication. The density and degree of coverage of PTCDI-C8 NWs could be maximized through a hierarchical self-assembly process[1] as illustrated in Figure 2c and Figure S6 (Supporting Information). Highly uniform nanofibers films with almost $100 \%$ coverage could be achieved as revealed by the OM and SEM imaging (see Figures S7 and S8, Supporting Information).

Following such strategy, the planar heterojunctions (PHJs) of P3HT and PTCDI-C8 NWs have been selectively connected to the periodic Au and Al bimetal nanostructures, respectively. In this way, such PHJs were successfully integrated into one single device as they were laterally connected head-to-tail in an asymmetric and periodic way. Upon illumination with white light, the collective voltage of such PHJs device increased rapidly and reached a plateau of $2 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ (Figure 2d). The measurement was performed by keeping current bias to zero while recording the open-circuit voltage. The dynamic test of open-circuit voltage and short circuit photocurrent upon switching cycles was also performed; the device exhibited a response time below 5 s for light/dark switchover (Figure S9, Supporting Information). As more commonly employed for OPV characterization, the $J { - } V$ curves under dark and illumination conditions are also reported as the inset of Figure 2d, also revealing a $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ exceeding $2 0 \ \mathrm { V } .$

To gain insight into the origin of photo-generated charges, the photoresponsivity (R) spectrum was recorded when irradiating the device with monochromic light from 350 to $7 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ The photoresponsivity was calculated and plotted (Figure 2e) by dividing the short circuit current with light power density at different wavelength. For comparison, the UV-vis absorbance of PTCDI-C8 nanowires and P3HT is also portrayed in Figure 2e. It can be deduced that both the p-type and n-type components in the PHJs have contributed to the total photovoltaic effect as evidenced by the wavelength dependence, with best response observed at the absorption peaks of PTCDI-C8 and P3HT (Figure 2e).[50]

Significantly, this method is also compatible with flexible substrates such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET). We fabricated a large integrated module comprising 500 repeat units on PET support, which exhibited an open-circuit voltage of $9 4 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ over an area of $5 \times 4 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ (Figure 3a). After 6 days of storage in the glove box, the output voltage increased up to

107 V under the same test conditions. Such a markedly high improvement can be attributed to the doping effect that occurs in the active layer during storage. To exploit such a physical process even further, the module was tested again after exposure to air. Not surprisingly, the output voltage continued to increase under illumination in air and finally reached ${ \approx } 1 4 0 \ \mathrm { V } ,$ thanks to the achieved greater level of doping (Figure 3b). Further experiments showed that the phenomenon of photovoltaic voltage enhancement upon aging should originate from the oxidative doping of P3HT,[52,53] as detailed in Figures S10 and S11 (Supporting Information). In order to verify the stability of the flexible devices to mechanical bending, fatigue tests were carried out at a well-defined bending radius and repeat cycles. After 500 bending cycles over a $3 2 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ diameter cylinder, the output voltage dropped by ${ < } 1 0 \%$ (Figure 3c). The output voltage of flexible device as a function of bending radius was also tested and the results are illustrated in Figure 3d. It reveals three different bending modes, i.e., outward bending, inward bending, and bending over an axis in parallel with the voltage generating direction. Significantly, the output voltage could remain above $90 \%$ when the device is bent, indicating a superior mechanical robustness. To sum up, these results provide evidence that the high-voltage generators based on OPV series have reliable bending durability when they are exploited in flexible devices. In Figure 3e, we summarized all the previous reports on voltage-boosting photovoltaic modules and compared them with this work. Obviously, the zebra-like device has predominated in terms of output voltage and voltage generation per area. To the best of our knowledge, the zebra-like device represents the level of state-of-the-art by integrating 500 repeat cells in just $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ area. Considering the plenty room for the improvement of power conversion efficiency (PCE) in such zebra-like photovoltaic devices, further optimization to this regard is ongoing.

# 2.3. Mechanism Study of Photoinduced High Voltage Generation

The open structure and the size-adjustability of planar device geometry make it very convenient to study the generation and transverse transmission of photogenerated carriers in heterojunctions. Here, we prepared a set of OPV cascade devices with different cathode-to-anode composition ratios. In each repeat unit, the gold electrode, which has been modified with P3HT layer, would occupy a different area ratio, from $20 \%$ to $80 \%$ with a step of $10 \%$ . Meanwhile, the remaining area is occupied by aluminum. The schematic diagram and SEM images of electrode structures with gold/aluminum composition ratio of $2 0 \% { : } 8 0 \%$ and $8 0 \% : 2 0 \%$ are shown in Figure 4a. The remaining devices with other area ratios have been characterized by optical microscopy, and the images are shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information).

Interestingly, the open circuit voltage $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ could be significantly tuned by varying the geometric proportion of microelectrodes. For example, the photoinduced output voltage in P3HT/PTCDI-C8 NWs device is positively correlated with the area fraction of gold electrode (Figure 4b; Figure S13, Supporting Information). Specifically, when the composition ratio of gold to aluminum is $20 \mathrm { - } 8 0 \%$ or vice versa, the resulting $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ amounts to 5 and $2 3 \ \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively (Figure $^ { 4 \mathrm { c } ) }$ . Such an outcome demonstrates that the heterogeneous interface for excitons separation plays a vital role in the photovoltaic process of such composite nanostructures. Therefore, when the heterojunction area is larger, more excitons can be effectively separated and collected at the p-n interface, yielding higher external quantum efficiency (EQE) and short-circuit current density $( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } )$ (Figure 4d, Table 1; Figure S14, Supporting Information). Moreover, the increased proportion of Au/P3HT surface also altered the shape of photo responsivity spectrum, with the photovoltaic performance becoming more pronounced under long wavelength irradiation (e.g., $5 7 0 \mathrm { n m }$ vs $4 6 0 \mathrm { n m }$ )(Table 1; Figure S14, Supporting Information). This phenomenon also suggests that the contribution by P3HT layer has increased with more $\mathrm { A u } /$ P3HT surface ratio, considering the absorbance differences in P3HT and PTCDI-C8.

However, when neat P3HT or PTCDI-C8 NWs are deposited onto the naked Au/Al bi-metallic electrode array without modification, we observed the opposite behavior: the larger the proportion of aluminum, the higher the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of the device (Figure S15, Supporting Information). It can be reasonably inferred that the exciton separation mainly occurs at the Schottky junction formed in contact with the aluminum electrode. In other words, the interface between aluminum and organic semiconductors can compensate the minority carriers in molecular excited state by charge transferring. As a result, photovoltaic effect has been observed, with the efficacy much lower than that in p-n junctions according to the vast difference of orders of magnitude in the short-circuit current density (Figure 1f). This hypothesis can also be verified by the opposite PV polarity in devices based on neat P3HT and PTCDI-C8, i.e., Al behaves as cathode for p-type semiconductors like P3HT whereas it acts as anode for n-type PTCDI-C8 (Figure S15, Supporting Information).

Since aluminum can neutralize both electrons and holes in organic semiconductors, it would serve as recombination center if contacting simultaneously p-type and n-type semiconductors. Therefore, in bulk heterojunction-based cascade device made of P3HT/PCBM, the increase of aluminum content is detrimental to the device performance. The $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ degraded to one sixth of its original value from 13 to $2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ if Al duty ratio increased from $20 \%$ to $80 \%$ (Figure S16, Supporting Information). All these phenomena provide unambiguous evidence that the cascade device does not function as a simple sum of series planar photovoltaic cells. The sensitivity of output voltage and EQE to both the device geometry and the choice of heterojunction materials indicates that there is still plenty room for improving the photoelectric conversion efficiency and the output voltage generated per unit area or length. Indeed, the high PCE and EQE are also critical to promote the development of self-powered opto-electronic applications. Further optimization can be made by shrinking the repeat units and/or by increasing the interfacial contact area between the asymmetric electrodes.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have devised a dual self-aligning technique and exploited to fabricate a novel asymmetric, periodic and oriented nanocomposites made of gold and aluminum stripes. Such bi-metallic nanostructures can wire-up $\mathrm { p - n }$ heterojunctions in a controlled fashion organized in aligned direction. The illumination of arrays of small rectangular stripes of $5 \times 4 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ could produce a high collective voltage exceeding $1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ on a flexible substrate. The electric potential generation per unit length reached values as high as $3 5 0 \mathrm { V } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ . Although the use of an external LC voltage-booster circuit also enables to enhance the output of batteries to hundreds of volts, we have demonstrated for the first time that such a high voltage can be generated by a self-powered and compact on-chip system. By exploiting the numerous strategies developed in the past decades for manufacturing array of linear patterns towards high-density solid-state memory, the performance of such "zebra" optoelectronic devices can be further improved in the future by adopting other highly precise and low-cost patterning technologies such as nano imprinting. Of course, besides such "top-down" strategy, it is desirable and envisioned use of "bottom-up' hierarchical self-assembly method to achieve a similar goal. Our findings have boosted the transformation between light and electric, reaching performance that makes it useful for the regulation of piezoelectric and electro-optic materials in self-powered smart instruments by supplying large enough electrostatic field.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: P3HT (number-average molecular weight $M _ { \mathfrak { n } } = 8 5 ~ 0 0 0 -$ 100 000), PTCDI-C8 $9 8 \%$ pure) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich without further purification. PCBM $( 9 9 . 5 \%$ pure) was purchased from Hebei J&K Scientific Co., Ltd, China.

Preparation of PTcDI-c8 NWs Film: First, PTcDI-C8 solution in chloroform was prepared with a concentration of $0 . 2 5 ~ \mathsf { m g } ~ \mathsf { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ . Then $2 0 0 ~ \mu \iota$ PTCDI-C8/chloroform solution was injected rapidly into $4 \ m L$ pure ethanol. PTcDI-C8 molecules would precipitate within seconds in non-solvent of ethanol thereby assembling into nanowires. The PTCDI-C8 NWs were carefully collected, washed, and re-dispersed in a solvent mixture of hexane/ethanol $( \nu / \nu , \ 4 ; 7 )$ by sonication. Finally, the PTCDI-C8 NWs being dispersed in hexane/ethanol was dropped onto water surface to form floating fibrous film, which was compressed by the droplets of the ethyl acetate (EAc) solution with poly(4-vinylphenol) (PVP) $( 5 0 \mathrm { \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } } )$ . The compressed PTCDI-C8 NWs film could be transferred onto the pre-patterned substrate. The samples were annealed at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ in nitrogen atmosphere for $3 0 ~ \mathsf { m i n }$ before optoelectronic testing.

Preparation of Anode Modification Layer and BHJ Photovoltaic Layer: As the modification layer for Au, 16 nm P3HT flm was spin-coated from $8 \mathrm { \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ chlorobenzene solution at 3,000 rpm. Then the samples were annealed at $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ for $3 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ before next manufacturing steps. As the BHJ active layer, a solution of $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ P3HT and PCBM $( m / m$ 1:0.8) in chlorobenzene were spin-coated at 2,500 rpm. for $6 0 ~ \mathsf { s }$ onto the substrate with pre-patterned strips array. The film was dried after storing in vacuum for $4 \textmd h$

Fabrication of Zebra Asymmetric Heterojunctions on PET: PET substrate used in the study was successively cleaned with acetone and isopropanol and then dried with flowing nitrogen. PVP and poly(melamine-co-formaldehyde) methylated (m/m, 2:1; $6 0 \ m g \ m L ^ { - 1 }$ in poly(ethyleneglycol) dimethacrylate) was spin-coated at 4,000 rpm. for 40 s onto PET substrate to smooth it. The PVP cross-linking process was performed by a two-step thermal treatment at $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ for $3 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ and at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . Subsequent fabrication procedures on PET substrate would be the same as those been described in the main text on glass substrate (Figure 2a).

Optoelectronic Characterization: The optoelectronic characterization was performed under nitrogen atmosphere if not differently noted. The monochromatic light source was purchased from Zolix (Beijing). The output light power at different wavelength had been calibrated by PMio0A Power Meter from Thorlabs company. Keithley 2634B semiconductor characterization system was used to perform the electrical characterization. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) and photo responsivity (R) are extracted according to the following equations: $\mathsf { E Q E } =  ( J _ { \mathsf { S C } } \times h c ) / ( P _ { \mathsf { i n } } \ q \lambda ) , \ \mathsf { R } = J _ { \mathsf { S } }$ $R = { \cal J } _ { \sf S C } / P _ { \mathrm { i n } } ,$ where $J _ { \mathsf { S C } }$ is the short-circuit current density being obtained by dividing the shortcircuit current with the surface area of Au-Al gap, $h$ is the Plank constant, $c$ is the velocity of light, $P _ { \mathrm { i n } }$ is the incident light power, $q$ is the elementary charge, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of incident monochromatic light.

Morphological and Uv-Vis Characterization: Morphology and microstructures of organic semiconductor thin flms were measured using BX53MRF-S optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), Hitachi S4800 field-emission SEM, and FM-Nanoview LS-AFM (FSMPRECISION, Suzhou, China). UV-2600 UV-vis spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU, Suzhou, China) was used for UV-vis spectrum characterization.
