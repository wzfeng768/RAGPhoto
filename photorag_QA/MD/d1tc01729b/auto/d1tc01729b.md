# Pyrene-1,5,6,10-tetracarboxyl diimide: a new building block for high-performance electrontransporting polymers†

Yao Li, $\textcircled{1}$ Zefan Yao, $\textcircled{1}$ Jiajun Xie, Han Han, Guantao Yang, Xudong Bai, Jian Pei $\textcircled{1}$ \* and Dahui Zhao $\textcircled{1}$

Both naphthalene and perylene derivatives bearing six-membered-ring dicarboximide groups are proven valuable modules for preparing organic electron-transporting semiconductors. However, obtaining their analogue with pyrene appeared challenging, as all previous endeavors resulted in 5- instead of 6-membered dicarboximide rings. Here, we report the first successful synthesis of pyrene-1,5,6,10- tetracarboxyl diimide (PyDI), a centro-symmetric structure comprising two 6-membered dicarboximide groups. Moreover, this molecule undergoes regioselective dihalogenation to afford a 3,8-dibromosubstituted derivative, which may conveniently be incorporated into conjugated polymers via crosscoupling with suitable comonomers. Two representative conjugated polymers, PyDI-T and PyDI-TT, are thus prepared, composed of alternating PyDI and thiophene or bithiophene units. OFETs are then fabricated using these polymers under solution-processing conditions. An optimal electron mobility of $0 . 3 3 5 \mathsf { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ is achieved by PyDI-TT, which clearly demonstrates the potential of 1,5,6,10-PyDI as a potent building block for developing new high-performance electronic-transporting materials.

# Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) dicarboximides are potent electron-deficient building blocks useful for developing n-type organic small-molecule and polymer semiconductors.1,2 Among the various PAH dicarboximide molecules, naphthalenediimide $\left( \mathrm { N D I } \right) ^ { 3 - 5 }$ and perylenediimide $\mathrm { ( P D I ) } ^ { 6 , 7 }$ are two most widely studied and used functional modules for exhibiting excellent optical and electronic properties, and diverse NDI and PDI derivatives are conveniently prepared from the corresponding commercially available tetracarboxylic anhydrides. In view of the outstanding performance of NDI and PDI, researchers have designed and synthesized a range of new PAH diimides over the years,1,8,9 such as anthracenediimide,10,11 pentacenediimide,12 terrylenediimide,13,14 biazulenediimide,15,16 hexacenediimide,17 coronenediimide,18–21 etc. However, as an important member of the family, pyrene-1,5,6,10-diimide, featuring two six-membered dicarboximide groups, like NDI and PDI, has not been obtained.

Pyrene, with four fused benzene rings,22–24 is of an intermediate size between naphthalene and perylene. Such a medium size on the one hand confers adequate aromatic stacking interactions and on the other hand ensures the dicarboximide groups installed around the periphery to induce an ample electron-withdrawing effect on the PAH skeleton, both of which are crucial to promoting optimal electrontransporting capacity. In terms of chemical properties, the six carbons in the non-K-region (1, 2, 3, 6, 7, and 8) are observed generally more reactive toward electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions than 4, 5, 9, and 10 positions in the K-region (Fig. 1).25 Upon introducing dicarboximide groups, a number of regioisomers of pyrene diimide (PyDI) can be drawn. Some of us first acquired 1,2,6,7-PyDI by exposing pyrene-1,6-dicarbonyl dichloride to $N \cdot$ -alkylated isocyanate and realized imidization and cyclization through a Friedel–Crafts (FC) acylation-type of reaction.26 Yu’s group then reported the synthesis of 4,5,9,10- PyDI, using 1,2,3,6,7,8-hexahydropyrene as the starting material, which facilitated the site-selective functionalization of the K-regions.27 Later, by blocking the 2 and 7 positions in the non-K-regions with tert-butyl groups, Zhang et al. obtained 2,7- disubstituted 4,5,9,10-PyDI via direct bromination followed by carboxylation of all four carbons in the K-regions. An impressive average electron mobility of up to $2 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was achieved by such 4,5,9,10-PyDI derivatives in small-molecule field-effect transistors.28 More recently, Leclerc and colleagues accomplished the first preparation of a PyDI-based conjugated polymer using 4,5,9,10-PyDI-2,7-diboronate as a monomer.29 While evident conductivity was recorded for the composites formed by the polymer with single-walled carbon nanotubes, negligible electron mobility was detected with the pristine polymer.

In contrast to the successful attainments of above isomers, the synthesis of 1,5,6,10-PyDI has not been reported. The difficulty partly arises from the dicarboximide groups necessarily installed to bridge the K and non-K regions with different reactivities.30 Nonetheless, 1,5,6,10-PyDI has been an attractive target for years, because the six-membered-ring dicarboximide functionality has been suggested to bring about superior properties, including high stability, low electron-injection barrier, optimal radical anion stability, and favourable rotational and slipping packing geometry.31,32 Hence, in spite of the anticipated challenges we endeavored to make 1,5,6,10-PyDI, and after exploring a number of different routes the molecule is finally obtained with a desirable overall yield. Moreover, site-selective bromination of carbons 3 and 8 in the non-K regions is also achieved, allowing for subsequent facile preparation of regioregular conjugated polymers. That is, by applying 3,8-dibromo1,5,6,10-PyDI (diBr-PyDI) for cross-coupling with thiophene and bithiophene comonomers, two conjugated polymers (PyDI-T and PyDI-TT) are obtained and characterized. When fabricated into organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) under solution-processing conditions, both polymers demonstrate n-type semiconducting capability, and PyDI-TT is shown to manifest an impressive electron mobility of up to $0 . 3 4 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ . These results indicate that 1,5,6,10-PyDI is a potent building block for making new highperformance electron-transporting materials and helps enrich the toolbox for developing the functionality of PAH diimides.

# Results and discussion

In designing a suitable route for synthesizing 1,5,6,10-PyDI, we first speculated about the approach via direct imidization of a 1,5,6,10-pyrenetetracarboxyl derivative. However, a necessary precursor to the tetracarboxyl molecule, 1,5,6,10-tetrabromopyrene, was not attainable upon a number of attempted methods (Scheme 1a). As an alternative protocol, double acylation of a dicarboxyl substrate was next considered. However, when pyrene-1,6-dicarbonyl dichloride was previously subjected to a reaction with an isocyanate (R-NCO), 1,2,6,7-PyDI was consistently produced via tandem substitution and cyclization steps (Scheme 1b).26 Under such conditions, 1,5,6,10-PyDI formation was not detected. That is, rather than forming the six-membered dicarboximide rings by reacting with C5 and C10 in the K-region, the electrophilic substitution took place with C2 and C7 in the non-K region and produced two 5-membered dicarboximide groups. We thus contemplated that, since four-membered rings are much more difficult to form compared to five-membered analogues, by devising an intramolecular Friedel–Crafts (F–C) acylation with pyrene-1,6-diacetyl chloride, the cyclization should be directed to occur at C5 and C10. The expected dicyclopentapyrenedione derivative could then be converted to pyrene-1,5,6,10-tetracarboxyl dianhydride (Scheme 1c). Unfortunately, after screening a series of different conditions, including varied Lewis acids, solvents, and temperatures, all the reactions afforded some insoluble, unidentifiable precipitates. As all the above results suggested insufficient nucleophilicity of the K-region, we then turned to the radical substitution mechanism for activating the desired cyclization sites (Scheme 1d).33 However, when subjecting 1,6-bis(bromoacetyl)pyrene to light irradiation, only a trace amount of a side product featuring only one closed dicarboximide ring was detected by NMR, and no desired dicyclopentapyrenedione was identified, with most of the substrate found decomposed. All these unsuccessful endeavors suggested that the strategies entailing cyclizing the K-region positions were not feasible, likely because of their low reactivity.34 Hence, a synthetic route featuring a ring-closure direction from the $\mathbf { K } \mathbf { - }$ to non-K regions was subsequently investigated. This pathway necessarily entailed 4,9-bisfunctionalized pyrene derivatives as the precursor. Again, the direct imidization method was examined first. Although the reaction between pyrene-4,9-dicarbonyl dichloride with isocyanate generated a major product, its $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectrum indicated that the product possessed a non-centrosymmetrical structure, which was likely 1,4,5,10-PyDI. Presumably, the first dicarboximide ring was formed as designed, but its appearance caused considerable electron deficiency, rendering lowered reactivity at C6. As a result, the second imidization/cyclization was diverted to C4, with the formation of a 5-membered rather than 6-membered ring (Scheme 1e).

Finally, we harnessed the F–C acylation method to circumvent the adverse regio-selectivity in the double cyclization step (Scheme 2). Upon lithiation of 4,9-dibromopyrene (1),35 nucleophilic substitution to diethyl oxalate was conducted to produce a bisoxoacetate derivative (2). This compound was then reduced after hydrolysis,36 to afford pyrene-4,9-diacetic acid 4.37 Upon converting the diacid into diacetyl chloride, intramolecular F–C acylation was performed at room temperature in the presence of $\mathbf { A l C l } _ { 3 }$ . Therefore, the desired dione 5 with the expected regioisomerism was obtained. Apparently, with diacid 4 both F–C acylation/cyclizations took place at the desired positions, because reactions at alternative positions would have resulted in vastly disfavoured 4-membered rings. Then, oxidation of dione 5 with $\mathrm { S e O } _ { 2 }$ generated tetraone 6. Although the structure of compound 6 could not be properly characterized due to its poor solubility in most organic solvents, subsequent oxidation into diacid anhydride, followed by imidization by amines leading to the targeted PyDI products, clearly confirmed the identities of compounds 5 and 6. The structures of thus obtained 1,5,6, 10-PyDI derivatives were fully characterized with NMR, mass spectroscopy and elemental analyses. The simple aromatic proton resonance signals shown in the $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ NMR spectra unambiguously proved the centrosymmetric 1,5,6,10-substituting pattern of the obtained PyDIs (see the ESI†). The very large chemical shifts exhibited by the three aromatic protons between 8.7 and $9 . 3 \ \mathrm { p p m }$ also evidenced the strongly electron-withdrawing effect of the diimide functionality.

When linear hexyl side chains were attached to the nitrogen atoms in the dicarboximide groups, the molecule displayed good crystallinity. Yellow needle-like crystals of PyDI-C6 were obtained upon slow evaporation of a chloroform–methanol binary solvent. As shown in Fig. 2a and Table S1 $\left( \mathrm { E S I \dag } \right)$ , the single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis clearly depicted the molecular structure of PyDI-C6 to possess a completely rigid and planar polycyclic scaffold, consisting of a pyrene skeleton installed with two 6-membered dicarboximide groups via the 1, 5, 6, and 10 positions. The molecule crystallizes in a triclinic lattice with a $P \bar { 1 }$ space group. The adjacent molecules assume a slipped face-to-face packing along the $c$ axis, which is normal to the aromatic plane (Fig. 2b). A short stacking distance of $3 . 2 0 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ evidences very strong $\pi - \pi$ interactions among the molecules (Fig. 2c). It is also worth noting that a partial brick-wall packing motif is manifested, which has been suggested to promote enhanced transfer integrals and facilitate the charge transport capability.38 Besides the strong $\pi - \pi$ stacking interactions exhibited among vertically aligned molecules, $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { H } { \mathrm { - } } \cdot { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { O }$ type hydrogen bonding (H-bonding) interactions are observed between molecules from neighboring stacks (Fig. 2d). Short distances of $2 . 5 { - } 2 . 6 \mathrm { ~ \normalfont ~ \AA ~ }$ are measured between the aromatic protons on the pyrene skeleton and the imide carbonyl oxygen atoms from the adjacent molecule, and the $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { H } { \cdot } \cdot \cdot \mathrm { O }$ bond angles around $1 5 0 ^ { \circ }$ are also supportive to the H-bond appearance. Both the slipped $\pi - \pi$ stacking and the occurrence of $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { H } { \cdot } \cdot \cdot \mathrm { O }$ H-bonds presumably reflect the electron-deficiency of the PyDI skeleton by virtue of the diimide functionality.

As PyDI-C6 is highly crystalline, unsurprisingly it shows very limited solubility in common organic solvents, which renders difficulty with polymer preparations. Hence, alternative side chains are examined. Based on our previous experiences with

PDI and NDI polymers, branched 2-octyldodecyl groups are selected, as such chain lengths and the $\beta$ -branching character are expected to confer improved solubility without significantly dampening the intermolecular stacking ability. PyDI-C8,12 is thus synthesized, which indeed exhibits much better solubility compared to its linear-side-chain analogue.

Next, the electronic and electrochemical properties are investigated (Fig. 3 and Fig. S1, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ). Both PyDIs display substantial bathochromic shifts in the UV-vis absorptions compared to pyrene,39 with major peaks observed between 350 and $4 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Their emission spectra clearly corroborate the shape persistency of the PyDI chromophore, by presenting a nearly perfect mirror reflection of the transitions arising from the lowest excited state, with well resolved 0–0, 0–1, and 0–2 vibronic peaks. Moreover, very small Stokes shifts of merely ca. $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ are observed with both PyDI-C6 and PyDI-C8,12, along with emission quantum yields of 0.63 and 0.58, respectively. Cyclic voltammetry (CV, Fig. S1, ESI†) depicts two reversible reduction processes, with the half-wave potentials shown at ca. $- 0 . 5 3$ and $- 0 . 8 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ (vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } } )$ ). The corresponding LUMO levels are determined to be around $- 3 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for both PyDIs, which are lowered by 0.2 to $0 . 4 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ compared to those previously reported for other 1,5,6,10-PyDI isomers.26,28 The lowered LUMO is favourable for both chemo-stability and electron-injection susceptibility, required by the n-type semiconducting ability.31

As both the planar geometry and strong intermolecular interactions are beneficial to the charge-transport capability exhibited by 1,5,6,10-PyDI, we then set out to develop conjugated polymers with it, aimed for electron-transporting applications.40 In order to obtain regio-regular conjugated polymers, a suitably bisfunctionalized PyDI of centrosymmetry is necessary. Under typical bromination conditions, carbons 3 and 8 in the non-K regions in the meta-position to the carbonyl groups are observed to undergo facile substitution reactions with optimal selectivity (Scheme 2). In the presence of diBr-PyDI, various comonomers can be used to make conjugated PyDI polymers. Bithiophene is one of the first choices, because of its reputation for fostering superior semiconducting properties. Its centrosymmetric geometry is also suggested to bestow more linear chain conformations and hence higher long-range order in chain packing. Another comonomer unit examined is thiophenediyl.

Since we expect 1,5,6,10-PyDI to exhibit a stronger packing tendency than NDI, due to its enlarged polycyclic skeleton, a zig-zagged chain geometry, entailed by the nonlinear thiophenediyl connectivity, is investigated for modulating the chain packing propensity. The two polymers are then prepared by Stille crosscoupling between diBr-PyDI and distannyl-substituted thiophene or bithiophene. Just as predicted, PyDI-T exhibits noticeably higher solubility than PyDI-TT in common organic solvents, such tetrahydrofuran and toluene, while the latter is only soluble in chloroform at room temperature. The number-average molecular weights $\left( M _ { \mathrm { n } } \boldsymbol { s } \right)$ of PyDI-T and PyDI-TT were determined to be 44.8 and $5 2 . 4 \mathrm { \ k D a }$ , respectively (Table 1). Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) reveal the excellent thermal stability of the two polymers, with decomposition temperatures of over $4 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , below which no obvious phase transitions are detected by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Fig. S3, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ).

In chloroform solution, a long-wavelength absorption band is exhibited by both PyDI-T and PyDI-TT, with maxima observed at around 570 and $6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ n m }$ , respectively. The substantial absorption bathochromic shifts exhibited by these polymers relative to the PyDI monomer prove the effective $\pi$ -conjugative extension realized by the PyDI-3,8-diyl unit, while the lowenergy broad band suggests the charge transfer feature between the (bi)thiophene and PyDI moieties within the polymer backbones. It is also worth noting that such absorption wavelengths shown by PyDI-T and PyDI-TT are considerably longer than that of a previously reported conjugated polymer consisting of an alternative PyDI isomer,29 featuring five-membered dicarboximide rings. The maximum extinction coefficient of PyDI-TT in the lower-energy region is comparable to those of NDI- and PDI-based polymer analogues (Fig. S4, ESI†). Between the two new polymers, the absorption of PyDI-TT is shifted to even longer wavelengths compared to that of PyDI-T, which might be attributed to the stronger electron-donating ability of bithiophene.41 In the thin-film state, while the absorption maxima remain roughly the same as in solution, the band shapes are clearly broadened, showing evident tails reaching over 900 and $1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for PyDI-T and PyDI-TT, respectively. A similar trend of bandgap narrowing was also observed with NDI polymers. According to the CV profiles collected from the thin films, very similar LUMO levels at $- 3 . 8 5$ and $- 3 . 8 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ are detected with PyDI-T and PyDI-TT, respectively. Combined with the corresponding optical bandgap values estimated from the absorption spectra, the HOMOs of the two polymers are calculated to be around $- 5 . 6 5$ and $- 5 . 3 9 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ (Fig. 3c and Fig. S2, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations are then conducted at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level to help understand the frontier orbital features of 1,5,6,10-PyDI and its polymers. Conforming to the crystal structure, the energy minimized 1,5,6,10-PyDI is depicted with a completely planar polycyclic skeleton (Fig. 2a). While the HOMO is mostly located with the pyrene moiety, the LUMO is distributed over the larger PyDI framework. The calculated HOMO and LUMO levels are in good agreement with the experimental data. For the polymers, the HOMO and LUMO are demonstrated to be delocalized over a couple of repeat units in both PyDI-T and PyDI-TT (Fig. S5, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ), substantiating the effective $\pi$ -extension ability of the PyDI-3,8-diyl segment. Additionally, the dihedral angles between the PyDI and thiophene units in PyDI-T and PyDI-TT are calculated to be smaller compared to those exhibited by the NDI- and PDI-based polymer analogues, suggesting improved backbone planarity of PyDI-based polymers (Fig. S5, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ . Further insights into the absorption properties of PyDI-T and PyDI-TT are unveiled by the time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level (Fig. S7–S10, $\mathrm { E S I \dag }$ ). In general, the absorption properties of the two polymers are well reproduced by the TD-DFT data. For PyDI-T, the low-energy absorption band is suggested to comprise mixed transitions of ${ \mathrm { S } } _ { 0 }  { \mathrm { S } } _ { 1 } , { \mathrm { S } } _ { 0 }  { \mathrm { S } } _ { 3 } ,$ , and $\mathrm { S } _ { 0 }  \mathrm { S } _ { 4 }$ , with $\mathrm { S } _ { 0 }  \mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ being the major contributor to the oscillator strength. For PyDI-TT, the long-wavelength absorption is dominated by a single transition of $\mathrm { S } _ { 0 }  \mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ . Moreover, the calculated isosurfaces of holes and electrons in the excited states also corroborate the intramolecular charge transfer feature in the two polymers.42,43

Next, organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) are fabricated with the two PyDI polymers to investigate their semiconducting capabilities. Using an amorphous fluoropolymer (CYTOP, ca. $5 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ thick) as the dielectric layer and phenylethanethioltreated Au electrodes, a top-gate/bottom-contact (TG/BC) device configuration (Fig. S11, ESI†) was used. The top-gate dielectric layer effectively helps protect the active layer from moisture and oxygen, favouring the device stability. On the basis of the device output and transfer characteristics under optimized conditions, both PyDI-T and PyDI-TT exhibit unipolar n-channel field-effect behaviours with minimal hysteresis (Fig. 4 and Table 2). Remarkably, upon annealing at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ the thin film of PyDITT exhibits an electron mobility of up to $0 . 3 3 5 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \bf V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \bf \Phi } _ { S } ^ { - 1 }$ with good current modulation $( 1 0 ^ { 4 } – 1 0 ^ { 5 } )$ . Such a value is among the best performance of the reported solution-processed n-type semiconducting polymers,1,44–47 whereas PyDI-T displays a decent electron mobility of $0 . 0 8 7 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ under similar conditions. It is noted that the OFETs are operated under ambient conditions, which indicates the optimal air stability of the polymer devices. All these results demonstrate that the 1,5,6,10-PyDI unit bearing two six-membered dicarboximide groups offers favourable electronic effects and suitable molecular packing motifs for stabilizing negative charges and transporting electrons. Provided with suitable linkers, additional high-performance n-type semiconductive polymer materials may be developed.

The thin-film morphologies of these polymers are analyzed by the grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) technique. The 2D-GIWAXS patterns are shown in Fig. 5 and the corresponding 1D out-of-plane and in-plane scattering profiles with crystallographic parameters are provided in the ESI† (Fig. S12, S13 and Table S2). For PyDI-TT, based on the (100) diffraction peak along the out-of-plane direction, the ${ \mathrm { d - d } }$ distance of the polymers is estimated to be $2 3 . 5 \mathrm { \AA } .$ , and the $\pi - \pi$ stacking distance calculated from the (010) peak is about $3 . 7 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ , which is typical for conjugated polymers showing high carrier mobilities.48 In particular, the long-range diffraction $\left( h 0 0 \right)$ and $\pi - \pi$ stacking (010) peaks are both observed in the out-of-plane direction, suggesting the mixed edge-on and face-on orientations of the crystallites.48,49 While the edge-on orientation is suggested to offer high electron mobilities according to the conventional charge-hoping theory, the bimodal textures are more recently regarded as favourable microstructures for OFET performance, presumably due to the formation of 3D carrier transporting channels.50,51 In addition, secondary diffraction peaks (200) are recorded in the in-plane direction, indicating the high crystallinity of this polymer. The GIWAXS patterns obtained from the PyDI-T film is evidently different from those recorded from PyDI-TT. A ring-like scattering feature suggests that the crystallites are likely oriented randomly with respect to the substrate, which is probably one of the reasons accounting for the less desirable electron-transporting performance of this material.

# Conclusions

In summary, we report herein the syntheses of 1,5,6,10- pyrenetetracarboxyl diimides (PyDIs) for the first time. A single crystal structure is obtained, which unambiguously proves the centrosymmetric and completely planar molecular geometry of the 1,5,6,10-PyDI skeleton. By virtue of the two six-membered dicarboximide groups installed around the PAH framework, the LUMO of 1,5,6,10-PyDIs is perceivably lowered compared with previously reported PyDI regio-isomers. Moreover, site-selective functionalization of 1,5,6,10-PyDI is proven viable and by applying 3,8-dibromo-1,5,6,10-PyDI as the key building block to cross-couple with thiophene and bithiophene derivatives, two conjugated PyDI polymers are obtained via facile polymerizing procedures. The absorption spectroscopy, CV and DFT calculations all provide supportive evidence for the highly conjugated backbone possessed by the polymers, indicating the effective $\pi$ -extension capacity of 1,5,6,10-PyDI. In solutionprocessed OFETs, both polymers are shown to exhibit n-channel semiconducting properties. In particular, PyDI-TT displays an impressive electron mobility of $0 . 3 3 5 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ . Such performance qualifies the material among the best solution-processible n-type semiconductive polymers. All these results have demonstrated the great potential of 1,5,6,10-PyDI as a potent new building block in developing high-performance electron-transporting materials.
