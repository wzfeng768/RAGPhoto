# A Room-Temperature Processable PDI-Based Electron-Transporting Layer for Enhanced Performance in PDI-Based Non-Fullerene Solar Cells

Jiangsheng Yu, Yuyin Xi, Chu-Chen Chueh,\* Dongbing Zhao, Francis Lin, Lilo D. Pozzo, Weihua Tang,\* and Alex K.-Y. Jen\*

In this study, it is demonstrated that a facile room-temperature processed amine functionalized perylene-diimide (PDIN) can function as an efficient electron-transporting layer (ETL) to enhance the photovoltaic performance of inverted PDI-based non-fullerene solar cells. It is showed that the PDIN ETL possesses respectable mobilities and interfacial doping capability to the PDI-based acceptors to facilitate the charge transport and extraction in the devices. Moreover, it can modulate the morphological evolution of the bulkheterojunction (BHJ) atop to result in a more crystalline, face-on orientation because of its improved compatibility to PDI-based acceptors. Consequently, the PDIN ETL enables the derived devices to have $10 \%$ higher power conversion efficiency (PCE) than the reference device. In addition, the PDIN can also be used as a surface modifier on $ \tt z n o$ to result in a ${ \approx } 1 4 \%$ enhanced PCE than that of the pristine ZnO-based device. This study shows the feasibility of modulating the BHJ of non-fullerene organic photovoltaics by rationally selected ETLs to facilitate exciton dissociation and collection of the devices.

# 1. Introduction

The rapid progress of non-fullerene organic photovoltaics (OPVs) has shown their promise as an alternative to traditional fullerene-based OPVs since the non-fullerene acceptors have better tunability in spectral absorption, frontier energy levels, molecular design, and cost-effective synthesis compared to the fullerenes.[1-7] Among the diverse non-fullerene acceptors developed so far, perylene-diimide (PDI) based compounds are among the most frequently exploited ones for high-performance non-fullerene OPVs.I5-12] At present, the best PDI-based non-fullerene OPVs show high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of ${ > } 8 \%$ demonstrating their great potential for solar energy applications.5,10] The main contribution to this advancement lies on the optimization of the PDI acceptors based bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) morphology through rational molecular design.[6]

To realize a high-performance OPV, interfacial materials is the area that requires special attention in addition to the photoactive semiconductor layer.[13]

The employment of proper charge-transporting interlayers (CTLs) can facilitate charge transport and collection in devices by effectively alleviating the interfacial energy barriers.[13] Furthermore, the surface energy and texture of CTL also play an important role in modulating the morphological evolution and vertical phase separation of atop BHJ. It has been proven in the fullerene-based OPVs that this BHJ morphological change can significantly affect the resultant charge dissociation and transport in the derived devices.[13] To date, versatile solution processable CTLs including inorganic[14] and organic semiconducting materials[15-20] have been vigorously exploited for accomplish efficient non-fullerene OPVs. However, the study encompassing the optimization of BHJ morphology enabled by appropriate CTLs is seldom reported.

Recently, the development of water/alcohol-soluble organic electron-transporting layers (ETLs) have been recognized as an efficient way to simplify device fabrication process and improve their efficiencies.21,22] Among the reported ETLs, the fullerene, poly[(9,9-bis $3 ^ { \prime }$ (N,N-dimethylamino) propyl)-2,7-fluorene)-alt2,7-(9,9-dioctylfluorene)] (PFN)-, and PDI-based derivatives are the most commonly used ones to date. For example, we have developed a series of fulleropyrrolidines surfactants that serve as effective ETLs to enhance the device performance.13,23,24] Huang and co-workers and Wu et al. have synthesized versatile PFN-based derivatives to serve as efficient water/alcohol-soluble electrode buffer layers in OPVs.[15,19,21,22,25] Li and co-workers have reported amine group functionalized fullerene derivatives and an amine functionalized PDI (PDIN) to serve as thickness-insensitive cathode interlayers to yield high-performance devices.20,26] Nonetheless, all these organic ETLs are mainly employed in the conventional configuration (Indium tin oxide (ITO)/hole-transporting layer (HTL)/BHJ/ETL/electrode).

Currently, most of the high-performance PDI-based nonfullerene OPVs are realized in the inverted structure (ITO/ETL/ BHJ/HTL/electrode), wherein zinc oxide $( \mathrm { Z n O } )$ is commonly used ETL due to its high electron mobility, suitable energy levels, and high transparency across the UV-visible spectral range. However, it possesses several deficiencies, including surface charge traps and incompatibility to organic materials, which need to be further improved.[27,28] We have previously demonstrated that by passivating ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ surface with a fullerenebased self-assembled monolayer $\left( \mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 } . \mathrm { S A M } \right)$ could improve PCE due to enhanced electron coupling at the $\mathrm { Z n O / B H }$ interface and better compatibility between $\mathrm { Z n O }$ ETL and the organic active layer.29-31] Considering the facile processability of the aforementioned organic ETLs and the influence of their surface energy and texture on the atop BHJ morphology, it is feasible to apply them to inverted non-fullerene OPV to replace or modify ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ for further enhancing device performance.

As discussed earlier, the PDI-based acceptors are the most frequently used building blocks for high-performance nonfullerene OPVs. In this regard, the PDIN with two alkylamino side-chains are soluble in alcohol/acetic acid and can serve as an efficient bottom ETL for improving the photovoltaic performance of inverted PDI-based OPVs because of its self-doping characteristics in solid-state $\mathrm { f l m } ^ { \left[ 3 2 \right] }$ and the similar molecular geometry to PDI acceptors. Therefore, PDIN can potentially enhance electron extraction in devices and provide the ability to modulate the BHJ evolution simultaneously to improve OPV device performance.

In this study, the efficacy of PDIN ETL in enhancing the performance of inverted PDI-based OPVs has been unveiled. By using PDIN as the bottom ETL, it can effectively modulate the atop BHJ morphology to result in more favorable out-of-plane $\pi - \pi$ stacking and associated lamellar orientation. The decent charge-transporting properties in PDIN ETL can endow the derived device with $10 \%$ higher PCE than the reference ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ -based device. In addition, the PDIN can also be used as a surface modifier on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ to further enhance PCE by $14 \%$ compared to that of the pristine ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ -based device.

The recombination mechanisms in these devices are further investigated by studying the light intensity $( P _ { 0 } )$ dependence of $J { - } V$ characteristics.31,34,35] In principle, there are two main recombination mechanisms existing in the BHJ based devices: monomolecular and bimolecular recombinations. Monomolecular recombination involves one free carrier at a time and is synonymous with any first order process while bimolecular recombination is known to obey the Langevin expression and refers to the recombination of free electrons and holes in the BHJ layer.[36] The recombination in OPV devices is dependent on the external voltage applied to the device, for which the $J { - } V$ curves are affected by the monomolecular recombination from the short-cicuit condition to the maximum power point and the bimolecular recombination in the range of voltages from the maximum power point to the open-circuit condition.

The variations of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ as a function of $P _ { 0 }$ were studied and modeled with the equation of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } } \propto P _ { 0 } ^ { \alpha }$ It has been documented that $\alpha$ will be close to 1 under short-circuit condition if the bimolecular recombination is negligible since all the generated excitons are swept out prior to recombination. Figure 3a shows the dependence of $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ on $P _ { 0 }$ of the studied devices, in which the fitted slope $( \alpha )$ by the power law is 0.895, 0.902, and 0.913 for the ${ \mathrm { Z n O } } .$ , PDIN-, and ZnO/PDIN-based device, respectively. This result affirms that the PDIN ETL can reduce bimolecular recombination in the derived device due to enhanced charge extraction across the PDIN/BHJ interface.

Figure 3b shows the $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of the studied devices varies logarithmically with light intensity $( \mathrm { l n } P _ { 0 } )$ . In principle, the slope of $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ versus $\mathrm { l n } P _ { 0 }$ will be equal to $2 \ k T / q$ if the monomolecular recombination is dominant while the slope will be equal to $k T / q$ if bimolecular recombination governs. As shown, all devices possess a similar $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ dependence on $\mathrm { l n } P _ { 0 }$ with a slope of $1 . 1 ~ k T / q$ at high light intensities (25 to $1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ m W ~ c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ). However, the pristine $\mathrm { Z n O }$ -based device exhibits an increased slope of $1 . 4 ~ k T / q$ at low light intensities (1 to $1 0 \mathrm { m W } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ , revealing the existence of trap-assisted recombination in the device. The PDIN-based device shows a smaller slope of $1 . 2 k T / q$ at low light intensities while the ZnO/PDIN-based device shows the slope of $1 . 1 ~ k T / q$ at low light intensities. This result clearly shows that the PDIN ETL can effectively alleviate interfacial trap-assisted recombination in device to improve device performance.

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is also employed to understand the charge transfer efficiency between the studied ETLs and the used photoactive components. As shown in Figure 3c,d, ${ \approx } 8 1 \%$ of B-DIPDI fluorescence or ${ \approx } 8 9 \%$ of PTB7-Th fluorescence is quenched after depositing the derived BHJ film on $\mathrm { Z n O }$ ETL. If PDIN is used as the ETL, the degree of PL quenching is notably increased in the derived BHJ films, which unequivocally manifests the enhanced charge transfer between the PDIN/BHJ interface. The PL lifetime of the BHJ films deposited on the studied ETLs is also evaluated by using time-correlated single photo counting technique (Figure S1, Supporting Information). As shown, the BHJ film grown on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ ETL possesses a PL lifetime of 1.70 ns and it is further reduced to $< 0 . 4$ ns when PDIN is used as the ETL. The shorter PL lifetime also verifies the more efficient charge transfer between the BHJ/PDIN interface over the BHJ/ZnO interface.[37,38]

After understanding the influence of the PDIN ETL on enhancing the eletronic properties of the device, we next investigated its possible impacts on atop BHJ morphology. Considering the molecular geometry of PDIN might improve compatibility to the PDI-based acceptor in BHJ than ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ the thin-film morphology of B-DIPDI photoactive component grown on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ and PDIN layers was first investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM) for comparison. As shown in Figure 4a,b, the B-DIPDI film deposited on PDIN layer shows a more homogeneous morphology than the film grown on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ layer, wherein the root-mean-squared (RMS) surface roughness of PDIN and ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ samples is 0.55 and $1 \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively.

The PTB7-Th:B-DIPDI BHJ films deposited on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ and PDIN layers are also characterized by AFM as shown in Figure 4c,d. Similar to the morphological variation observed in pristine B-DIPDI film, a more homogeneous and smoother surface morphology can also be observed in the BHJ film grown on PDIN than ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ layer, wherein the RMS surface roughness of PDIN and ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ samples is 0.82 and $1 . 5 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ respectively. This result reveals that the PDIN ETL can effectively modulate the atop BHJ film morphology to result in enhanced exciton diffusion/dissociation and charge transport in the derived devices.

To obtain more in-depth structural information in the formed BHJ films, grazing incident wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement are conducted on the BHJ films and the pristine PTB7-Th or B-DIPDI films deposited on $\mathrm { Z n O }$ and PDIN layers. As shown in Figure 5a,b, the BHJ film grown on PDIN layer shows a more defined lamellar peak at $q = \approx 0 . 3 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ compared to that on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ layer, suggesting an improved crystallinity of the BHJ film when coated over PDIN layer. This may be attributed to the crystallinity and oriented surface texture of PDIN layer and to an improved compatibility with the PDI-based BHJ film. The scattering pattern of pristine PDIN film and other datasets are presented in Figures S2 and S3 (Supporting Information).

The GIWAXS images of the pristine PTB7-Th and B-DIPDI films deposited over ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ and PDIN layers are shown in Figure 5c,d and Figure 5e,f, respectively. It is clear that the PDIN sub-layer promotes improved ordering for both photoactive components when compared to a $\mathrm { Z n O }$ layer. It is also interesting to note that PTB7-Th film grown on PDIN layer (Figure 5d) exhibits a higher intensity lamellar correlation peak along the in-plane direction than along the out-of-plane direction. This suggests that there is significant orientation of the polymer chains in a face-on configuration. In contrast, the PTB7-Th film grown on ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ shows a random distribution of polymer chains. The presence of a clear $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking peak at $q = { \approx } 1 . 5 9 \ \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ also demonstrates that the polymer maintains a highly crystalline structure. This suggests that a crystalline face-on orientation is facilitated when coated over a PDIN layer, which results in improved out-of-plane charge transport in devices.

It is known that a thicker BHJ layer facilitates more efficient light-harvesting and easier printing process.39,40] As shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information), the PDINbased device shows less dependence on the BHJ thickness than the $\mathrm { Z n O }$ -based device. Forming a thicker ETL layer in OPVs is advantageous to large-area manufacturing process.[21] As summarized in Table S2 (Supporting Information) wherein an optimized BHJ thickness of $8 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ is used, the PDIN ETL shows a reasonable thickness tolerance. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have systematically investigated the potential of using a PDI-based ETL to enhance the performance of PDI-based non-fullerene OPVs. We demonstrated that a water/ alcohol-soluble and room-temperature processable PDIN ETL can effectively improve the PCE of an inverted PDI-based solar cell. On one hand, the PDIN ETL possesses respectable charge-transporting properties and possible interfacial doping capability to the PDI-based acceptors to improve the charge transport and extraction of the derived devices. On the other hand, it can modulate the BHJ morphology atop due to its better compatibility with PDI-based acceptors. The GIWAXS results clearly reveal that a more crystalline face-on orientation of the BHJ film can be achieved when a PDIN ETL was used. As a result, the PDIN ETL can enable the derived device with a $10 \%$ higher PCE than that of the reference $\mathrm { Z n O }$ -based device. Besides, it can also be used as a surface modifier on $\mathrm { Z n O }$ to result in a ${ \approx } 1 4 \%$ higher PCE than the pristine $\mathrm { Z n O }$ -based device. This study shows the feasibility of modulating the BHJ of OPVs by proper selection of ETL to facilitate exciton dissociation and collection for enhancing device performance.

Where $J$ is the dark current density $( \mathsf { m A } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ $\mu _ { 0 }$ is the zerofield mobility $( \mathsf { c } \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ $\varepsilon _ { 0 }$ is the permittivity of free space, $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { r } }$ is the relative permittivity of the material, $V$ is the effective voltage $( V = V _ { \mathsf { A p p l i e d } } - V _ { \mathsf { B u i l t - i n } } - V _ { \mathsf { s e r i e s \ r e s i s t a n e } } )$ , and $L$ is the thickness of the active layer.

GIWAxs Measurement: GIwAXS measurements were performed on an Anton Paar SAXSess instrument. The X-ray source is a $\mathsf { C u } \ \mathsf { K } \alpha$ tube with a wavelength of $1 . 5 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA } .$ Samples were prepared by spincoating layers over either ZnO/Si or PDIN/Si substrates with an area of $1 . 5 \ : \mathsf { c m } \times 1 . 5 \ : \mathsf { c m }$ . Samples were aligned with an angle of $0 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ relative to the main beam. The 2D scattering pattern was recorded using image plates over $\rceil 5 \mathrm { h }$ of integration. The data were corrected by accounting for the Distorted Wave Born Approximation during GlwAxs data reduction. Raw images were reduced to a $Q \gamma Q z$ plane using a customized IGOR Pro reduction macro with scattering intensities normalized to total scattering time.[41]
