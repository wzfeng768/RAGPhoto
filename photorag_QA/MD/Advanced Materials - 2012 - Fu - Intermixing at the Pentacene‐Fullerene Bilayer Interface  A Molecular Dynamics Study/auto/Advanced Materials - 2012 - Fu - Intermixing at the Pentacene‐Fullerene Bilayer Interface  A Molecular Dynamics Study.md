# Intermixing at the Pentacene-Fullerene Bilayer Interface: A Molecular Dynamics Study

Yao-Tsung Fu, Chad Risko, and Jean-Luc Bredas\*

Organic photovoltaics (OPVs) hold promise as a technology for low-cost, large-area power conversion.1-4] Though conceptually straightforward, the processes required for efficient operation of an organic solar cell -- photon absorption and exciton (electron-hole pair) formation, dissociation of the exciton into separated charges, and collection of the charges - are inherently complex due to the characteristics of the $\pi$ -conjugated organic materials, i.e., weak van der Waals intermolecular interactions, low dielectric constants, strong electron-electron interactions, and large electron-vibration couplings. The effective separation of the electron-hole pair requires that the active layer consist of two components, an electron-rich (hole-transport) donor material and an electron-deficient (electron-transport) acceptor material.ll This condition leads to a key bottleneck, both in terms of operation and basic understanding, as there exists a delicate interplay between charge-separation and (geminate and non-geminate) charge-recombination processes at the interface between the donor and acceptor materials.

While the importance of the donor-acceptor interface has long been recognized, recent experimental evidence has shone new light on the morphological complexity; this is particularly the case in polymer-fullerene bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) solar cells,[6,7] and more recently in molecule-molecule and polymermolecule bilayers.[8,9] In addition to the possible miscibility of the two components, the deposition protocol (e.g., chemical vapor deposition, solution casting, or spin coating to name a few(2,10) and post-processing procedures (e.g., removal of solvent additives or solvent and thermal annealing[11,12) can impact the interface morphology. This morphological variability leads to difficulties, then, in recognizing the underlying physical processes at the interface as the relevant electronic states and electrostatic interactions are highly dependent on the molecular packing configurations between the donor and acceptor molecules or chain segments.3,13-16,17] Hence, to achieve optimal OPV performance, one must be able to understand and ultimately control the morphology of these heterojunctions.[12]

OPVs based solely on small molecule donors and acceptors have recently shown considerable improvement,[4,18] with power conversion efficiencies for (solution-processed) singlejunction devices reaching $6 . 7 \% ^ { [ 1 9 ] }$ and (vacuum-deposited) tandem devices surpassing $1 0 . 7 \%$ [20] Small-molecule active layers employing pentacene as the electron donor and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ as the electron acceptor have served as prototype systems to detail a number of key features of OPVs.[21,22]

Due to the relatively small size and rigid structural characteristics of these molecules, the pentacene. $C _ { 6 0 }$ interface has also undergone a number of theoretical investigations, ranging from electronic-structure calculations on molecular complexes to atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.13-15,23,24-27] Regarding the latter, different configurations of pentacene and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ have been considered. Cantrell and Clancy[23,25] performed a series of studies of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ deposited on pentacene layers to examine the (anisotropic) diffusion of a single fullerene in the valleys of a corrugated pentacene landscape and to investigate the formation of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ nanowires along pentacene step edges; importantly, Cantrell and Clancy have shown that the thin-film phase of pentacene has a more energetically isotropic surface than the bulk phase, which results in more ordered fullerene film growth. Muccioli et al. have modeled the vapor-phase deposition and growth of the first layers of pentacene on the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ (001) surface.[27] The growth of pentacene on the fullerene surface was shown to include two coverage-dependent steps for the monolayer: the pentacene molecules initially lie flat (parallel/horizontal orientation) in a disordered fashion on the fullerene surface through approximately six-tenths of a monolayer, followed by a collective reorientation of the pentacene molecules into a vertical (perpendicular) orientation on the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ surface and the eventual onset of the herringbone packing configuration.28] Finally, Zheng et al.24] examined intimately intermixed pentacene $C _ { 6 0 }$ structures that could be found in a BHJ film; interestingly, the simulations began to show initial stages of phase segregation.

Our goal in the present work is to investigate via MD simulations whether the nature of the pentacene surface - either the (001) surface where the molecules stand upright (nearly perpendicular) to the surface, or the (010) surface for which the molecules lie parallel to the surface - plays a role in the formation of the interface upon deposition of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ (we recall that in conventional pentacene- $C _ { 6 0 }$ bilayer devices, the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ layer is indeed deposited on top of the pentacene layer). Also, we consider what consequence terraced/ stepped pentacene surfaces may have. We note that the formation of terraced/stepped surfaces is determined by the surface energy, roughness, and temperature of the substrate on which the pentacene film is deposited along with deposition temperature and rates.[29,30]

Our findings, consistent with the "burrowing' picture of Cantrell and Clancy,[25] indicate that, when the pentacene (010) face is open to interact with $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ the interface can become significantly disordered and lead to intermixing between the two components. Hence, the heterojunction takes on a more graded or mixed composition8,9,31,32] vs. the strict planar bilayer structure often depicted.[15]

Our atomistic MD simulations are based on the MM3 force field of Allinger and co-workers,[7,32,33] as this force field has been shown to be applicable to the study of pentacene and pentacene $C _ { 6 0 }$ interfaces.23,25] To derive models of the pentacene (001) and (010) surfaces, we started with the pentacene unit cells structures of Holmes et al.[34] Super cells, up to $1 0 \times 8 \times$ 5 $( 6 . 3 \ \mathrm { n m } \times 6 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m } \times 7 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ , were constructed and employed to model sufficiently large, flexible layers. The super-cell periodicity was kept within the xy plane, while the z-direction was elongated to $2 2 . 8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ to create cleaved surfaces (vs. the bulk structure). MD simulations in all instances were equilibrated for $6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { p s }$ with a further 900 ps for data collection. We note that while this time scale may seem somewhat limited at first sight, all atoms/molecules in the simulation are fully allowed to move and the standard deviation of the potential energy during the data collection is less than $1 \%$ $( - 2 0 \mathrm { \ k c a l / m o l } )$ , indicating that the time scale is reasonable; in addition, vida infra, the main findings from these simulations are well reached within this time frame. The simulations were carried out under the NVT ensemble using the modified Beeman integrator with a time step of 1 fs, with temperature (298 K) and pressure (1 atm) maintained by the Berendsen thermostat and barostat, respectively. A spherical cutoff of $1 . 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the summation of van. der Waals interactions and Ewald summation for Coulomb interactions was used throughout. Further details on the simulations, including the description of the simulations on isolated pentacene surfaces, are given in the Supporting Information. We note that the pentacene bulk phase is well reproduced in the simulations.

As a preliminary step, it is useful to understand the strength of the interactions between pentacene and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ over different scales. We started by analyzing the binding energy of a single pentacene- $C _ { 6 0 }$ complex via energy-minimization calculations (MM3) as a function of distance and pentacene orientation. The calculations indicate, as could be intuitively expected, that pentacene and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ are more strongly bound when the pentacene face is parallel with the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ cage $( - 9 . 4 4 \mathrm { ~ k c a l } / \mathrm { m o l }$ $[ 0 . 4 1 \ \mathrm { e V } ]$ and $- 8 . 9 2 \mathrm { \ k c a l / m o l }$ [0.37 eV] when the pentacene center of mass is aligned with the five- and six-membered ring on the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ respectively) vs. perpendicular $( - 2 . 2 7 \ \mathrm { k c a l } / $ mol and $- 2 . 4 0 \ \mathrm { k c a l / m o l }$ for interactions with the five- and six-membered ring on the $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 } .$ respectively). Analysis of the cohesive (adsorption) energy of a single $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ on the pentacene surfaces of interest is consistent with this picture. We find that $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ preferentially binds to the pentacene (010) face $( 3 3 . 5 \pm 2 \mathrm { \ k c a l / m o l }$ $1 . 4 5 \pm 0 . 0 9 \mathrm { ~ e V } ]$ vs. the (001) face $( 1 8 . 9 ~ \pm$ $2 \mathrm { k c a l / m o l } [ 0 . 8 2 \pm 0 . 0 9 \mathrm { e V } ] )$ ; these trends also agree with previous estimates (that also take into account various polymorphs).[23,25]

In addition, we evaluated the surface energies for pentacene and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ as stronger interfacial interactions between lattices can be expected when the surface energies are most similar. For these calculations, we cleaved the (001) and (010) planes of a multilayer pentacene model (cell size $6 . 3 \ : \mathrm { n m } \times 6 . 5 \ : \mathrm { n m } \times 7 . 5 \ : \mathrm { n m } )$ to form slab models, and then optimized the structures via energy minimization. Total energy averages for an equilibrated bulk pentacene $( \mathrm { E _ { T } } )$ with 3D periodic conditions and the pentacene slabs $( \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { S } } )$ with 2D periodic conditions were determined via MD simulations with an NVT ensemble that included $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { p s }$ for equilibration followed by 400 ps for data collection. The sur. face energy (Equation 1) was then determined by the relation:

$$
E = \left( E _ { S } - E _ { T } \right) / 2 A
$$

where A is the cross-sectional area. A similar procedure was followed for $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$

The computed surface energies show trends similar to those for the complex binding energies and cohesive/adsorption energy for a single $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ on a pentacene surface. For pentacene, the surface energies for the (001) and (010) are 53.0 and 91.8 $\mathrm { m } { } J / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ , respectively;, periodic slab calculations employing density functional theory (DFT) have determined the energies of these faces to be 49.7 and $7 6 . 9 ~ \mathrm { m J } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ , respectively.[35] On the other hand, contact angle measurements have derived a surface energy of approximately $3 4 \sim 3 8 ~ \mathrm { m J } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ for the thin-film pentacene structure that is expected to present mainly an (001) surface with the pentacene molecules standing up;[29,36] the notable differences with respect to the calculations, however, can be due to the difficulty in identifying the exact geometrical parameters of the thin-film pentacene used in the experiment (including the phases, planes, and any surface reconstruction). For $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ the calculated surface energies of the (001) and hexag. onal-close packed (h.c.p.) faces are 98.5 and $8 8 . 0 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { J } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ , respectively (again, there is deviation with the empirically-determined $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ surface energy of approximately $4 2 \ \mathrm { m J } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } )$ [37,38] Based on the entirety of the results from the cohesive/binding energy and surface energy evaluations, we anticipate that $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ interacts more strongly with the pentacene (010) surface than the (001) surface, given their more similar surface energies.

For the predominantly (001) structures $[ \mathsf { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } ]$ the pentacene molecules along the now open (0-10) and (100) faces readily disorder and intermix with the. $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ molecules. There is also disruption of the molecular packing of the pentacene layers underneath the exposure (Figure 4). For the steps created in the pristine (010) interface $[ S _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { S } _ { 4 } ]$ , the degree of disorder and interdiffusion is even larger, as one might expect from the results on the pristine surfaces.

When $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ is deposited on the pentacene (010) surface, however, there is a considerable rearrangement of the upper pentaof terraces. In agreement with a growing number of experimental and theoretical[39] results for polymer-fullerene blends and molecule-molecule and polymer-molecule bilayer architectures, we find large degrees of disorder and intermixing among the pentacene and $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ molecules depending on the crystalline face on which the fullerenes are deposited and the presence of terraces. This picture is consistent with the results of time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (TOF-SIMs) experiments on copper phthalocyanine $( \mathrm { C u P c } ) – \mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ interfaces that reveal an intermixed layer of $6 . 5 \mathrm { n m }$ when $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ is deposited on CuPc (and $2 . 7 \mathrm { n m }$ when the deposition order is reversed).[8]

Importantly, the "local' interface morphology depicted here will have considerable impact on the electronic processes cene layers within a very short period of time due to the increased intermolecular interactions noted above. The fullerenes tend to sit in the middle portion of the pentacene molecules and eventually 'burrow' or displace the pentacene molecules in the upper layer(s), consistent with previous results of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ on the pentacene (010) surface.[25] Importantly, this leads to interfacial disorder and intermixing within the top pentacene layer, see Figure 2. These results suggest that fullerenes deposited on the (010) surface should lead to a mixed structure right at the interface between the two materials, in stark contrast with what is seen for the pentacene (001) surface.

As mentioned above, the surface energy, roughness, and temperature of the substrate on which the pentacene film is deposited can have a considerable impact on the heterojunction interface as terraced/ stepped surfaces could be prevalent. To consider such situations, we constructed four terraced surfaces - two each for (001) [denoted $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } ]$ and (010) (denoted $\mathrm { S } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { S } _ { 4 , \dag }$ surfaces (see Supporting Information for more details on the surface construction and equilibrium structures)-and placed a random distribution of $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ molecules on top of these surfaces to create the interface. For instance, the rates of exciton dissociation, charge separation, and charge recombination can vary over several orders of magnitude depending on the interfacial packing configurations and resulting electronic couplings between donors and acceptors;[13] also, the rough (i.e., intermixed) or smooth (i.e., clean) character of the interface has been shown to influence substantially the overall power conversion efficiency.[40] Hence, in lieu of the oft-considered planar bilayer structure, structural details such as those evidenced in the present work, definitely need to be taken into account during the investigation of the electronic interfacial processes.
