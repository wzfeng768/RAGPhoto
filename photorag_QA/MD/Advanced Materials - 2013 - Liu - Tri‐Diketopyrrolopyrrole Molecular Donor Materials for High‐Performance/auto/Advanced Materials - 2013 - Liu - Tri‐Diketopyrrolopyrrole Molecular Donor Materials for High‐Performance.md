# Tri-Diketopyrrolopyrrole Molecular Donor Materials for High-Performance Solution-Processed Bulk Heterojunction Solar Cells

Jianhua Liu, Yanming Sun, Preecha Moonsin, Martijn Kuik, Christopher M. Proctor, Jason Lin, Ben B. Hsu, Vinich Promarak, Alan J. Heeger, and Thuc-Quyen Nguyen\*

Interest into molecular donor materials for use in solution processed bulk heterojunction (BHJ) organic solar cells has rapidly grown in the past few years.1-7] This increased attention is largely due to molecular materials' advantages over their polymeric counterparts such as well-defined molecular structures, low batch-to-batch variation and access to more characterization methods,[8-10] as well as their recent achievement of power conversion efficiency (PCE) up to $7 . 9 \%$ [11-14] To absorb the solar spectrum efficiently, these donor molecules are typical composed of high optical density chromophores, e.g., oligothiophenes,[15-17] merocyanines,[18,19] isoindigo,[20,21] squaraines,[22-25] and benzothiadiazole analogues,12,26-28] with molecular architectures comprising alternative push-pull characteristics.

Diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP)or pyrrolo[3,4-b]pyrrole1,4(2H,5H)-dione, a typical industrial pigment chromophore with high optical density,[29,30] has demonstrated its valuable utility as a building block for use in constructing donor molecules due to its facile synthesis from low-cost starting materials, strong electron affinity that enables lower frontier energy levels and ease of structural modification that allows for tailored optoelectronic properties.15-8,31-35] Additionally, DPP derivatives are found to exhibit high carrier mobility in organic thin film transistor (FET) devices.[36] Dictated by the numbers of DPP chromophores involved in the molecular structures, the state-of-the-art DPP-containing donor molecules can be classified into two subgroups: (1) mono-DPP in which one DPP chromophore is included and (2) bis-DPP in which two DPP chromophores flank on a central electron-donating conjugated moiety. Successful usage of a mono-DPP unit was found in 3,6-bis(5-(benzofuran-2-yl)thiophen-2-yl)-2,5-bis(2-ethylhexyl) pyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-dione $( \mathrm { D P P } ( \mathrm { T B F u } ) _ { 2 } )$ which employs a DPP chromophore as the central group and benzofuranyl as end groups.[7,8] When blended with phenyl-C71-butyric acid methyl ester $( \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M } )$ , this mono-DPP molecule produced a solar cell performance with a PCE up to $4 . 8 \%$ . Another highperformance mono-DPP derivative, similar to $\mathrm { D P P } ( \mathrm { T B F u } ) _ { 2 }$ but with C2-pyrene end groups, was demonstrated to produce a solar cell performance with a PCE of $4 . 1 \%$ reported by Frechet and coworkers.[32]

Recent development of DPP-containing donor molecules is mainly focused on extending molecular conjugated backbones to bis-DPPs,37-40] following the rationale that an increase in conjugation into bis-DPP is expected to lower the band gap, increase the absorption, and enhance the charge carrier mobility. For example, Marks et al. reported a donor molecule having a naphthodithiophene central group flanked with two DPP units.41] Using $\mathrm { P C } _ { 6 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as the acceptor, the optimized solar cells exhibited a PCE of up to $4 . 0 6 \%$ . However, thus far, most bis-DPP donor molecules with extended conjugated backbones have not yielded the anticipated enhanced solar cell performances as compared to typical mono-DPPs. The overall efficiencies of DPP-based donor molecules reported in literatures remains less than $5 \%$ Nevertheless, extension of the conjugated backbone to DPP-containing polymers has proven to yield efficient donor materials,[42,43] comprising of solar cells with PCEs up to $6 . 5 \%$ [44] Further developing DPP-containing donor molecules is critical not only with better device performance to fill the performance gap between molecular and polymeric DPP donor materials, but also to guide the synthesis of novel DPP-containing donor molecules.

Here we report on two new high performance DPP-containing donor molecules employing a novel molecular architecture with three DPP chromophores (tri-DPP) in the conjugated backbone (Scheme 1). A key feature of this molecular architecture is the use of a DPP-phenyl group as the central moiety instead of the various electron donating conjugated moieties commonly used in bis-DPPs.[37-41] The two tri-DPP molecules, differing only in the presence of either 2-ethylhexyl (5a) or n-hexyl (5b) alkyl substituents (Scheme 1). The incorporation of an extra electron-deficient DPP unit imparts this series of tri-DPPs with low-lying highest occupied molecular orbital (HOmO) energy levels. Moreover, the resultant phenylthiophene linkages induce a slight twist to the conjugated backbone's conformation, which further deepens the HOMO energy levels and improve the molecules' solubility in organic solvents.33,45] When blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ , 5a and 5b lead to solar cell devices with optimized PCEs up to 4.8 and $5 . 5 \%$ respectively. This transition from bis-DPP to tri-DPP demonstrates a useful approach to synthesize high-performance donor molecules by extending the conjugation length and the pushpull characteristics of the molecule. Additionally we explore the influence of two common solvent additives on morphology and device performance. The results demonstrate that solvent additives play completely different roles on both molecules even with a very subtle structural variation on alkyl substitutions, which hints at a more complex structural dependence than expected. Moreover, our results suggest that tri-DPPs can be regarded as intermediates between conjugated small molecules and polymers, in both optimal device processing conditions and device performance. This new class of materials then indicates an evolution from small molecule to polymeric DPPcontaining materials.

The synthetic routes of tri-DPP molecules are displayed in Scheme 1. The starting materials, two widely-used DPP building blocks (1 and 2), were synthesized via previously reported procedures.5,33] Compound 1 was then converted to the diboronic ester precursor 3 by reacting with bis(pinacolato)diboron via a palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling.46] Compound 2 was subjected to N-bromosuccinamide to afford the mono-bromoniated precursor 4.41] Precursors 3 and 4 were then coupled together under Suzuki condition to form tri-DPP 5. Detailed synthetic procedures and characterization are described in the Supporting Information (SI). Two tri-DPP molecules (5a and 5b) were synthesized with a structural difference of alkyl substitution on central DPP moieties (5a: 2-ethylhexyl, 5b: n-hexyl). Tri-DPP molecules 5a and 5b can be synthesized with high-yield and at low-cost due to the efficiency of the reactions described in Scheme 1 as well as the commercially available starting materials. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPs) was employed to determine the HOMO levels of 5a and 5b (Figure S1, SI). The determined HOMO levels are $- 5 . 4$ and $- 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ for 5a and 5b, respectively, which are deeper than that of most reported DPP-containing molecules and polymers,[37,42] due to the electron-deficient DPP unit employed as the central group in the molecular structures. The lower value of 5a is probably due to its more steric 2-ethylhexyl substitutions on the central DPP unit, resulting in a more twisted conformation in the solid state,33,45] as well as much lower tendency of crystallization in 5a films (vide infra). Such low-lying HOMO levels are expected to increase open circuit voltages $( V _ { \mathrm { O C } } )$ in solar cell devices using tri-DPP molecules as compared to most bis-DPP molecules.

In addition to frontier energy levels, other physical properties as shown in Table 1 were characterized to evaluate the two tri-DPP molecules. The solubility of 5a in chloroform is over $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ ; however, replacing the branched alkyl chains with the linear alkyl chains reduces the solubility substantially to $1 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ for 5b. The melting temperature $( T _ { \mathrm { m } } )$ and crystallization temperature $( T _ { \mathrm { c } } )$ of 5a and 5b were determined from the onset of the differential scanning calorimetry (DsC) thermograms (Figure S2, SI). Both the $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ and $T _ { \mathrm { c } }$ decrease significantly from $2 5 4 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $2 2 8 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and from $2 3 4 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $1 9 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , respectively, when replacing linear alkyl chains with the branched ones.

The optical absorption of 5a and 5b are displayed in Figure 1a. Compound 5a and 5b exhibit a similar Uv-Vis absorption profile in chloroform but different behavior in the solid state. The as-cast film of 5a only possesses a weak redshift compared to its solution absorption. Thermal annealing leads to a significant red-shift and vibronic shoulder peaks. In contrast, compound 5b, for both as-cast and thermal annealed films, exhibit similar absorption profiles with a significant $\lambda _ { \mathrm { o n s e t } }$ red-shift $( \approx 9 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ compared to its solution absorption profile and a strong vibronic shoulder peak. Such differences in optical absorption of the two molecules are caused by the different alkyl substituents. Branched 2-ethylhexyl substitution, compared with n-hexyl substitutions, give 5a a low tendency for crystallization, i.e., high intermolecular disorder in as-cast films, which is associated with no obvious redshift and lack of vibronic shoulder peaks observed in as-cast films and lower $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ and $T _ { \mathrm { c } }$ This difference in crystallinity between 5a and 5b in the film state were confirmed by thin film X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Figure 1b, 5b exhibits distinct diffraction peaks (d spacing: $1 . 5 3 \mathrm { n m }$ ) in the small angle region for both as-cast and annealed films while compound 5a only shows a weak diffraction peak at a similar 20 value after thermal annealing.

The lateral carrier transport properties of 5a and 5b were measured in bottom gate, top contact FET geometry. Both 5a and 5b exhibit ambipolar transport characteristics, which is common for DPP-containing organic materials.47-49] However, the hole mobilities are found to be much higher (4 6 fold) than electron mobilites for both compounds, indicating dominant p-type behavior. At room temperature, the saturation hole and electron mobilities $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } )$ of ${ \boldsymbol { 5 } } \mathbf { a }$ are $4 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $7 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, whereas for 5b, $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ are higher than those of 5a $( \mu _ { \mathrm { h } } = 3 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } = 8 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ of 5a and 5b at different annealing temperatures are displayed in Figure 1c. For 5a, both $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ and $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ increase with temperature and peak at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ then decrease at higher temperature. The $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } }$ of 5b shows a similar trend but the $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } }$ remains constant between $1 0 0 ~ -$ $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Highest carrier transport mobilities (5a: $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } } ~ = ~ 6 . 1 ~ \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 4 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } = 1 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ ; 5b: $\mu _ { \mathrm { h } } = 4 . 3 \times$ $1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mu _ { \mathrm { e } } = 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ were observed after thermal annealing of as-cast films at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Further annealing at higher temperature leads to a gradual decrease in both electron and hole mobilities. The vertical charge carrier transport properties of 5a and 5b (Figures S3,S4) were determined from hole-only diodes using the space charge limited current (SCLC) model. The extracted hole mobilities of as-cast 5a and 5b films are $3 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ and $3 . 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Thermal annealing of 5a film leads to the highest mobility of $4 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { \bar { V } } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and a significant decrease of the mobility $( 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mathrm { ~ c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ at higher temperature. Similarly, the highest mobility of 5b film $( 6 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ is also obtained after a thermal annealing of $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ Higher annealing temperature $( 1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ results in a gradual decrease of the mobility $( 5 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ . Such annealing effects and greater diode mobility of 5b than 5a agree with the corresponding results observed in FET devices and XRD results. The greater mobility of 5b is possibly due to its high tendency of crystallization during film formation, a result of liner alkyl substitutions on the central DPP moiety, as is evidenced by two mono-DPPs with similar structural difference in their alkyl substitutions.33] Moreover, the as-cast and annealed film morphologies of 5a and 5b (Figure S5) were investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM). The as-cast film of 5a exhibits a featureless morphology while that of 5b shows distinctive rod-like crystallites, indicating much greater degree of crystallinity for 5b film. Thermal annealing of 5a greatly changes film morphology as evidenced by the formation of large crystallites and high surface roughness, which is similar to other DPP-containing small molecules.33,47] Compared with 5a, 5b exhibits less morphology variation during thermal annealing due to its high crystallinity and $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ . Overall, compared with other solution-processed molecular donor materials,[8,37,38] the lateral and vertical hole mobilities of the two tri-DPPs are among the highest mobilities reported. Given the low lying HOmO levels, good solubility, narrow optical bandgaps and good hole mobilities, the two tri-DPPs are suitable for use as donors in solution processed BHJ solar cells.

Clearly compounds 5a and 5b show completely different behavior regarding the two solvent additives, even though the two donor molecules only have a subtle structural difference on alkyl substitutions. To understand the effects of solvent additives, the film morphology at different processing conditions were investigated using tapping mode AFM. Figure 3 shows the surface topography of $\mathsf { J a } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ and ${ \bf 5 6 } { \bf : P } { \bf C } _ { 7 1 } { \bf B } { \bf M }$ blends prepared from different conditions. It is found that the $\mathsf { J a } { : } \mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ blend dramatically changes the film morphology. The film without any solvent additives exhibits circular features with large domains $( 3 0 0 - 5 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ , which might hinder the efficient exciton dissociation and carrier transport, resulting in low $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and $F F$ as compared to 5b. The addition of DIO greatly reduces the domains and the film is composed of networks with more defined crystallites. As a result, the corresponding solar cells exhibit a high PCE of $4 . 8 \%$ with a high $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and $F F _ { \ast }$ Different from DIO, the CN additive leads to a film without distinguished phase separation, in line with its poor device performance. The surface roughness of these films at different processing conditions also varies greatly from $2 6 . 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (without additive) $7 . 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $1 \%$ DIO) and $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ $1 \%$ CN), which is associated with the significant variation of the film morphology. Unlike 5a, 5b exhibits a similar film morphology as well as the surface roughness $3 . 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ without solvent additive, $8 . 6 \mathrm { n m }$ with $1 \%$ DIO and $6 . 2 \mathrm { n m }$ with $1 \%$ CN) when blended with $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ as shown in Figure 3d,e.

Compared with the film without solvent additive, $5 \mathrm { b / P C _ { 7 1 } B M }$ films fabricated with DIO solvent additive exhibit more distinctive morphology with interconnected rod-like domains. The improved network morphology leads to a higher $J _ { \mathrm { S C } }$ and hence a high PCE of $5 . 1 \%$ . When CN solvent additive was added into solutions, BHJ films are smoother and more uniform with an average domain size of ${ \approx } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , allowing for efficient charge generation and transfer. Therefore, solar cells incorporating with CN additive generate the highest PCE of $5 . 5 \%$ . Overall, considering the significant difference in the crystallinity of 5a and 5b, we believe the different alkyl substitutions impart the materials with different ability for crystallization (or aggregation) as well as different interaction behavior with solvent additives, leading to distinctive film morphologies and therefore solar cell performance.

A slightly higher $V _ { \mathrm { O C } }$ of 5a devices agrees with its deeper HOMO level due to a more twisted conformation caused by bulky substitutions. Surveying the literature one notices an empirical trend that the $F F$ of solution-processed DPP-containing small molecule BHJ devices increases with molecular size. Typically, mono-DPP compounds were found to have FFs less than $5 0 \% ^ { [ 5 - 8 ] }$ whereas bis-DPP molecules usually exhibit higher FFs.137,38,50] In this work, tri-DPP compounds are found to have FFs exceeding $60 \%$ which is comparable to FFs in polymer BHJ devices. It has been suggested this trend can be explained by the increased hole mobility that comes with the improved $\pi { - } \pi$ intermolecular stacking in molecules with larger conjugated backbones,[32,50] which is consistent with the FET and diode mobilities observed here. Additionally, we notice that the triDPP devices are optimized with higher acceptor content similar to what have been observed for most polymer BHJ solar cells whereas some of high performance solution-processed small molecule BHJ devices are usually optimized at higher donor contents.[7,11-13] In terms of the observed FFs and donoracceptor blend ratios for optimized devices, the tri-DPPs indicate an evolution from small molecules to polymers.

In conclusion, we report the synthesis and characterization of two high-performance DPP-containing molecules for use in solution-processed BHJ solar cells. The two molecules differ structurally by their alkyl substitutions (2-ethylhexyl vs. n-hexyl), but employ the same molecular architecture composed of three DPP chromophores in the conjugated backbone. Intrinsic properties of these tri-DPPs have been characterized to determine frontier energy levels, optical absorption, film crystallinity, lateral and vertical charge carrier transport, and other physical properties. Solar cell devices fabricated from the tri-DPPs and $\mathrm { P C } _ { 7 1 } \mathrm { B M }$ produce a PCE of $5 . 5 \%$ . The effects of solvent additive on the device performance are found to be significantly different for the two tri-DPP molecules due to the structural difference of alkyl substitutions. Such structural difference of alkyl substitutions on the central DPP moiety leads to the two compounds with different tendency for crystallization and probably different intermolecular interaction motif, as evidenced by results from DsC, optical absorption and film XRD, which in turn cause the distinctive film morphology, charge transport ability, and therefore impacts solar cell performance. Our investigation demonstrates that high-performance DPP-containing donor molecules can be achieved through rational design of molecular structures. The success of creating high-performance molecular donor materials by extending bisDPPs to tri-DPPs could possibly be translated to other conjugated systems.
