# synlanti-Oligothienoacene Diimides with up to 10 Fused Rings

Jingjing Wen, Fei Qiu, Han Liu, Xinyue Liu, Hui Hu, Yuxiao Duan, Zhaohui Wang, and Lei Zhang\*

Abstract: We report a facile and powerful strategy to prepare libraries of oligothienoacene dimides that include anti- and syn-isomers using palladium-catalyzed $C$ -H activation and an unexpected 1,2-sulfur migration. Through this strategy, a series of oligothienoacene diimides containing 6, 8, and 10 fused rings were synthesized. The molecular geometry and extent of $\pi$ -conjugation have dramatic effects on the electronic properties, degree of crystallinity, and charge-carrier transport properties. Notably, single-crystal microfibers of syn- $_ { 3 c }$ show electron mobilities up to $4 . 2 c m ^ { 2 } V ^ { - I } s ^ { - I }$ illustrating the significant potential of these materials for organic electronic devices. Our work demonstrates the versatility of this strategy for the development of oligothienoacene diimide libraries, in particular complex and large syn-oligothienoacene diimides, which are difficult to prepare by present methods.

# Introduction

Incorporation of electron-withdrawing dicarboximides into the peri-positions of (hetero)acenes has emerged as a successful strategy to create n-type semiconductors with distinct optoelectronic properties. In addition to improving stability and solubility, dicarboximides provide numerous options to control and optimize molecular assembly and tune materials properties,] allowing for the continued evolution of more traditional materials derived from (hetero)acenes and their derivatives.l For example, the smallest acene diimides, 1,4,5,8-naphthalene diimides (NDIs) (Figure1a), have proved to be one of the most important n-type building blocks to construct semiconductors for organic electronic devices, due to their chemical accessibility, high stability, and strong electron affinity.4 In 2015, Yamada and co-workers reported the synthesis of bisimide-fused anthracene, tetracene, and pentacene by bismuth triflate-mediated doublecyclization reaction.l Recently, we successfully demonstrated the incorporation of bisimide groups into the peri-positions of tetracene and hexacene to obtain tetracene diimides (TDI)

and hexacene diimides (HDI) by one-pot $\mathrm { Z r }$ -mediated coupling.[] These acene diimides show stabilized HOMO and LUMO levels compared to parent acenes, increasing the electron-accepting ability and photo-oxidative resistances, making them the stable semiconductors.

For a better understanding of the structure-property relationships of (hetero)acene diimides and to fully realize potential candidates for semiconductor materials, a more diverse array of (hetero)acene diimides, as well as more complex systems, are important synthetic targets. A widely followed strategy towards this goal functionalizations NDIs on their periphery with aromatic moieties, followed by oxidative cyclization, or other specific chemical transformation, to lead to the desired systems. Since brominated NDIs are readily accessible building blocks, this approach provides facile access to many heteroacene diimides, which contain five- or six-membered aromatic rings in a row (Figure 1a).[1b,2,8] However, it remains inefficient for preparing large-scale libraries of (hetero)acene diimides, in particular large (hetero)acene diimides.?l This is partially due to the numerous synthetic challenges involved that have limited number of functional sites on NDIs that can be used.

Here we introduce a new synthetic method that offers streamlined access to a novel series of oilgothienoacene diimides, with an NDI core and fused thiophene wings, oriented as either $C _ { 2 h }$ anti-isomers or $C _ { 2 \nu }$ syn-isomers (Figure 1b). Through this series we are able to study how the precise orientation of sulfur atoms within isomeric structures as well as the number of fused thiophene rings affect differences in electronic structures and molecular properties. Notably, while 4,5,9,10-naphtho[2,3-b:6,7-b']dithiophene diimides (NDTI, Figure 1a) have drawn attention as promising building blocks for semiconducting materials,[10] the chemically robust nature of these oligomers reported here allow the use of the standard thiophene chemistry, thus enabling molecular diversification of the parent systems for tuning electronic properties. We discuss in detail the synthetic methods and report the structure-property relationships in the oligothienoacene diimides, including in the solution and solid state.

# Results and Discussion

We began our study of the synthesis of large, symmetric thienoacene diimides (TIPS-anti-3a-d) under palladiumcatalyzed intramolecular arylation reactions (Scheme 1). To access the key intermediates 2a-d, a double nucleophilic substitution reaction between dibromo-NDIs bearing different substituents $R = 2 { , } 6 { - }$ diisopropylphenyl, 2-octyldodecyl, $n$ -octyl, and- $\mathrm { . C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { F } _ { 7 }$ ) in the imide groups and 2- triisopropylsilyl(TIPS)-3-mercapto-thiophene provided the corresponding 2,6-dithienylthio-NDIs derivatives 1a-d in moderate yields $( 6 0 - 7 0 \% )$ , which could be readily brominated utilizing NBS in chloroform and dimethylformamide (DMF) to afford the desired intermediates in very high yields $( 8 5 - 9 0 \% )$ . Surprisingly, the subsequent palladium-catalyzed $\mathrm { C - H }$ activation coupling of $\bf { 2 4 - d }$ in the presence of $\mathrm { P d } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { d b } \mathrm { a } ) _ { 3 }$ $( 5 0 \mathrm { m o l } \% )$ and 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene(DBU, 4.0 equiv) in dimethylacetamide (DMAc) at $1 6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ gave two purple compounds in a molar ratio of 1:1, which exhibit the same mass ion peak. The less polar products prove to be the desired compounds, which exhibit the $C _ { 2 h }$ symmetry. For the second purple compounds, their $\mathrm { ^ { 1 } H N M R }$ spectra are indicative of the $C _ { 2 \nu }$ symmetric structures. On the basis of these data, we assign the second purple compounds as new isomers, which are subsequently confirmed by single-crystal X-ray analysis (see below), revealing the formation of the unexpected syn-isomers (TIPS-syn-3a-d), in which the two inner thiophene sulfur atoms exhibit a syn-conformation with respect to the NDI units.

Single crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained for TIPS-anti-3a and TIPS-syn-3d by slow diffusion of methanol into chloroform at room temperature.11 The isomeric structures are confirmed by X-ray single-crystal analysis (Figure 2). TIPS-anti-3a is nearly planar, with the carbonyl groups slightly twisted from planarity with torsion angles of $0 . 8 9 ^ { \circ }$ and $5 . 8 8 ^ { \circ }$ . In the TIPS-syn-3d crystal, there are two inequivalent molecules, which have different conformations. For one molecule, the torsion angles are $1 . 2 6 \mathrm { - } 1 5 . 6 0 ^ { \circ }$ while a slightly higher degree of planarity is observed for the other molecule with torsion angles of $0 . 3 2 – 7 . 1 0 ^ { \circ }$ , as illustrated in Figure $^ { 2 \mathrm { b } }$ and c. The $\mathbf { S } ^ { - } \mathbf { C }$ bond lengths in TIPS-anti-3a and TIPS-syn-3d range from 1.727 to $1 . { \overset { - } { 7 } } 4 7 \mathrm { \AA }$ , which fall within the typical $\mathrm { S } ^ { - } \mathrm { C }$ bond values for thiophene rings.[12]

To the best of our knowledge, the syn-oligothienoacene diimides have not been reported. Our present results indicate that the synthesis of TIPS-syn/anti-3a-d could be obtained in one-step intramolecular arylation of the intermediates 2a-d. This result indicates that the reaction mechanism possibly occurs through two distinct catalytic cycles, which appear to involve both the usual palladium-catalyzed $\mathrm { C - H }$ activation mechanism and an unexpected 1,2-sulfur migration mechanism. As shown in Scheme 2, Cycle 1 is the usual palladiumcatalyzed intramolecular direct arylation, which would involve oxidative addition (OA), concerted metalation deprotonation (CMD), reductive elimination (RE) to generate B. Alternatively (Cycle 2), the arylpalladium intermediate A would undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution $( { \mathsf { S } } _ { \mathrm { E } } { \mathrm { A r } } )$ at the 2-position in 2,6-dithienylthio-NDIs, leading to the spirocycloarenium intermediate C, which subsequently undergo 1,2-sulfur migration to give intermediate D, followed by the deprotonation and then reductive elimination to afford intermediate E. Proceeding from catalytic Cycle 1 or Cycle 2, the intermediates $\mathbf { B }$ or E could lead to the corresponding syn! anti-isomers, respectively. The novel sulfur migration observed in our system is reminiscent of the rearrangement proposed to account for the formation of a 1:1 mixture of 5- and 6-substituted benzo[b]thiophenes from arylthiovinyl sulfonic esters in the presence of $\mathrm { B F } _ { 3 }$ etherate.[13] These sulfur migration results suggest that there is the possibility of trapping the intermediates generated by 1,2-sulfur migration by many other synthetic methods. We are currently examining this possibility.

To examine the feasibility of this strategy for other oligothienoacene diimides, we extended the strategy to the synthesis of larger analogs. Scheme 3 shows the synthesis of anti/syn-oligothienoacene diimides with eight fused rings (TIPS-anti/syn-5a,b) and ten fused rings (TIPS-anti/syn7a,b), which is in a similar fashion to TIPS-anti/syn-3a,b. As expected, these prerequisite intermediates 4a,b and 6a,b (Scheme S2) can be readily converted into the corresponding anti/syn-isomers in a molar ratio of 1:1.

In addition to $\mathrm { ^ { 1 } H N M R }$ and mass spectrometry, the structures of TIPS-anti/syn-5a and TIPS-anti/syn-7a were unambiguously corroborated by X-ray structure analysis.[11] As shown in Figure3, these four new compounds are generally planar with the carbonyl groups slightly twisted away from planarity with torsion angles of $1 . 2 { - } 8 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ It is surprising to see that these four compounds crystallize with - stacked dimeric structures with their long axes parallel to each other, even though there are bulky TIPS and 2,6- diisopropylphenyl substituents present. TIPS-anti-5a and TIPS-anti-7a are observed to align in a slipped $\pi$ stacked dimers with large pitch angles of $5 0 ^ { \circ }$ and $5 6 ^ { \circ }$ , respectively, which may be attributed to a favorable interaction between the thienothiophene part (donor) and the NDI part (acceptor) of the adjacent molecules. TIPS-syn-5a and TIPS-syn-7a, on the other hand, align in an antiparallel $\pi$ stacked dimers. The packing difference among the syn- and anti-isomers is partially due to stabilizing dipole-dipole interactions in the syn-isomers, which are responsible for the antiparallel alignment in the crystals (see below).[14]

For a deeper understanding of their electronic properties, TIPS-functionalized $N \mathbf { \mathcal { N } } ^ { \prime }$ -bis(2-octyldodecyl)-NDTI (TIPSNDTI) was also synthesized according to the literature (Scheme S3).[2d] Figure 4a shows the Uv/Vis absorption spectra of oligothienoacene diimides in chloroform. There is a red-shift in the maximum absorption going from TIPSNDTI to TIPS-anti-3b to TIPS-anti-5b to TIPS-anti-7b, with the optical gap decreasing from 2.06 to 1.94 to 1.74 to $1 . 6 5 \mathrm { e V } ,$ which is attributed to the extended $\pi$ -conjugation as the number of thieno rings increase. Time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of theory are consistent with these trends: The calculated lowest lying absorptions for these compounds are estimated to be $5 8 2 \mathrm { n m }$ $( f _ { \mathrm { c a l c } } = 0 . 1 6 9 1 )$ for TIPS-NDTI, $6 0 9 \mathrm { n m }$ $( f _ { \mathrm { c a l c } } = 0 . 1 6 4 7 )$ for TIPS-anti-3b, $6 8 2 \mathrm { n m }$ $( f _ { \mathrm { c a l c } } = 0 . 4 7 9 7 ,$ for TIPS-anti-5b, $7 2 0 \mathrm { n m }$ $( f _ { \mathrm { c a l c } } = 0 . 9 1 5 9 )$ for TIPS-anti-7b, and in each case the lowest energy transition is described as being predominantly $\mathrm { H O M O } \longrightarrow \mathrm { L U M O }$ (Table S3). Similar trends are also found in TIPS-syn-3b to TIPS-syn-5b to TIPS-syn-7b, among the three syn-isomers, TIPS-syn-3b showed a blue shift of maximum absorption compared to the other two syn-isomers, with a very low intensity absorption shoulder extending to $6 3 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . All three syn-isomers exhibit broad and featureless spectra, which are likely ascribed to the weak intramolecular charge transfer induced by the dipole moments in the molecules (Figure 4d).[15] The absorption maxima of synisomers are blue-shifted compared to those of their corresponding anti-isomers indicating a better conjugation in these anti-isomers.

The redox properties of oligothienoacene diimides were examined by cyclic voltammetry (CV; $0 . 1 \mathbf { M }$ $\mathrm { T B A P F } _ { 6 }$ $1 0 0 \mathrm { m V s ^ { - 1 } } )$ in dichloromethane, with the onset oxidationreduction potentials determined relative to $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ $( 4 . 8 \mathrm { e V } )$ (Figure4b and Table1). The CVs of oligothienoacene diimides exhibit two reversible reduction peaks and their first-wave reduction potentials are comparable (Figure 4b), as also suggested by the DFT calculations (Figure 4c), which indicate that, regardless of the thieno ring numbers and the thiophene conformations, the LUMOs for all the compounds are similar in shape and mainly localized on the NDI core. The ionization potentials (IPs) of each pair of syn- and antiisomers are very similar and a slightly upward shifting of ionization potentials (IPs) with increasing number of thieno rings. The DFT calculations reveal that the HOMOs of the compounds are mainly distributed over the aromatic cores and the extended conjugation in these compounds can decrease the first oxidation potential, again in agreement with the electrochemical measurements. Notably, the sulfur atoms with large coefficients in TIPS-NDTI and TIPS-anti/ syn-3b have great contribution to HOMOs, resulting in effective delocalization distributions of the HOMOs. However, in TIPS-anti/syn-7b, the sulfur atoms have limited contributions to the HOMOs. Overall, the asymmetric HOMOs and LUMOs in syn-isomers lead to a dipole moment of $1 . 4 5 \mathrm { D }$ for TIPS-syn-3b, $1 . 4 6 \mathrm { D }$ for TIPS-syn-5b, and $1 . 6 4 \mathrm { D }$ for TIPS-syn-7b, respectively (Figure 4d). According to their onset potentials, the EAs of these oligothienoacene diimides are ranging from $- 3 . 9 6$ to $- 4 . 0 2 \mathrm { e V } ,$ implying that these compounds may serve as electron transporting organic semiconductor materials.

The TIPS groups of oligothienoacene diimides can be removed upon treatment with tetrabutylammonium fluoride to obtain protodesilylated oligothienoacene diimides (Scheme S4). In particular, the protodesilylated oligothienoacene diimides NDTI, syn/anti-3b, syn/anti-5b, and syn/anti-7b (Figure 5) are soluble enough for use in solution-processed organic thin film transistors (OTFTs), which were fabricated in the "bottom-gate bottom-contact' geometry with octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS)-treated $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ as substrate. The thin film devices were measured under nitrogen and the device characteristics are summarized in Table S4. Figure 6a and b show the transfer characteristics of syn/anti-3b. For NDTI, it shows significantly limited electron carrier mobility, on the order of $1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . In contrast, anti-3b shows an average electron mobility of $0 . 0 2 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and a maximum mobility of $0 . 0 3 \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \ : \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , which are more than two orders of magnitude higher than that of NDTI. Interestingly, syn-3b exhibits the highest device performance, with an average electron mobility of $0 . 0 5 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and a maximum mobility of $0 . 0 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ which are two-fold higher than that of anti3b. For syn-5b, the devices exhibit average electron mobilities in the order of $1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , the best mobility reaching $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \thinspace \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The electron mobility value of syn-5b is nearly one order of magnitude higher than that of anti-5b, which shows ambipolar characteristics with average electron and hole mobilities on the orders of $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ and $1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively. For syn/anti-7b, both compounds also exhibit ambipolar characteristics, although the p-type characters were observed under largely negative bias conditions with $V _ { \mathrm { t h } }$ of $- 5 0$ and $- 6 0 \mathrm { V } .$ The two compounds show low electron and hole mobilities on the order of $1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . Our results suggest that oligothienoacene diimides are versatile semiconductor materials with tunable-polarity characteristics that are dependent on the molecular geometry and number of thieno rings in the thienoacene backbones.

To understand the mobilities of oligothienoacene diimides in relation to the microstructures in the solid state, the thin films were studied by grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) (Figure S4). The diffractograms of syn! anti-3b show that the diffraction peaks are aligned vertically indicating that the crystalline grains are aligned with the substrate planes (Figure 6c and d). The $d$ spacings of the outof-plane diffractions are $2 9 \textup { \AA }$ for syn/anti-3b, respectively, which suggest they adapt the edge-on orientation on the substrates. It is interesting that though the $\pi { - } \pi$ stacking peak does not lie in the substrate plane, which should be detrimental to the charge transport in these films,[16] syn! anti-3b show decent electron mobilities. The syn/anti-5b image shows a significantly lower degree of ordering than those of syn/anti-3b (Figure S4). In syn/anti-7b films, there is significant arching in the out-of-plane peaks along $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { z } }$ axis indicating that the syn/anti-7b grains are no longer aligned with the substrate surface plane but instead more randomly oriented (Figure S4). As a consequence, the syn/anti-5b and syn/anti-7b thin films contain small grains and exhibit low crystallinity with poor orientation, which limit charge-carrier transport in these films..

As the branched alkyl chains may interrupt the nature tendency of a material to form an idealized molecular packing required for efficient charge transport, syn/anti-3c (Figure 5) were employed to get a better understanding of the intrinsic charge transport properties using single-crystal organic field effect transistors (SC-OFETs). The single crystals were prepared by a typical vapor transport method. The AFM images in Figure 7a, e indicate that these microfibers were about 18 and $3 2 \mathrm { n m }$ thick with smooth surfaces. In addition, these microfibers exhibit very strong Bragg reflections, indicating the high-quality of the crystals. For anti-3c, the $d \cdot$ spacing is $1 7 . 6 0 \mathrm { \AA }$ which is very close to the calculated distance in the case of interdigitation $( 1 6 . 5 0 \mathrm { \AA } )$ (Figure S3), suggesting a possible alkyl chain interdigitation in the anti-3c microfibers. The $d$ spacing $( 2 0 . 8 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } )$ calculated from the first peak of syn-3c crystal is close to the calculated molecular length $( 2 3 . 9 0 \mathring \mathrm { A } )$ (Figure S3), which indicates that molecules probably have the long axis upright to the substrate in the microfibers with the tilting angle of $6 0 ^ { \circ }$ Using the prepared single-crystalline microfibers, field-effect transistors were fabricated by a gold strips technique on the $\mathrm { O T S - S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ [17] Figure 7c, d and $\mathbf { g }$ h show the representative output and transfer characteristics of anti/syn-3c microfibers, respectively. The anti-3c microfibers show exclusive electron charge transport with an average mobility of $2 . 3 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and a maximum mobility up to $3 . 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (Table S5). The mobility values are 5- to 7-fold higher than that of NDTI derivatives.[8k] For syn-3c, the microfibers exhibit better performance than those of anti-3c, with an average mobility of $2 . 9 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \mathbf { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { \mathbf { S } ^ { - 1 } } }$ and a maximum mobility of $4 . 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ Our results indicate that these materials have considerable potential for use in organic electronic devices, with the possibility that further functionalization may allow even further enhancement in charge-carrier transport properties.

# Conclusion

We describe a synthetic pathway that involves both a palladium-catalyzed $\mathrm { C - H }$ activation and a 1,2-sulfur migration, which generates a series of syn/anti-oligothienoacene diimides in a molar ratio of 1:1. The molecular structures and resulting molecular electronic properties can have dramatic effects on the material electronic properties, degree of crystallinity, thin-film morphology, and charge-carrier transport properties. We also observe a difference in the charge-carrier transport properties of the anti/syn-isomers, with the syn-isomer showing higher mobility relative to the corresponding anti-isomer. Notably, the anti-3c and syn-3c microfibers exhibit electron mobilities up to 3.7 and $4 . 2 \thinspace \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { V } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Our present study provides the practical strategy to develop oligothienoacene diimides for organic electronic devices. The versatility of this synthetic approach extends the construction of oligothienoacene diimides and enables the preparation of large and complex structures.[18]
