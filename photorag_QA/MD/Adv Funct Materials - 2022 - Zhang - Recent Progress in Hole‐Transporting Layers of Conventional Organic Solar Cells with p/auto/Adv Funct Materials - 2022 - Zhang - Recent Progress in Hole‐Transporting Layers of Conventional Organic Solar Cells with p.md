# Recent Progress in Hole-Transporting Layers of Conventional Organic Solar Cells with p-i-n Structure

Xuning Zhang, Hong Zhang,\* Yanxun Li, Saud-uz Zafar, Shuo Yang, Jianhui Chen, Huiqiong Zhou,\* and Yuan Zhang\*

Recently, organic solar cells (OsCs) have received rapid boosts in the power conversion efficiency (PCE), due to progresses in materials and device engineering. Several groups have reported champion PCEs over $19 \%$ in singlejunction, ternary, and tandem OsCs. In addition to the concentrated focus on the new design of OsC active materials, buffer layer materials, used for the interface layer providing the functionalities of interface charge transport and collection in OsCs, are of critical importance for the optimization of PCE. Compared with the electron transport layers (ETL), the hole transport Iayers (HTLs) have received less attention and are still dominated by the commercial material poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS), which has limitations for the efficiency and OSC device stability enhancement. In this Review, the recent progress in HTL materials, including the modifications for PEDOT:PSS, alternative HTL materials based on polymers and inorganic oxide materials, etc, is summarized. This review also provides a summative prospect aiming to help scientists apprehend the current possibilities and challenges in this field.

# 1. Introduction

Over the past few decades, the photovoltaic technology has become realistic product to impact our life and are under continuous development, including silicon photovoltaics,ll dye sensitized solar cells,2] perovskite solar cells (PsCs),[3] and organic solar cells (OsCs).(4,5] Among them, solution-processed thin-film OsCs have the potential to be fabricated by low-cost and high-throughput roll-to-roll manufacturing techniques, associated with the benefits of lightweight, low-cost, flexibility, and the ability to prepare large-area devices by solution processing.[4-8] The active layer of OsCs is mainly constructed by employing the bulk heterojunction (BHJ) structure.[9] BHJ OSCs have seen tremendous progress in photon harvesting and resultant good power conversion efficiencies (PCEs). These achievements are synergistically promoted by rational molecule design of donor and acceptor materials,[10] coupled with interface modification to achieve proper molecular energy level matching and desirable thin film morphology in BHJs.[11]

Generally, the structure of organic solar cells is dominated by a sandwich structure, which contains multiple layers, including organic semiconductor (small molecules or polymers)-based active layers, interface modification layers, and metal electrodes or other types of electrode layers with high electrical conductivities. We describe the electronic and optical processes that take place during the operation of an organic solar cell as the following steps (Figure 1a). i) Photons are absorbed by the active layer and excitons are formed. ii) Excitons diffuse to the donor-acceptor (D-A) interface inside the BHJ. ii) Excitons are dissociated at the D-A interface and mobile holes and electrons (more strictly, weakly bound electron-hole pairs) are created. iv) Holes and electrons are transported in their respective bulk phases. v) Holes and electrons are collected at the electrode.[11,12]

The commonly adopted structure of single-junction OSCs includes the photoactive (or light-absorbing) layer, HTL and ETL coupled with the electrodes (see illustration in Figure 1b) where semiconductive polymer or small molecule materials serve an absorption layer with the thickness ranging between 100 and $2 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . After photon absorption and interfacial charge transfer, the exciton becomes a weakly bound electron-hole pairs and for generating photovoltaic effect, they must be transported through multiple interfaces along their paths and then are collected at the two electrodes.11] Therefore, interface properties in the device play a vital role in the processes of charge extraction, surface trap passivation and interface recombination.[13] Charge transporting layers (CTLs), often referred to as "buffer layers" or "interface modification layers," are of critical importance to achieving the performance maximum of OsCs.

By choosing appropriate CTLs, the charge separation driving force, usually referred as the built-in electric field in the device can be desirably provided to efficiently transport and extract the two charge carriers to their respective electrodes.[14] For the sake of device operation, it is extremely important to achieve Ohmic contacts and satisfactory charge transport between the active layer and the electrode of OSCs, for which CTLs play a fundamental significance. The main functions and key criteria of CTLs are summarized as follows:

i) To form a junction with the absorber layer (active layer) while allowing the maximum amount of light to enter the junction region and active layer. Thin-film heterojunction solar cells deliver more light to the junction because it has a wide-bandgap CTLs compared to the best low-bandgap active layer. This offers the most reliable way to improve cell efficiency.   
ii) The CTL must form an Ohmic contact with the corresponding charge transport and collection layers, which is beneficial to reduce recombination losses and improve device performance.[15]   
iii) The CTL needs to have the functionality of changing the energetic arrangement between the active layer and electrodes; adjust the interfacial barrier of the metal-semiconductor contact.   
iv) The CTL should have minimal absorption losses and should be able to drive photogenerated carriers with minimal recombination losses, and the resistance should be small enough to transport photogenerated carriers to external circuits. For high luminous flux with minimal resistive loss, the bandgap of the interface layer should be as large as possible and the thickness should be as thin as possible to maintain low series resistance.   
v) The CTL should also have the ability to modify the surface chemistry of the active layer to provide stable interfacial protection for the subsequent window deposition process.

The built-in potential $( V _ { \mathrm { b i } } )$ in OsCs is the driving force for the separation and drift of photogenerated mobile carriers to render the photovoltaic effect. Differing from silicon solar cells where $V _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ is created through doping of the homojunction,[16] the $V _ { \mathrm { b i } }$ in OsCs originates from the work function (WF) difference of electrodes.17] In this regard, interface materials with a large difference in WF is favorable. Generally speaking, interfacial ETLs should have a shallow level of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) to facilitate electron collection toward the cathode, while the hole transport interface layer, or HTL should have a deep level of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) to match with the HOMO of the donor material for hole extraction. Proper selection of hole and electron interface transporting layers is of importance to obtain high-efficiency and stable OsC devices.[18] In cases where the intrinsic material properties of CTLs cannot meet the criteria or provide the required functionalities discussed above, interface engineering is applied, which involves the tuning on the energy level arrangement between the electrode and the organic material, afforded by specific surface treatments to the electrode material or inclusion of a thin modification layer (such as dipole layer) between the electrode and active layer. Inorganic and organic ETLs based on Calcium,[19] ZrAcac,[20] $\mathrm { Z n O }$ [21] PDIN,[22] PFN,[23] PFN- $\mathrm { \cdot B r } ^ { [ 2 4 ] }$ have been used for OsCs, showing effectiveness in enhancing the electron transport and extraction at the cathode interface of OsCs. In comparison to the rich ETL materials reservoir, PEDOT:PSS so far is the most commonly used HTL material for OSCs with a conventional device architecture (p-i-n type). Due to the intrinsic shortages of PEDOT:PSS, such as the acidic nature and high anisotropy in the electrical conductivity,[25] there have been the design of alternative HTL materials for OsCs, such as polyelectrolytes,[26] metal oxides, and graphene-based materials,[27] which have generated benefits for the improvement of interfacial hole transport/extraction and eventual boosts of PCEs.[25] In this Review, we will be focusing only on the discussion of recent development of HTL materials and modifications on the interfacial properties and photovoltaic behaviors in OsCs adopted with the conventional device architecture.

In the so-called conventional structure, the ITO substrate is the anode and it is coated with an HTL, on top of which a BHJ layer formed via a mixture of n-type and p-type materials is deposited. The BHJ layer is generally covered by an ETL and finally a cathode. In this Review, we focus on the discussion of HTLs developed for OsCs with conventional structure and the reasons are as follows. i) The conventional structure is the prototype device configuration during the development of OsCs that to date is widely adopted in the lab-based device fabrication. ii) The buried bottom HTL can induce a regulating effect on the morphology of organic light-absorbing layers, providing extra handles on the morphological control. Thus, we feel that a review on the development of HTLs for conventional OSCs will be of high relevance to the active researches in the OSC communities. iii) For realistic applications of OsCs, devices with low-cost and large-areas are needed. To this end, solutionprocessible interface materials are favorable for the reduction of fabrication costs and are suited for use in the fabrication of OsCs with conventional structure. We would point out that for inverted OSCs (n-i-p structure), the HTLs are representatively implemented through thermal vapor deposition of metal oxides atop the photoactive layer. With regard to the dominantly used $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ -based HTLs, efforts on seeking alternative HTLs (for inverted OSCs) are sparsely documented.

Depending on the HTL materials category, this Review is organized as follows: we will first come to the HTL based on nonmodified PEDOT:PSS which so far is dominantly used in the lab-based Osc fabrication; following this, we will be discussing the recently reported approaches for PEDOT:PSS modifications, which has been evidenced with the ability to enhance the hole extraction, as well as the control of nanomorphology of OSCs. After that, alternative HTL material designs for OSCs based on conjugated polymer/polyelectrolytes, graphene oxides, and transition metal oxides are discussed. At last, we will provide a summative discussion on the current status of HTL development and outline the directions toward future HTL materials design with which exciting opportunities may be generated for further improving the performance merits of OSCs for realistic energy conversion applications.

# 2. PEDOT:PSS-Based HTLs

# 2.1. Nonmodified PEDOT:PSS

PEDOt:Pss is a highly conductive and water-dispersible polymer. So far, it is the dominant HTL material used for OSCs, which is on the basis of its conductive nature with a high tunability afforded by doing and the suitable WF $( \approx 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ and surface wettability for solution-deposition of organic BHJs. PEDOT:PSS is generally obtained through polymerization of 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene after mixing with polysty rene sulfonate. By doping so, the solubility and conductivity of the final product PEDOT:PSS in water can be tailorized.[28] Since it is composed of two different polymers, different types PEDOT:PSS can be obtained based on the ratio of PEDOT and Pss, leading to changes of WF, electrical conductivity and acidity. However, it is noteworthy that PEDOT:PSS still has some intrinsic drawbacks. For example, the commercially available PEDOT:PSS (Clevios PVP AI. 4083) contains a massive proportion of insulating PSS (PEDOT:PSS ratio of 1:6), which causes a poor conductivity of PEDOT:PSS. In addition, the acidity $\mathrm { ( p H } = 1 - 2 \mathrm { ) }$ of PEDOT:PSS can corrode the ITO surface, resulting in the diffusion of indium into the photoactive layer, causing device degradation.[29] Furthermore, it readily absorbs oxygen and moisture from the air, causing the penetration of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ molecules into subsequent layers to negatively affect the device performance.30] Therefore, researchers have employed various approaches to modify the properties of PEDOT:PSs, including doping optical agents/additives (e.g., ethylene glycol,[31] dimethyl sulfoxide,[32] ionic liquids,[33] or nanoparticles[34) into PEDOT:PSS in the original solution and the post-treatment of the PEDOT:PSS with suitable materials such as hexafluoroacetone,[35] surfactant,[36] salts,[37] and acids.38] In the next subsections, we will discuss the successes in treatments and modifications of PEDOT:PSS.

In this context, Pan et al. doped PEDOt:PSS with ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ nanowires. The ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ nanowire layer could effectively block electrons from the wrong direction, while increasing the incident light paths in the hybrid transport layers due to refraction and reflection. The pseudotexture effect of ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ nanowires leads to higher photon absorption by the photoactive layer, which makes OSCs with such mixed buffer layers superior to buffer layers composed of only PEDOT:PSS or ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ nanowire monolayers.[40] Jiang et al. doped PEDOT:PSS with $\mathrm { W O } _ { x } ,$ obtaining a decreased series resistance and greater shunt resistance, which increased the carrier transport and device stability.[30] By using solution-processed PEDOT:I $ { \mathrm { P S } }  { \mathrm { S } }  { : }  { \mathbb { W } }  { \mathrm { O } } _ { x }$ as the HTL, improved device fill factor and short-circuit current $( J _ { \mathrm { s c } } )$ could be achieved in OSCs.[30]

# 3.2. HTLs Based on Conjugated Polyelectrolytes

CPE is a kind of polyelectrolyte consisting of a conjugated main chain and side chains with ionic functional groups,[97] which were first reported by Heeger and co-workers to serve HTLs in OsCs,98] with several unique advantages such as remarkable connections, high optical transparency, easy modification, pH-neutral and good solubility in water and alcohol. In addition, the chemical structure of CPE can be easily varied by synthetic chemistry, providing an enormous opportunity to tune the electrical optical, and chemical properties of HTL materials.[99]

CPE-K,100] which consists of thiophene and phenyl groups, was the first reported conjugated polyelectrolyte material applied as an HTL in OSCs.[98] Compared with PEDOT:PSS, neutral CPE-K does not cause corrosion of electrodes. # 3.3. HTLs Based on Conjugated Polymers

PANI is a promising conducting polymer due to its facile synthesis, low cost, unique redox behavior, environmental and chemical stability.[109] In addition, it is soluble in organic solvents when it is protonated by functionalized protonic acids such as camphor sulfonic acid.[10,111] Mixing PANI and PSS together, may work well as a hole extraction layer.[112,113] The WF of HTLs prepared with water-based PANI:PSS proved to be lower than that of HTLs prepared with organic solvents (IPA-based).[111] Besides, the influence of the ratio of PANI and PSS was investigated by von Hauff and co-workers. # 3.4. HTLs Based on Self-Assembled Small Molecules

Except for the CMPs, CPEs and other polymer-type HTL materials, self-assembled monolayers are also introduced in OsCs as HTLs. The advantage of self-assembled monolayers is that the devices using small-molecule organic surface modifiers may be cost-effective and air-stable. This is because they avoid unnecessary synthetic routes and purification processes and have a neutral $\mathrm { p H }$ value.82,130-134] Polar self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) were first introduced at the ITO/donor interface in chloroaluminium phthalocyanine (ClAlPc); $\mathrm { { \stackrel { \prime } { C } } _ { 6 0 } }$ discrete heterojunction OSCs by Jones and co-workers, the PCE can be dramatically increased from $1 . 3 \%$ to $3 . 3 \%$ [130] Ginger and coworkers[133] reduced the contact resistance between an inorganic metal oxide and organic active layer by adding a fluorinated SAM on the ITO surface instead of PEDOT:PSS, thereby increasing the $V _ { \mathrm { o c } }$ of bulk heterojunction OSCs. In other works, Choi and co-workers employed chlorobenzoic acid (CBA) as a small-molecule organic surface modifier for the production of high WF ITO electrodes by a simple spin-coating method.132,134] The enhanced light absorption within the active layer due to the high transparency in the visible range, the formation of an Ohmic contact for hole transport from the active layer to ITO anode through cascade energy level alignment, and the minimized recombination losses, demonstrate an improved PCE of $8 . 4 8 \%$ . However, preparing the SAM on the substrate requires immersing in solution for long times $( > 2 0 \mathrm { h } )$ and there have been few studies of the preparation of highly ordered SAMs by simple spin-coating of organic small molecules. Further studies on the rapid self-assembly of small molecules on substrate surfaces are therefore necessary.

# 5. Transition Metal Oxide-Based HTLs

Inorganic materials can generally be referred to as p-type and n-type interface materials regarding the position of the valence and conduction band. As to the p-type interface materials, the valence band of the materials should match with the HOMO level of the polymer; for n-type interface materials, electron transfer from LUMO of the acceptor to the conduction band of that inorganic material.

Several inorganic interface materials are metal oxide p-type in nature and can be used as HTL in OsCs. And we summarized a series of device properties of this type of HTL in Table 5. HTLs help the interface to support and pass holes to the respective side of the electrode and block electrons to reach and decay the performance of the device. It is important to note that metal oxides are more stable and cheaper, compared to organic p-type interface materials. Inorganic HTL includes many compounds introduced in OsCs like $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ , NiO, ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ and $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ All of them are used in OsCs fabrication and everyone has their own pros and cons such as high WF and good hole conductivity. These oxides are usually deposited thermally such as the thermal evaporation method which is compatible with anode deposition for gold, silver or aluminum. In this section, we will discuss the advantages, disadvantages, and related aspects of their properties.[146,147]

# 5.1. HTLs Based on Molybdenum Oxide $( \mathsf { M o o } _ { 3 } )$

$\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ is an inorganic chemical metal oxide that has a hierarchical crystal structure and because of this, it can be used in the process of electrochemical fabricating devices. It is a p-type metal oxide used for the HTL in the OSCs with a WF of 2.3 to $5 . 3 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ good conductivity and also compatible with anode (Ag or Au) properties.148] They fabricated their system of OsCs by using $\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ as a hole selective layer and experimented with different thicknesses, finding that each thickness has its own impact on the device, but optimizing the thickness is necessary to achieve better results. They propose that this type of $\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ has multifunctional properties for hole transport and electron blocking. $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ can replace PEDOT:PSS in conventional devices. Therefore, it has a good HTL. Due to the conduction band of $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ $( 2 . 3 \mathrm { e V } )$ , it can and usually serves as an electron and exciton blocking layer for the used acceptor of the active layer so, one must try to have an acceptor with such a low WF to achieve better performance for their function.149-154] As in their experiments, they used P3HT:PCBM as an active layer with a lower LUMO than that of $\mathsf { M o O } _ { 3 }$ [155] Also to deposit $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ some used another method to synthesize and spin-coat it, namely using PAD (polymer-assisted deposition), as it is an eco-friendly strategy, by which $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ showed a good crystallinity with a LUMO of $3 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ wavelengths with transparency greater than $9 5 \%$ [156]

# 5.2. HTLs Based on Nickel Oxide (NiO)

Nickel oxide is a chemical compound classified as basic metal oxide. NiO adopts an octahedral structure, often referred to as rock salt structure. It is often nonstoichiometric. It is a p-type metal oxide used for the HTL in the OSCs with a WF of 1.7 to $5 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ good conductivity and also compatible with anode (Ag or Au) properties. Irwin et al.185] reported that the nickel oxide, a p-type semiconductor, is an efficiency enhancer anode interface layer as high as PCE of $5 . 2 \%$ . Yu et al. experimented by using NiO as an HTL by thermally evaporating it.[186] NiO films can modulate the Schottky barrier and form an Ohmic contact at the metal/organic interface, which helps it to act as an HTL. As the NiO was thermally deposited under a high vacuum so less or very few oxygen contents were there so that is why (Ni) conductivity is high. Later Yang et al.[187] reported that electrodeposit NiO nanostructure was used as an HTL. The structures of NiO were tailored by the electrodeposition time and current density to have nanoisland or interconnecting nanorods.

# 5.3. HTLs Based on Tungsten Oxide $\left( \aleph \aleph _ { 3 } \right)$

Tungsten oxide is a chemical metal oxide compound and an n-type material with a high WF from 1.6 to $5 . 1 \mathrm { e V }$ so that is why it can be used as an HTL. The crystal structure of $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ is totally dependent on temperature so from high to low it goes from tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic to triclinic. During the past decades, a lot of research has been done for some metal oxide and it includes $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ as well for the use of HTL over other old HTL (PEDOT:PSS). In 2009, Han et al.[195] investigated the thermally evaporated $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ (tungsten oxide) on OsCs and compared the pros and cons with PEDOT:PSS. It was shown that $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ layers thermally evaporated on ITO, forming a uniform amorphous film and relatively low surface energy. Chan et al.[196] used $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ as an HTL in their system in vacuum-deposited $\mathrm { C u P c } / \mathrm { C } _ { 6 0 }$ solar cells. It is noted that the fact that HTL will work in all rather it is vacuum-deposited or inverted geometry BHJ cells does not necessarily mean it will work also in BHJ of conventional geometry in which active layer is deposited on top of buffer one by solution processing, because the fabrication cannot be compatible and also the phase-separation properties of BHJ influences by it. In 2010, some researchers work with $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ transition metal layer and deposited it with hot filament vapor deposition for use in hybrid inorganic OLED.[197] $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ is a nontoxic compound for both humans and the environment and an alternative, promising material to use as an HTL using a sol-gel process.198] Other than this it does not need annealing, which is important for industrialization. To improve the performance of the interface layer, simplify the preparation process of $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ the $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ plasma process was applied to instead of the thermal annealing method.[199]

This plasma-treated layer that was prepared by spin-coated shows a high conductivity, high light transmittance, and enhanced interfacial charge transfer (see Figure 17). This layer showed better results than PEDOT:PSS and $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ (normal annealed). Density functional theory calculations further confirm that $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ has better charge transfer and better conductivity. Later tungstite nanoparticles were synthesized and used in the fabrication of OsCs.200] This microwave-assisted simple method was providing a low-cost production rate for the $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ nanoparticles. The deposition of $3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ platelet-like particles dispersed and thin films of it give a suitable energy level alignment with the WF of $5 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ Incorporating these nanoparticles gives rise to exhibiting good photovoltaics performance as HTL.

Tungsten carbide (WC), a modified compound of $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ , has special properties such as high corrosion resistance, electrical conductivity, and excellent catalytic activity.[202] In OsCs, the WC nanoparticle layer can also act as a highly efficient HTL. The device made from this method does not need a further post-treatment and can act as in inverted OsCs and also it has been reported that solvent treatment also enhances the charge extraction[210] Their results with different BHJs were also remarkable while using WC in place of $_ { \mathrm { e - M o O } _ { 3 } }$ . It is also a new way to use these WC nanoparticles in an interface anode layer and can be used in various electronic device systems.

$\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ is well known for its adequate electrical characteristics, wide bandgap, high transparency, and reasonably hole mobility. Lampande et al.(203] used $\mathbb { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ as interface layers separately to see the results with these two layers under thermally evaporated one. As there is a difference in bandgap so both will perform and give differently. $5 \mathrm { n m } \mathrm { W } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ gave high PCE as compared to $1 0 \ \mathrm { n m \ M o O _ { 3 } }$ . This may be due to the better contact at the anode side and as a smooth surface gives less leakage current, decrease contact resistance and also improve hole extraction with better conductivity $( 0 . 3 5 \mathrm { ~ S ~ c m ^ { - 1 } } )$ . Sol-gel processed nanocrystalline $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ results in a much higher PCE of the device due to its good electronic properties, such as reduced leakage current and better Ohmic contact.

Some researchers performed experiments on the hydrogen ated under-stoichiometric tungsten oxides $( \mathrm { H } _ { \gamma } \mathrm { W O } _ { 3 - x } )$ as hole collection interlayers in OSCs.204] Hydrogenated tungsten oxide under stoichiometric exhibits gap states near the Fermi level which are beneficial for charge transport and they maintain a high WF.

Vanadium oxide $( \mathrm { V } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 } )$ is an n-type inorganic compound with a high WF ranging from 2.4 to $5 . 4 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ for different oxidation states. Solution-processed ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ as an interlayer can effectively avoid leakage current at the organic/electrode interface by blocking electrons at the anode (see Figure 18).[39] Compared to the thermal vacuum-deposited layers,[211] low-temperature coating solution-processed ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ interlayers are more facile and favorable for mass production, which is crucial for the target of low-costs. # 6. Conclusion and Perspective

The current pressing goal in the OsC-relevant researches lies in obtaining satisfactory photovoltaic efficiency and lifetime of devices toward a wide range of potential applications. Given the importance of interface engineering for the performance in OsCs, tremendous efforts have been made on the design of interface materials, and great progress has been achieved.

Based on nonfullerene organic solar cells, insights into the role of surface properties of modified PEDOT:PSS HTLs in regulating the nanomorphology of photoactive layers of OsCs have been attained, offering exciting possibilities of controlling the molecular arrangement in organic heterojunctions. This has led to widening the efficiency optimization windows through interface handles. As alternative HTL materials to the widely used PEDOT:PSS, there have been the successes on the design of HTL materials for OSCs, including conjugated polymers and polyelectrolytes or small molecules, graphene-based molecules and transition metal oxides (e.g., ${ \mathrm { V } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 5 }$ $\mathrm { M o O } _ { 3 }$ , NiO). Despite that these materials have been proven with the applicability for use as HTLs of OSCs, none of them is yet perfect, considering the cost, stability, and processibility.

To this end, the development of new interface HTL materials, and better controls and understandings of the functionalities of existing HTL materials are urgently needed. The established understandings on HTLs are as follows.

i) Appropriate HTL material selection rules are to have sufficiently high electrical properties to facilitate interfacial carrier transport with appropriately large bandgaps to limit the absorption of sun light, as well as to block the interfacial extraction of opposite carriers.   
ii) The deposition process of HTLs should have the ability to passivate the surface states of the active layer and meanwhile provide energetic alignments of their valence bands with those of active layer materials to warrant satisfactory charge collection.   
iii) The surface properties of HTLs should aid the self-assembly of molecules constituting the photoactive layer of OSCs toward favorable phase morphology.   
iv) The interlayer materials should possess the tolerance to photothermal and hydro-oxygen imposed stimuli to allow for high stability of organic solar cells.

We foresee that the combined progress achieved on the holetransporting and light-absorbing materials alongside device engineering would push forward the efficiency of organic solar cells to the $20 \%$ regime.
