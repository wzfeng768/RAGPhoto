# Improving Photovoltaic Performance of Non-Fullerene Polymer Solar Cells Enables by Fine-Tuning Blend Microstructure via Binary Solvent Mixtures

Yue Yu, Rui Sun, Tao Wang, Xinxin Yuan, Yao Wu, Qiang Wu, Mumin Shi, Wenyan Yang, Xuechen Jiao, and Jie Min\*

Studies of the relationship between blend microstructure and photovoltaic performance are becoming more common, which is a prerequisite for rationally improving device performance. Non-fullerene acceptors usually have planar backbone conformation and strong intermolecular packing, normally resulting in excessive phase separation. Herein, an effective co-solvent blending strategy to turn the molecular organization of a chlorinated small molecule acceptor Y6-2Cl and phase separation of the corresponding active layer with PM6 as donor is demonstrated. The in situ photoluminescence measurements and relevant morphological characterizations illustrate that the film-forming process is fine-turned when using the mixtures of chloroform (CF) and chlorobenzene (CB) solvents, and the blend showed high domain purity with suitable phase-separated networks. Thus, better exciton dissociation and charge generation, more balanced charge transport, and less recombination loss are obtained in the co-solvent blade-coated devices. As a result, a maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) of $1 6 . 1 7 \%$ is achieved, which is much higher than those of CF- and CB-bladed devices $7 4 . 0 8 \%$ and $1 1 . 4 4 \%$ , respectively). Of note is that the use of this co-solvent approach in the other two high-performance photovoltaic systems is also confrmed, demonstrating its good generality of employing in the printing organic solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Solution-processed organic solar cells (OSCs) have shown the potential for nextgeneration photovoltaic technology due to their unique merits, such as lightweight, low-cost, semi-transparent property, and fexibility,1-6] With the development of material design, morphology control, and device engineering, remarkable improvement in performance has been achieved, OSCs with a bulk heterojunction (BHJ) photoactive layer has  demonstrated power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) over $1 8 \%$ [7-12] A large amount of work has been done on designing molecular structures (like Y6-series) to optimize their optoelectronic properties.7-16] In particular, studies of the relationship between blend morphology and photovoltaic performance are becoming more common in these years.[17-25] The optimal morphology of active layer should have suitable phaseseparated domains, high domain purity, and bi-continuous interpenetrating network, which guarantee efficient exciton separation, carrier transport, and collection as well as low carrier recombination loss.[26-28] In order to obtain a bi-continuous network, many vital factors, including molecular weight, donor/acceptor (D/A) weight ratio, D/A miscibility, etc., should be elaborately selected.[29-32]

In general, blend films kinetically frozen away from thermodynamic stable states during the rapid solvent evaporation of the solution, resulting in low phase separation behavior.[28,33] Thus, various processing techniques were used to achieve the optimal morphology, such as thermal annealing (TA), solvent vapor annealing (SVA), selection of solvent, mixed solvent, and solid additives.19,21,28,34 40] A better understanding of the BHJ morphology evolution employed by various processing conditions provides great opportunities to establish clear blend microstructure-property relations. However, in some cases, many morphology control strategies, like SVA and TA treatments, are not useful for the possible large-scale fabrication in industry scenarios.[41,42] As expected, development of appropriate approaches to improve the printed film formation, BHJ morphology, and composition distribution for blend films is of great significance and urgent, especially under the background that the efficiency of the currently reported photovoltaic systems has basically met commercial requirements.[43,44]

Among the above-mentioned strategies, binary solvent mixture (like chlorobenzene (CB)/1,8-diiodooctance (DIO) and chloroform (CF)/1-chloronaphthalene (CN) binary solvents), which has been successfully used in morphology control in fullerene and non-fullerene based OsCs,414548] is one effective and very simple approach to fine-tune the composition distribution of active layers. By mixing a few volume percent of DIO or CN with the host solvent (CB or CF), the device performance can be improved significantly.49] Many other solvents with few volume percent, like $N \mathrm { . }$ methyl-2-pyrrolidone,[40]1,8-octanedithiol,[50] diphenyl sulfide,[51] diphenyl ether,[52] and 4-bromoanisole,[40] can also be used in OsCs and achieved fruitful results. It is because when guest solvents (e.g., CN and DIO) were added to the host solvent, the kinetics of film formation would be changed significantly, resulting from the solubility differences of guest and host solvents to each component of the D:A blend. Thus, it can be summarized that molecular packing, domain size as well as phase separation in the blends can be modified effectively by using binary solvent mixtures.45 48] And finding new approaches to fine-tune the BHJ morphologies of the high-performance photovoltaic systems is timely and highly desired.

Recently, small-molecule non-fullerene acceptors (SMNFAs, like Y6-series) with fused-ring benzotriazole-based A'-DAD-A' structure as electron-deficient-core are widely used in OSCs.53-55] In most cases, photovoltaic performance of the Y6-series-based OSCs are quite sensitive to blend morphology, which generally needs both CN addition and TA treatments to optimize their bulk microstructure.55,56] It's associated with their strong crystallinity characteristics and poor compatibility and miscibility of the relevant D/A materials.[57] Notably, a chlorinated Y6-2Cl acceptor is a classical SMNFA fluorinated Y6 derivative in the field of OSCs and has a higher molecular aggregation behavior in the solid-state.[58] However, as known, high device performance of Y6-2Cl-based sys. tems is more difficult to achieve, as compared to its Y6 counterpart. In this work, employing a doctor-blade processing technique, a CF/CB binary solvent strategy was developed and employed to fine-tune the film formation for achieving suitable BHJ morphology. Notably, the doctor-blade-coating method is inexpensive and easily transferable to a roll-to-roll coating environment. To fine-tune bulk microstructures in relevant devices, we carefully modified the volume ratio of the two ingredients of the CF and CB solvents. Optimized Y6-2Cl-based devices with a CF:CB volume ratio of 1.2:1 exhibit a PCE of up to $1 6 . 1 7 \%$ by incorporating the polymer donor PM6 (as illustrated in Figure 1A), which is much higher than those of CF- and CB-bladed devices $1 4 . 0 8 \%$ and $1 1 . 4 4 \%$ , respectively). The results are also associated with their different film formation kinetics, supported by systematic morphological characterizations and physical analysis. Of note is that the use of this co-solvent approach in the other two high-performance photovoltaic systems was also confirmed, demonstrating its good generality of employing in the printing organic solar cells.

In order to gain more insight into how the film-forming kinetics impacts on the molecule orientation, crystallinity, and phase separation structure of relevant films, we used in situ photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy to investigate the real-time evolution of the CB-, CF-, and CF: CB co-solventbased blends morphologies fabricated by a doctor-blade technique.59] This enabled the in situ characterization of the blend morphology as the processing solvent was evaporating in the initial phase of film formation. The relevant in situ PL results are shown in Figure 2A for the CB-bladed blend, Figure 2B for the CF-bladed blend, and Figure 2C for the CF:CB co-solventbladed blend, respectively. The PL intensities of the different solvent processes gradually reduced from 0 s to $5 \mathrm { ~ s ~ }$ . The different limit of saturation of $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ materials in various solvents, as well as the surface energy of D/A materials and their miscibility, will significantly influence the connectivity and growth of the $\mathrm { D } / \mathrm { A }$ aggregates formed. When using CF as the bladecasting solvent, the rapid removal of CF solvent due to its low boiling point $( 6 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ rapidly concentrates PM6:Y6-2Cl mixture, well past the solubility limit, driving the wetting deposition to layer-thinning transition. In contrast, when using CB or the binary solvent as the casting solvents, the film drying time is prolonged compared to the CF solvent.

As exhibited in Figure 2D,E, we analyzed the peak position of PL intensity and peak height of the different solvent blends, respectively. The peak position of CF solvent blend rapidly increased from 804 to $8 3 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in less than $_ \mathrm { ~ 2 ~ s ~ }$ of bladecoating process, indicating that the PM6:Y6-2Cl mixture force a phase separation before a proper crystallization of the film, which probably lead to negative phase separation. Moreover, when using CB as the casting solvent, the film formation time is prolonged and the peak position of CB solvent blend gradually increased from 810 to $8 4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ after 5 s of blade coating, indicating that Y6-2Cl molecules have strong intermolecular packing tendency and strongly crystallization. The different initial positions also illustrate that the D: A mixture have stronger pre-aggregation behavior in CB solution, also supported by the temperature-dependent absorption spectra of the PM6:Y6-2Cl in diluted CF and CB solutions (Figure S4). Notably, as compared to the CF film formation with the height of the PL peak (Figure 2B), that of CB film formation was much higher (Figure 2A), also indicating the pre-aggregation behavior in solutions. This phenomenon may also be the reason for the inadequate film forming process of the active layer materials dissolved in CF solvent. The largest change of the peak position and the film formation time with CB solvent blend indicate the excessive crystallites of Y6-2Cl, causing a large phase separation and rough surface, which limit the carrier transport and collection properties. The peak position of the CF: CB cosolvent-processed blend increased from 817 to $8 3 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ after 3 s of blade coating, the least change of the peak position related to the suppressed aggregates and crystallization of Y6-2Cl through fine-turned film formation time. Meanwhile the phase separation are optimized and facilitate a more suitable nanoscale morphology, supported by the higher intensity of the PL peak (Figure 2C) and more smooth curve of its height during the CF:CB film formation (Figure 2E). Obviously, the analysis of in situ PL characterizations suggests the different blend microstructures of the corresponding active layers.

Here, the blend morphological characteristics of PM6:Y6-2Cl active layers processed by different solvents were further investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and synchrotronbased grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements. As shown in Figure 3A-C, the root-meansquare (RMS) roughness of the AFM surfaces are $1 . 1 5 7 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the CB-coated blend, $0 . 8 9 7 { \mathrm { n m } }$ for the CF-coated blend and $0 . 6 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the CF: CB co-solvent-coated blend, respectively. When CB was used as the blade-casting solvent, the blend film form phase-separated morphologies with randomly aligned fibrillar domains in the surface, which is identical to the pre vious investigations.(8] The CF blade-coated blend shows relatively larger domains but a slightly smoother surface. In addition, it can be seen that the CF: CB coated blend forms defined phase separation with a bi-continuous interpenetrating network. The domain size decreases as well, which can be partially estimated from the reduced RMS value of $0 . 6 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ These observations correspond well with the in situ PL measurements, due to the various film-forming kinetics.

To obtain comprehensive information regarding the crystallization and aggregation behaviors of the photovoltaic materials within the active layers and demonstrate the impacts of the different solvents on the thin film morphology, synchrotron-based GIWAXS measurements were conducted for the PM6:Y6-2Cl films processed by different solvents. The 2D GIWAXS patterns of the corresponding blends are shown in Figure 3D-F, and the 1D profiles are plotted in Figure 3G. Previous work has shown that the neat PM6 film shows a disordered arrangement implying a less ordered nature of the film, in contrast the neat Y6-2Cl film is revealed to adopt a face-on preferential orientation with respect to the substrate.[58] All three blends remain in the preferred face-on orientation, evidenced by the appearance of an obvious (010) peak in the out-of-plane (OOP) direction, responsible for the efficient charge transport of the relevant OSCs. Nevertheless, it can be seen that the CF-coated blend as compared to the CB and CF: CB coated blends exhibits clearer arc-leak diffraction peaks, indicating that the CF processed film has partially ordered crystallographic orientation. In contrast, the co-solvent processed blend shows ellips diffraction peaks, which means it has more ordered crystallographic orientation and higher domain purity. Furthermore, as provided in Figure 3G and Table S4, all three blends display a strong (010) diffraction peak in the OOP direction. $( q _ { z } \approx 1 . 7 3 \mathrm { ~ \AA ^ { - 1 } } )$ together with an IP diffraction at $q _ { x \gamma } \approx 0 . 2 9 6 \ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ . The lamellar crystal coherence length (CCL) in the (100) direction can be estimated by using the Scherrer equation was $1 5 5 . 1 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ for CB solvent, $1 \dot { 4 } 4 . 0 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ for CF solvent and $1 4 7 . 4 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ for the CF: CB co-solvent, respectively. The longer CCL in the CB solvent blends indicates stronger molecular crystallinity and higher domain purity. Nevertheless, the CF: CB co-solvent employing in the bladed PM6:Y6-2Cl system can effectively reduce the intermolecular packing tendency and facilitate the formation of suitable nanoscale morphology with optimized phase separation. Notably, although the CF coated blend show the largest molecular aggregations as compared to the other two blends, demonstrated by the obvious (010) peak in the OOP direction, its domain purity is poor.

Mesoscale domain distriubtion has been probed by GrazingIncidence Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (GISAXs) measurement. The scattering patterns of Y6-2Cl and its binary blends parallel to the substrate are provided in Figure 3H. The CB and CF: CB coated blends generates similar small-angle scattering profiles along in-plane direction, indicating similar phase separation behaviors. Both blend films exhibit an apparent peak located at $0 . 3 1 \mathrm { n m } ^ { - 1 }$ [60] corresponding to a long period around $2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Further inspection of the scattering profiles indicates that PM6:Y6 processed by CF:CB co-solvent displays stronger overall scattering than that processed only by CB solvent. This suggests that the combination of CF and CB indcues higher domain purity between PM6 and Y6 although without changing the domain size. Besides, the scattering profile of the CF blend along in-plane direction demonstrated that its domain purity is very low, which is also supported by the analysis of GIWAXS measurements. Thus, the morphological characteristics of the CF-processed blend inevitably caused unbalanced charge transport properties in relevant devices as further discussed below.

Putting all of these results of morphology characterizations together, we schematically picture the nanoscale morphology of the three blends in Figure 4 with the main difference being the composition of the Y6-2Cl in the matrix phase and its domain size. When using CF as the casting solvent, the larger domain size can be obtained as compared to the CB- and CF:CBprocessed blends, but the excessive interpenetrating networks lead to the worse domain purity in the CF active layer with partially ordered crystallographic orientation. In addition, when using CB as the casting solvent, a more consistent molecular orientation can be obtained in the CB blend with a suitable domain size. From this analysis, we further conclude that the choice of processing solvents enables modifying the D/A interfacial area and fine-tuning the aggregation of the molecules, and eventually changing ordering of large crystals. This, in turn, is likely to cause variations in the fundamental photo-physical processes, resulting in the above-mentioned differences in device performance. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated an effective co-solvent blending strategy to turn the BHJ bulk microstructure, and established a straightforward relationship among film-forming kinetics, morphological characteristics, physical dynamics, and device performance for the PM6: Y6-2Cl system. The film-forming process as well as its film formation time can be fine-turned when using the mixtures of CF and CB solvents. The detailed morphology characterizations demonstrate that the CF:CB cosolvent can effectively suppress the molecular aggregations and crystallization, forming a more balanced phase separation with proper domain sizes and high phase purity. In contrast, the CF blend shows amorphous intermixing BHJ, even though possess more obvious (010) peak in the OOP direction as compared to the CF:CB processed blend. The morphological characterizations and photovoltaic performance investigated in these blends were further demonstrated by employing steady-state and transient spectroscopic techniques. As a result, better exciton dissociation and charge generation, more balanced charge transport, and less recombination loss were obtained in the co-solvent coated devices. As a result, a PCE of $1 6 . 1 7 \%$ was achieved in the doctor-bladed PM6:Y6-2Cl device with CF:CB co-solvent and without any post-treatments, which is much higher than those of CF- and CB-processed devices $1 4 . 0 8 \%$ and $1 1 . 4 4 \%$ respectively). Notably, the use of this co-solvent approach in the other two high-performance PM6-Ir1: BTP-eC9 and PM6: N3 systems was also confirmed, demonstrating its good generality of employing in the printing OsCs. Our work provides a fundamental understanding of the work mechanisms of binary solvent underlying formation of phase separation structure but also paves a way for fine-tuning the blend microstructure of high-performance photovoltaic systems and facilitating their development of high-throughput processing conditions, which play a crucial role in industrial scenarios.
